# Chapter 2: Europe after Rome

After the Fall of the Western Roman Empire, most of Europe became
markedly less complex in its economic, cultural, and administrative
organization. Compared to Roman Europe, society by the end of the Early
Middle Ages was predominantly agricultural and self-sufficient. As the
period drew to a close, however, Europe entered into a long period of
economic ascendancy that saw an expansion of trade, advances in
financial and business practices, population growth, growth in urban
centers, and the introduction of new technologies. With some
interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of
the Middle Ages and into the early modern period.

The modern history of money, payments, and banking largely begins during
Europe's economic ascendancy in the High and Late Middle Ages. Our
purpose in this chapter is to briefly provide the background conditions
against which this economic rise began. In *Section 1*, we describe the
economic, political, and social conditions in the Early Middle Ages.
Section 2 explains the early medieval origins of two key European
states: France and Germany. *Section 3* finally sketches how Europe at
the turn of the second millennium enjoyed somewhat more order and
stability.

## 1. The Early Middle Ages

From the end of the fourth century onwards, the Roman Empire was
permanently divided into a Western Roman Empire and an Eastern Roman
Empire, each with their own emperors.[^1] At the time of the permanent
split, the western half of the Empire roughly covered the area of
Continental Europe below the Rhine and Danube until modern-day Croatia
and Bosnia-Herzegovina in the East, the island of Great Britain until
Hadrian's Wall, and the northern coastline of Africa stretching from
modern-day Morocco to Egypt.

In 476, Odoacer—a commander of various groups of foederati, barbarian
troops serving the Western Empire—deposed Romulus Augustus in Ravenna,
and declared himself the King of Italy. The label "Italy" had become a
common name for the Peninsula by that time. Odoacer's reign lasted until
493 when he was killed by the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. From 535
onwards, the Byzantines began conquering regions in the Peninsula,
roughly from the South to the North. By the end of the Gothic War
(535–554), the Byzantine Empire controlled the entire Italian
Peninsula. But it was only short-lived. The Lombards, namely, invaded
Italy in 568 and took control over much of Italy, though the Byzantine
Empire retained certain strongholds such as the exarchate of Ravenna,
Naples, Rome, and Sicily. From this point onwards, control over the
Peninsula essentially remained fragmented to varying degrees until the
nineteenth century. Until the turn of the millennium, control was shared
primarily among the Byzantines, Lombards, Carolingian Franks, Arabs, and
the Papacy.

The year 476, when Odoacer declared himself the King of Italy, is the
standard date for the Fall of the Roman Empire. But the ending of the
Western Roman Empire was, of course, hardly a single event. Great
Britain was left by Roman troops already in the early fifth century,
ushering in an era of immigration by continental tribes—notably the
Saxons, Angels, Jutes, and Frisians—that lasted well-into the sixth
century. They pushed out the native Britons to peripheral areas such as
Cornwall and Wales.[^2] The Vandals had conquered the northern part of
Africa by 439 and famously sacked the city of Rome in 455. And many
areas in Roman Continental Europe had fully or largely already come, by
476, under the control of various tribes, including the Ostrogoths,
Visigoths, Franks, Burgundians, Lombards, and others. Many of these
tribes had been displaced into Western and Central Europe by the
extremely violent incursions of the Huns—a people who originated
somewhere deep from within the Eurasian Steppe—during the late fourth
and fifth centuries.

When the final emperor, Romulus Augustus, was deposed by Odoacer, Europe
had, therefore, to a large extent already transformed into a collection
of tribal kingdoms. These kingdoms were only loosely associated with
certain regions and the "inhabitants" identified more with charismatic
warrior kings and leaders, rather than certain territories. The new
inhabitants largely established themselves as a ruling, military elite.
The original inhabitants typically remained the majority. Some of these
original inhabitants would have identified as Romans. But as Romans
largely left local customs, laws, and institutions in place of the
territories they conquered, many other identities existed. Due to
intermarriage and integration between the new and original inhabitants,
identity was probably quite fluid in this period around the fall of the
Western Roman Empire.

The period of European history from the fall of the Western Roman Empire
until the year 1000 is standardly called the Early Middle Ages.
Traditionally, it has also often been called the **Dark Ages**. That
label was inspired by the supposed political, societal, and economic
collapse of Roman Europe in the fifth century and the region's
subsequent period of prolonged stagnation.

Modern historians and archaeologists have varied judgements on the
severity and speed of the impact of the events of the fifth century, and
the degree to which a characterization of the subsequent period as the
"Dark Ages" is actually justified.[^3] After the fall of the Empire, for
example, certain institutions such as systems of taxation and
administration, the church hierarchy, and Roman coinage continued to be
maintained in many of the new tribal kingdoms.[^4] Writing about the
Franks in the sixth century, Gregory of Tours could still identify those
families which belonged to the Roman senatorial classes.[^5] Overall,
however, scholars do broadly agree that the European territories
formerly within the Western Roman Empire were markedly different by the
ninth and tenth centuries than under Roman rule.

Roman political, commercial, and social life had centered around its
towns and cities. According to Hopkins, urbanization reached over 30
percent in Italy during the early Roman Empire.[^6] But during the Early
Middle Ages, the populations in Roman towns and cities dwindled, often
drastically. Many towns such as Trier and Tongeren were practically
abandoned for a long period.[^7] While Rome might have housed almost a
million inhabitants in the early part of the millennium, by the early
ninth century that number had dropped to a few tens of thousands.[^8] By
the ninth century, European building standards were also much less
sophisticated. Scholarship and literacy were much less widespread and
mostly confined to the Christian monasteries. Administrative and
taxation systems were much more primitive. And the economy was largely
agricultural, self-sufficient, and less specialized than in Roman times.

In particular, any commercial exchange was generally limited to the
local level during the Early Middle Ages. Long-distance trade was, by
contrast to Roman times, a minor economic activity.[^9] According to De
Roover,[^10] "\[w\]hat little \[long-distance trade\] survived was
carried on by groups of travelling merchants who catered for the rich by
selling them luxuries or who exploited the poor by charging high prices
for necessities in times of famine or distress". Given the hazardous
conditions, a merchant "had to possess a great deal of physical
endurance and be ready to face many hardships".[^11] In addition, "moral
stamina and some intellectual baggage were indispensable prerequisites
to success".[^12]

The fall of the Western Roman Empire did not impact all parts of Roman
Europe equally. The overall decline seems to have been most significant
in the north.[^13] Most extremely, in what had once been Roman Britain,
few remnants of classical civilization apparently survived the start of
the Early Middle Ages, as pagan Anglo-Saxon control over the region
spread in the fifth and sixth centuries. The usage of Roman coins, for
instance, stopped within a few decades of the departure of the Roman
armies.[^14] Despite the overall stronger regression in the north, Dean
and Waley still offer the following reflection on the transformation
that took place in Italy during the Early Middle Ages:[^15]

> Between the fifth and tenth centuries AD \[municipal\] institutions
> were shattered and the very nature of political and social authority
> was reshaped by a series of events which included the conquests and
> settlements of Goths and Lombards. The gradual but profound
> transformation of these centuries involved a great contraction in city
> life and an almost complete break in urban government. A few of the
> larger cities, among them Rome itself, and Milan, survived as
> important centers of population, but most of the ancient
> municipalities must have declined into sordid if picturesque decay,
> villages in a setting of classical ruins.

The area of Europe outside of the Western Roman Empire was predominantly
inhabited by agricultural and tribal societies. This very much remained
that way after the events of the fifth century. In general, these
peripheral areas were also marked by very limited urbanization, little
long-distance trade, unspecialized economic activities, low rates of
literacy, and so on. The regions in scope here are those north of the
Rhine, as well as north and north-east of the Danube; modern-day
Scandinavia; and the parts of the British Isles that were not settled by
the Romans, including modern-day Ireland and Scotland.

From the overall picture of an economically undeveloped and rural Europe
during the Early Middle Ages, we must exclude the Iberian Peninsula and
Byzantium.

The Visigoths, a Germanic tribe, sacked Rome in 410 and then in the
early fifth century established a kingdom centered around southwestern
Gaul, a Roman name for a region that encompassed much of Western Europe
including modern-day France and Belgium. The Visigoths then increasingly
expanded into the Iberian Peninsula. After a decisive defeat by the
Franks at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, the Visigoths largely retreated
into the Iberian Peninsula, eventually establishing their capital in
Toledo. The experience of Visigothic Spain was probably much the same as
the rest of Roman Europe at the beginning of the Early Middle Ages.
However, in 711 the region was invaded by Arab Muslims with profound
consequences.

From the early seventh century onwards, Arab Muslim forces rapidly
expanded territory from their original base in Medina, first under the
leadership of Mohammed (570–632) and later under the leadership of the
Rashidun (632–661) and Umayyad (661–750) caliphs. A caliph was the
name for a religious and political leader of the Muslim community. He
was considered the rightful successor to Mohammed. A caliphate was,
thus, a territory controlled by the caliph. In the early years of Islam,
Muslims—both Arab and non-Arab—were largely united territorially
within such caliphates, though not all Muslims at time recognized these
caliphs as rightful. The legitimacy of these early and subsequent
caliphs is disputed until the modern-day and at the root of the division
between Sunni and Shia Muslims.[^16]

After their expansion in North Africa, Arab Muslim forces invaded the
Iberian Peninsula in 711. Their incursion was only stopped near Paris at
the Battle of Tours in 732 by the Franks under the military leadership
of Charles Martel (c. 688–741), also known as "the hammer". From then
onwards the Muslim occupation of Europe was largely confined to the
Iberian Peninsula, which was part of the Umayyad Caliphate. After the
takeover of the Caliphate by the Abbasids (750 – 1258)—who notably
moved the capital from Damascus to Baghdad—the Umayyads actually fled
to the Iberian Peninsula and established, in 756, the Emirate of
Córdoba, the first Islamic state independent from the Caliphate. Muslim
Spain—then known as Al Andalus—remained unified under the Ummayyad
Dynasty until the early eleventh century. Under Muslim rule, this region
appears to have been more urbanized and developed than the rest of
Europe until the end of the Early Middle Ages. The Capital of Córdoba
was in its size and prosperity only rivalled by Constantinople at the
turn of the millennium.

Next, the Eastern Roman Empire continued to exist after Romulus Augustus
was deposed in the West. We commonly call it Byzantium from this point
onwards—after the Greek colony that was originally established at
Constantinople in the seventh century BC—though the inhabitants
themselves would have probably just seen themselves as Romans and their
polity as the continuation of the Roman Empire. Byzantium was much less
affected by the barbarian migrations and invasions that marked Europe in
the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries. After 476, Byzantium continued
to thrive, even conquering much of the former West Roman Mediterranean
under the leadership of Justinian I (482–565) in the sixth century.
Another notable way in which Byzantium diverged from Europe in this
period is by Greek replacing Latin as the primary language.

Byzantium shrank significantly in the seventh century as a result of the
Arab Muslim invasions. Notably, it lost much of the Levant and adjacent
territories in the Near East, as well as its territories on the north
African coast. European losses to the Arab Muslims were very limited,
though Byzantium did eventually permanently lose Malta (870) and Sicily
(902). Essentially, after the catastrophic losses to the Arab Muslims in
the seventh century, the boundaries of the Byzantine Empire continually
shifted until the end of the Early Middle Ages. So making any
generalizations about the Empire is difficult. If we largely confine
ourselves, however, to the core region of Byzantium, namely
Constantinople, Anatolia, and Greece, the empire seems to have been, in
general, substantially more wealthy and urbanized than the rest of
Europe during the Early Middle Ages. As already noted, Constantinople
and Córdoba were the two largest and most prosperous cities in Europe at
the turn of the millennium.

A key aspect about Europe during the Early Middle Ages was the frequent
waves of raiding, migration, and invasion. We have already mentioned the
Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, Franks, Saxons, and other tribes that
transformed Roman Europe in the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries.
While Islam was largely confined to the Iberian Peninsula from the
middle of the eighth century onwards—the conquests of Malta (870) and
Sicily (902) are notable exceptions—Muslims continued to intensively
raid Mediterranean Europe until the tenth century. Many Europeans were
taken as slaves to Africa and the Near East.

Other notable waves of raiding, migration, and invasion during the Early
Middle Ages came from the Magyars and Vikings. From the late ninth until
the middle of the tenth century, much of Western and Central Europe was
the subject of intense and extremely violent cavalry raids by the
Magyars, a people that originally came from the steppes east of the
Carpathian mountains. At the Battle of Lechfeld (955), the Magyars
suffered a heavy defeat against the East Frankians under the leadership
of King Otto I (912–973). The raids ended and the Magyars settled in
the region of modern-day Hungary.

The Vikings, in turn, is a collective name for the various peoples from
Denmark, Norway, and Sweden at the time. At the turn of the ninth
century, they began raiding the British Isles and much of Continental
Europe, predominantly along the coastlines and river routes. Later in
the ninth century, they also began to migrate and settle in certain
areas. Notable lasting Viking settlements and integration with local
populations occurred in modern-day Scotland, Ireland, England, France,
and in an area that spans across modern-day Ukraine, Belarus, and
Russia.

In the region of modern-day England, under the leadership of the king of
Wessex, Alfred the Great (c. 849–899), the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms united
to halt the progress of the Vikings by the end of the ninth century. The
territory in Eastern and Northern England settled by Vikings and
governed by their laws—known as Danelaw (865–954)—was eventually
integrated back into Anglo-Saxon territory under the leadership of
Alfred's son Edward the Elder (c. 874–924). The entire unified
territory increasingly became known as "England" and its people as the
"English". Supposedly, Athelstan (c. 894–937), son of Edward the Elder,
was the first to use the title "King of the English". In the early
eleventh century, Cnut the Great (c. 995–1035) from Denmark briefly
established the so-called North Sea Empire, consisting of England
(1016), Denmark (1018), and Norway (1028). Supposedly, he was the first
to use the title "King of England". Shortly after Cnut the Great's
death, in 1042, England came back under Anglo-Saxon rule with the
ascendancy of Edward the Confessor (1003–1066) to the throne.

In Ireland, Scotland, and other parts of the British Isles, the Vikings
also made lasting settlements, notably founding cities such as Dublin
and Cork. In Western Europe, the Viking leader Rollo in 911 made a deal
with Charles the Simple (879–929), the Carolingian king of West Francia
(or simply Francia). In exchange for the territory of Normandy—or
"land of the Northmen"—Rollo would convert to Christianity, swear
allegiance to Charles, and defend West Francia against other Viking
raiders. Norwegian Vikings mostly settled Iceland and Greenland,
territories on the periphery of Europe that were largely uninhabited at
the end of the Early Middle Ages.

Finally, a group of mostly Swedish Vikings known as the Varangians
established a large state known as Kyivan Rus in the late ninth century.
The territory eventually spanned the modern-day territory of Ukraine,
Belarus, and Russia. The state originally had its capital at Kyiv, but
eventually started to splinter into different principalities in the
eleventh century. The Varangians that settled in the region mixed with
the Slavs, Finnic, and other peoples, and eventually the inhabitants of
the region largely adopted the Slavic identity.

In the Early Middle Ages, fairly large territories were, at times,
united under single rulers. Odoacer and the Ostrogoths ruled Italy and
neighboring areas between 476 and 553. In the sixth century, Justinian
I, emperor of the Eastern Roman Empire or Byzantium, gained control over
much of southern Europe. The Anglo-Saxons largely unified England by the
end of the 10th century, and Kyivan Rus was also a fairly large state by
the end of the tenth century. But most notable with regards to
territorial unification in the Early Middle Ages are certainly the
Franks.

Clovis I (c. 466–511), king of the Salian Franks, united the various
Frankish tribes during his reign across a territory roughly equivalent
to Roman Gaul. After his death, Clovis' territory was split into several
separate Frankish kingdoms. The Merovingian dynasty that ruled these
various kingdoms—named after Clovis' grandfather Merovech—lasted
until 751. At this point, the Merovingian kings were deposed and the
Franks united under the son of Charles Martel, Pepin III or Pepin the
Short (c. 714–768). Notably, the Carolingians had formed an alliance
with the papacy to legitimize their ascendancy to the throne.

Pepin began consolidating and expanding the territory of the united
Franks. One conquest of note was that of various Lombard lands in Italy.
Early in Pepin's reign, Pope Stephen II urged him to attack the
Lombards. The latter had made several recent conquests in northern and
central Italy—including that of the Exarchate of Ravenna in 751—at
the expense of the Byzantine Empire. With much of the Peninsula now
under their control, the Lombards were threatening to conquer Rome.
While the Byzantine Empire technically controlled Rome, that control was
largely formal and the papacy exercised substantial independent power. A
conquest of Rome by the Lombards would have seriously threatened that
power. As the Byzantine Emperor was unable to help, Pope Stephen II
turned to his Carolingian allies for support. Pepin's forces
successfully conquered some of the Lombard-owned lands, including
Ravenna, over two invasions, one in 754 and one in 756. Rather than
annexing these lands or returning them to the Byzantine Empire, Pepin
granted them to the Papacy. While the Catholic Church had come to own a
substantial amount of land and property over the centuries, the
territories granted through the Donation of Pepin in 754/756 marked the
beginning of territorial political rule by the Papacy.

That expansion of the Franks continued more intensively under the reign
of Pepin's son, Charles the Great (c. 748–814) or Charlemagne. He had
annexed much of northern and central Italy, declaring himself King of
the Lombards, by 774. Other notable conquests were those of Saxony and
Bavaria. Charlemagne was famously crowned Holy Roman Emperor by Pope Leo
III in 800 to revive the idea of the Western Roman Empire. His kingdom
covered practically all of Western and Central Europe by that time.
After his death in 814, Charlemagne was succeeded by his son Louis the
Pious (778–840). After the death of Louis, the empire was divided among
his sons into three large kingdoms through the Treaty of Verdun (843).
West Francia was roughly the region of modern France and Belgium. East
Francia was roughly the region of modern Germany and some surrounding
territories. Middle Francia was a large strip of land that started in
the Low Countries, a common name for the region approximately equivalent
to the area of the modern-day Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg,
pertaining to the fact that much of the land is around or even below sea
level.[^17] It extended through the middle of Europe into northern
Italy.

While some polities during the Early Middle Ages clearly covered large
territories, these were often really little more than loosely united and
usually not closely governed and administered. Throughout the period,
influence was instead largely with local and regional rulers. The rule
of Charlemagne over the united Franks and various other peoples might
have been more organized and one of the few exceptions to the
decentralized political character of the Early Middle Ages. The more
organized Carolingian rule disintegrated, however, after the death of
Louis the Pious. So, in any case, nothing like the organized rule of the
Western Empire over Roman Europe was achieved in the period.[^18]

## 2. The Origins of France and Germany

We have already mentioned how England and English identity began to
develop at the end of the Early Middle Ages. In the wake of the Treaty
of Verdun, we see the origins of two other key modern European states
arise: while Middle Francia quickly disintegrated into various regions
after the treaty, West Francia and East Francia are often regarded as
the precursors to modern France and Germany.

In West Francia, the Carolingian dynasty lasted until 987 when Hugh
Capet (c. 939–996) assumed the throne. During the early years of the
Capetian dynasty (987–1328), West Francia or Francia increasingly
became known as France. While the main line of the Capetians ended with
the death of Charles IV in 1328, two cadet branches—the House of
Valois (1328–1589) and the House of Bourbon (1589–1792)—essentially
ruled France until the start of the French Revolution (1789).[^19]
Despite the existence of a monarchy, governance over West Francia or
France quickly became decentralized after the Treaty of Verdun. It
remained that way for centuries and only began to become more
centralized in the Late Middle Ages. Only in the early modern period did
France experienced centralized control in the fashion resembling that of
a more modern state.

After the start of the French Revolution in 1789, France was marked by
substantial political and social turmoil for about a hundred years. In
that period, it shifted between dictatorship, constitutional monarchy,
and republican rule. After a bad defeat against Prussia in the
Franco-Prussian War (1870), Napoleon III—Napoleon's nephew who was
essentially a military dictator—was captured and the Third Republic
(1870–1940) was proclaimed. While the Franco-Prussian war was
devastating to France, the country was finally able to enter a period of
real political and social stability uniting the various republican,
liberal, and conservative factions.

In East Francia, the Carolingian dynasty would last until 911. In 918,
Henry the Fowler (876–936) was then elected the King of the East
Franks. This title passed to his son Otto I (912–973) in 936. The
latter was, in 962, also crowned Holy Roman Emperor by the pope, after
the title fallen into disuse for several decades. During the tenth and
eleventh centuries, the people in this region would increasingly be
called Teutons or Germans. The King of the East Franks eventually became
known as the King of Germany and for almost a millennium the kingship
was an elected position. Before 1356, voting was the prerogative of
German princes and other powerful nobles though voting rights were not
always clear. After the promulgation of the Golden Bull in 1356 by Holy
Roman Emperor Charles IV, voting was more organized and the prerogative
of specific princes and ecclesiastical authorities, about a dozen at the
turn of the nineteenth century.

Usually, the King of the Germans was also crowned Holy Roman Emperor by
the pope at some point during his reign. The medieval and early modern
Kingdom of Germany was larger than modern-day Germany. While the precise
boundaries shifted and were contested, it generally included, for
instance, what is modern-day Austria. While the Kingdom of Germany was
always the core of the Holy Roman Empire, this political entity also
spanned across other territories. The Holy Roman Empire and the Kingdom
of Germany only ceased as political entities in 1806 because of the
Napoleonic Wars.

Unlike England and France, the Kingdom of Germany as well as the broader
Holy Roman Empire remained fairly decentralized political entities into
the modern era. From the late medieval period until 1806, the main power
within both political entities was the Habsburg Dynasty. Initially a
minor noble family from Switzerland, the Habsburgs gained control over a
sizeable land area around modern-day Austria in the thirteenth century,
officially becoming recognized from then onwards as the Dukes of
Austria. In 1358, the ducal title was elevated to that of Archduke,
though the title was fully recognized by the emperor only in 1453. The
Habsburgs slowly expanded their territory in central Europe during the
fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and from the sixteenth century
onwards became one of the foremost European landowners. From the first
half of the fifteenth century, every King of Germany elected, but one
exception (Charles VII of Bavaria, 1742–1745), was a Habsburg. Yet,
even the Habsburgs had limited influence over the other territories
within the Kingdom of Germany and the broader Holy Roman Empire.

The Congress of Vienna (1814–15) was a series of international
political meetings where European leaders attempted to draw out a
political order that could guarantee stability and peace after the
defeat of Napoleon. One product was the creation of a German
Confederation (1815–1866) consisting of thirty-nine states. In 1804,
the Archduke of Austria Francis II (1768–1835) had wanted a title equal
to that of Napoleon, so rebranded the Habsburg-controlled lands as the
Austrian Empire and declared himself emperor. It became the main state
in the new confederacy of German states, and the Habsburgs held the
presidency throughout the loose confederation's existence.

In the 1860s, the Prussians, who had become a formidable power by the
end of the eighteenth century, began a process of German unification
through the incorporation of allied states and conquest. After the
defeat of Austria in the Austro-Prussian War (1866), the German
Confederation was dissolved and the Austrian Empire—which transformed
into the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary in 1867—receded from German
affairs. After the Prussian victory over France in the Franco-Prussian
War (1870–1871), the various German states were formally unified on
January 18, 1871, into the Prussian-led German Empire with King Wilhelm
I of Prussia (1797–1888) crowned as the first emperor.

## 3. The start of a new millennium

By the thirteenth century, relations among the nobility in Europe were
marked by lordship, vassalage, and fiefs. A **fief** was most commonly a
piece of land held in tenure by a vassal from a lord; that is, the
vassal had the right to use and derive benefits from the land, but
technically at least it remained the property of the lord.[^20] Vassals
in return would provide certain services, particularly with regards to
military campaigns. A key contribution was that of mounted knights which
required substantial funds to furnish. In the case of small fiefs, the
vassal might just serve with a few members of the household. In the case
of larger fiefs, dozens of knights might have been provided. Vassals
would also provide other types of military personnel such as archers and
infantrymen as well as contribute provisions, money, and other types of
support for military campaigns.

The lord-vassal relationship existed between monarchs and higher nobles.
It also existed between the Holy Roman Emperor and the ruling nobility
of the allied territories. The higher nobles, in turn, standardly had
numerous vassals of their own. Sometimes, the hierarchy may have
extended even further downwards. The relationship between a lord and a
vassal was a personal one. It was cemented by an act of homage, a
ceremony in which the vassal swore loyalty and service to the lord in
exchange for protection. Typically the relationship involved a fief,
though this was not always the case. The opportunity to participate in
the lord-vassal structure was in the thirteenth century mostly
hereditary with titles and fiefs passing through noble families over
generations. Exceptions were, however, sometimes made.

The description of the lord-vassal system above is, of course, stylized.
Even in the European regions to which it was most applicable for the
thirteenth century, laws and customs varied considerably, and other
forms of landownership and military recruitment also existed.
Furthermore, the lord-vassal system was not the predominant mode for
noble relationships, military recruitment, and land organization
everywhere. Iceland, for example, was quite different in this regard,
both during the Free State period (c. 930–1262) and under subsequent
Norwegian (1262–1380) and Norwegian-Danish (1380–1814) rule.[^21]

The lord-vassal system seems to have obtained its first real foothold
among the Franks, and the origins of the system are commonly attributed
to reforms and events that transpired particularly during the rule of
the Carolingians.[^22]

To manage their Empire, the Carolingians relied on the Frankish elite
for local and regional administrative, judicial, and military tasks. The
key administrators were counts, but also included dukes, marquesses,
viscounts, and castellans (later barons). These were standardly
appointed positions. They typically came with lands that were held for
life, though sometimes the rights to the land extended to subsequent
generations. The Carolingians used these offices as well as independent
land grants to secure military service from nobles. Such practices were
certainly sensible given that the coinage systems which had existed in
much of Europe during Roman times had almost entirely disappeared.[^23]
Land and offices, therefore, functioned as "money".

In the wake of the disintegration of the Carolingian Empire, the local
and regional offices morphed into hereditary positions accompanied by
fiefs. It is during this period that the building of fortified
residences—better known as castles—also spreads throughout
Carolingian lands and later the rest of Europe. A key factor was the
consistent raids and invasions of Vikings. Initially castles were made
from wood. Over time, however, stone was increasingly used, resulting in
the buildings that are now iconically associated with the Middle Ages.

While medieval historians are shy to use the term today, the lord-vassal
system, was a paradigmatic feature of what has long been called
**feudalism** or the **feudal system** in history books, the term
"feudalism" originating in the eighteenth century from the medieval
Latin word for fief (*feodum*).

The lord-vassal system developed slowly over time, at varying rates in
different parts of Europe. But the system is associated primarily with
the High Middle Ages. A developed lord-vassal system already marked
France in the eleventh century and possibly already much earlier. It
also marked England by the end of the eleventh century. There is a
long-standing discussion among historians whether it was mainly
instituted by William the Conqueror (1066) or already existed in some
fairly developed form before the Norman invasion. The lord-vassal system
reached its peak in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and then
declined slowly over time.[^24]

Hence, from the Carolingian rulers onwards a hereditary aristocracy tied
to land slowly emerged throughout Europe. It was supported to a
significant extent by the lord-vassal system. While Europe had monarchs
and a Holy Roman Emperor that technically controlled large territories,
the continent, in practice, remained fragmented from the end of the
Early Middle Ages onwards. It consisted of many small, largely locally
governed and administered territorial entities. This local rule varied
from somewhat democratic to absolutist. England was a notable exception
to this picture of political and administrative fragmentation, and
already exercised more centralized control at this time than most of the
continent (though far from that of a modern state). Only towards the end
of the Middle Ages and into the early modern period do more strongly
centralized states generally begin to emerge.

Another institution commonly associated with the Middle Ages is the
relationship between lords and serfs. A **serf** was a peasant that
worked on and was tied to the land of a lord. Serfs enjoyed the
protection of a lord in exchange for a share of the agricultural output,
the payment of dues and taxes, and the provision of other services,
including military service. Even though the relationship with a lord was
normally highly exploitative, a serf was not a slave. While a slave was
seen as property and did not enjoy legal rights, serfs did indeed have,
even if normally limited, certain legal rights. During the Middle Ages,
many peasants were not serfs, and they enjoyed more rights like the
freedom of movement and the freedom to enter into contracts.

In the English-American tradition, the socio-economic system of largely
self-sufficient estates controlled by lords and worked by serfs is
normally called **manorialism**, though continental historians
frequently also characterize it as a paradigmatic feature of feudalism.
The institution of serfdom can be traced back at least to the third
century when many Romans fled the towns and cities for the countryside
due to political turbulence, economic stagnation, the Plague of Cyprian,
and other factors. These so-called *coloni* would work the land and
share part of their output with the landowner. Under the rule of
Diocletian at the end of the third century, these *coloni* were bound to
the land and not allowed to leave without permission. The social state
of *colonus* eventually became hereditary. While the Roman economy had
relied heavily on slave labor, during the Early Middle Ages slave labor
largely receded within Europe. By the start of the High Middle Ages,
serfdom was a far more common institution than slavery. Regions
naturally varied substantially in how much they were marked by serfdom.

By the end of the Early Middle Ages, Europeans had also largely become
Christian and followers of the Catholic Church, which was the name for
the mainstream Christian church at the time ("catholic" means
"universal"). In modern-day usage, the term "Catholic Church" of course
has a somewhat different meaning.[^25]

The Romans had been converting to Christianity for centuries when it was
finally embraced by authorities in the fourth century. During the fourth
and fifth centuries, many of the barbarian tribes also turned to
Christianity. However, they usually adopted a particular variant known
as Arianism, a Christian doctrine which had arisen in the fourth
century. This branch deviated quite substantially from the official
Catholic doctrine. An ecumenical council is a high-level meeting of
church bishops and other leaders to discuss doctrine, rules, and
practices of the Catholic church.[^26] At the Catholic Church's first
ecumenical council in Nicaea in 325, it was decreed that God, the holy
spirit, and Christ were, in fact, co-eternal and consubstantial. Arians,
in contrast, did not believe that Christ was co-eternal or
consubstantial with God.[^27]

Unlike the other Barbarian tribes, the Frankish king Clovis I in 508
converted to Catholic Christianity. Subsequently, various other tribes
began converting to Catholic Christianity from Arianism and Paganism,
including the Visigoths, Lombards, and Burgundians. The Irish supposedly
converted to Catholic Christianity during the fifth and sixth centuries.
According to legend, much of the effort was led by Saint Patrick. The
Anglo-Saxons in England mostly converted to Catholic Christianity from
Paganism in the seventh century. Charlemagne forced the continental
Saxons to convert at the end of the eighth century. By the end of the
ninth century, Christianity began spreading among Viking settlements and
in Scandinavia. In the late tenth century, the first ruler of the Polish
state, Mieszko I (c. 922–992), was baptized. In the early eleventh
century, the Hungarian king Stephen I (c. 975–1038) began promoting
Christianity among his people.

By the Fall of the Western Roman Empire, the Bishop of Rome had begun
claiming a leadership position over the other church Bishops, a claim
that had less support in the Eastern Roman Empire. A territorial church
hierarchy was also in place consisting of bishoprics and parishes. By
this time, the title of pope—from the Greek term *páppas* or
"father"—had started to be largely reserved for the Bishop of Rome,
though it only became a formal title in the eleventh century. While the
Catholic Church gradually acquired land and real estate, its independent
power was significantly boosted by the creation of Papal States through
the Donation of Pepin in the eighth century. The power of the Papacy was
still limited around the millennium. Notably, secular rulers often
appointed bishops and abbots—a practice that would eventually lead to
the so-called Investiture Controversy of the eleventh century—and
Papal lands were weakly controlled. Yet, the Papacy was the guiding
force in spiritual matters.

So, by the end of the tenth century, much of Europe was Christianized.
While some regions, particularly the Balkans and parts of Eastern
Europe, were converted under the auspices of Byzantium, much of Europe
followed the Bishop of Rome. Under Charlemagne's reign, substantial
effort had been expended on standardizing practices and customs in
churches and monasteries across western and central Europe. He had also
standardized the Roman alphabet in his effort to preserve books,
manuscripts, and learning. By the end of the tenth century, the raids,
migrations, and invasions that had so marked the Early Middle Ages also
had largely subsided. Only Eastern Europe would dramatically experience
another such event, namely the invasion of the Mongols in the thirteenth
century. Finally, the lord-vassal system was emerging and would
eventually spread throughout much of the continent by the thirteenth
century.

In this environment of somewhat more order and stability at the turn of
second millennium, Europe enters into a long period of economic
ascendancy marked by an expansion of trade, advances in financial and
business practices, population growth, increasing urbanization, and the
introduction of new technologies. With some interruptions and
fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of the Middle Ages.
