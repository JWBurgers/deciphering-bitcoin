# Chapter 1: Banks and banking

Banks are at the core of money and payments. Like it or not, they are some of the most fundamental institutions that make our society run (and sometimes come to a screeching halt). To understand money and payment practices in the United States, we, therefore, require starting with some basic background on banks and banking. 

We start in Section 1 by characterizing, specifically, commercial banks. They are by far the most important type of bank. After setting out a broad characterization of commercial banks, we examine their legal foundations in *Section 2*. In *Section 3*, we will turn to the concept of banking, and discuss various types of banks and other closely related financial institutions. We finish this chapter in *Section 4* with a brief discussion on the history of banking, money, and payments in the United States from its first settlement until the early days of the Republic. 


## 1. Commercial banks

We can distinguish between various types of banks. But by the far the most important type of bank is what is generally called a commercial bank in the United States. 

Commercial banks are key credit providers to consumers and businesses. They offer many products and services for payments, savings, and investments. They employ a great number of people. And a somewhat less well-known fact: commercial banks play a crucial role in determining the money supply. Unsurprisingly, their activities and financial performance have a massive impact on the overall state of the economy. 

Countries and regions across the world are typically dominated by a handful of commercial banks that wield the majority of economic influence and market share. In the United States, these are household names such as Citibank, JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, and the Bank of America. In France, we might think of BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, and Société Générale, or in Spain of Banco Santander, BBVA, and CaixaBank. The United Kingdom is dominated by a handful of **high-street banks**, including Lloyd’s, Barclays, HSBC, and NatWest. Japan’s banking landscape is dominated by several megabanks, called **city banks**, including MUFG Bank, SMBC, and Mizuho Bank.

Besides these well-known giants, there are thousands of smaller commercial banks. Most commercial banks in the United States are legally required to be members of the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** (**FDIC**). This is a government-owned corporation that insures the money which consumers and businesses keep in banks, at least up to a certain amount. Each quarter, the FDIC publishes a report on the financial performance of its members. According to the report from September 2024,<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> the United States counted a whopping 3,966 commercial banks with over $23 Trillion in combined assets. Most of these are smaller community and regional banks, often in rural areas. 

A similar picture emerges in Europe. While the number of credit institutions—mostly consisting of commercial banks—has been consolidating in recent years, the 27 members of the European Union still counted more than 4,614 credit institutions that were natively incorporated in 2020. Some states show much more variety than others. Germany, for example, has a much greater number of credit institutions than France and Spain, even accounting for differences in population size.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup>

So what exactly is a commercial bank? And what distinguishes it from other types of banks and financial institutions? 

While commercial banks offer a wide variety of services and, particularly the larger ones, are often complex organizations, we can offer a basic characterization that suffices for our purposes. Specifically, **commercial banks** are *for-profit* financial institutions that are marked by the following four properties and activities.

1. They manage deposits from consumers and businesses, including within accounts that offer withdrawals without notice and various payment products.
2. They extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses.
3. They require special approval from the government to operate and are subject to restrictive laws and regulations.
4. They affect the money supply, primarily through their lending activities. 

First, commercial banks accept and manage deposits on behalf of consumers and businesses. A **deposit** here is just a sum of money that is placed into a bank account. The owners of these accounts can, in turn, use these deposits in various ways.

Commercial banks notably offer bank accounts to consumers and businesses from which money can be *drawn without notice*. The main type of account here is what is typically called a **checking account** in the United States and a **current account** in the United Kingdom. Checking accounts, as we will call them, are those that consumers and businesses use on a day-to-day basis for receiving and making payments, typically through mobile or web-based software applications, or via physical or virtual payment cards. Money in these accounts can normally also be withdrawn in cash via a bank teller or more commonly from an **automated teller machine** (**ATM**). 

Besides checking accounts, commercial banks commonly offer various types of other accounts. Certain very restrictive accounts will accept deposits that cannot be withdrawn for several months or even years (except perhaps at a penalty), but they pay relatively high interest rates. In the English language, we see a variety of terms that pertain to such accounts, including **time deposits**, **term deposits**, **savings deposits**, and **certificate of deposits**. The latter is a particularly common term in the United States. 

Other types of very restrictive accounts offered by commercial banks are those specifically geared towards retirement. These accounts typically offer a means to save, invest, or a combination of both. In some cases, these **retirement accounts** are organized by employers or unions. Retirement accounts also exist that are organized by an individual, typically called **individual retirement accounts** (**IRAs**) in the United States and **personal pension accounts** in the United Kingdom. Retirement accounts come with various degrees of influence for their beneficiary with regards to the savings and investment decisions. 

Both time deposits and savings-oriented retirement accounts are generally considered to be special, very restrictive types of **savings accounts**. There are many different account products that travel by this name, but a more standard savings account might look as follows:

* Money has to be withdrawn to a checking account, either at the same bank or a different bank, before it can be used. Sometimes this process is quick and without real limitations. In other cases, the process is somewhat more restricted. 
* The account normally comes without payment cards or check books. 
* The account usually enjoys a positive interest rate, at least in fairly normal economic times. Checking accounts, because they are less restrictive, will often have no interest, or at least a lower rate of interest. 

Another common type of account offered by commercial banks is the **investment account**, where deposited funds can be used to buy stocks, bonds, and other types of investment products. These accounts sometimes also offer interest rates, and the funds have to be withdrawn to a checking account before they can be utilized more generally. Investment-oriented retirement accounts are really just restrictive investment accounts. 

Second, commercial banks are key institutions for lending to businesses and consumers. With respect to consumers, commercial banks mostly lend on a running basis through overdrafts on checking accounts and through credit cards. Commercial banks also furnish loans for, amongst other things, education, cars, and, most importantly, property purchases and renovations. With respect to businesses, commercial banks give a variety of loans that can be used for large investments and working capital. Many well-run businesses have a generous line of credit with their banks. For large commercial projects, commercial banks and other financial institutions will often join forces in extending loans. The latter types of loans are typically called **syndicated loans**. 

You will commonly hear the practice of lending referred to as “the extension of credit.” The term **credit** here just means a loaned sum of money. In Latin, “credit” means “one believes.” So the expression “the extension of credit” emphasizes the faith the lender has in the borrower to pay back the loan. A person or institution lending money is often called a **creditor**. 

Other than denoting a loaned sum of money, the term “credit” also takes on various other interrelated meanings in finance. A person or institution’s **credit**, **credit rating**, or **credit score**, for instance, is an indication of their general trustworthiness with regards to paying back loans, as in the expression “That person has good credit.” To **credit** someone’s account means just to add money to the balance of that account.

Third, commercial banks are standardly incorporated entities and require special approval from the government to operate as a bank. Most certainly in developed economies, operating as a commercial bank normally comes with strict regulatory and legal requirements that are much more extensive than those for other types of companies. The body of laws and regulations that applies to commercial banks as well as various other financial institutions is usually known as a jurisdiction’s **banking laws** or **bank laws**. So-called **compliance departments** ensure that a commercial bank meets all these requirements. 

The costs of compliance on modern commercial banks are typically very high. Many would argue that these costs are too high and that they hamper product development, customer service, and real innovation. 

A particularly salient target of criticism here are the laws and regulations which target **money laundering**: that is, the process of taking the money obtained from criminal activities and introducing it into the commercial and financial system, typically with significant efforts to conceal the criminal origins of that money. Financial crime expert Ronald Pol, for example, recently suggested the global anti-money laundering regime might be the “world’s least effective policy experiment.”<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup>

Fourth, commercial banks affect the money supply. Specifically, they can affect the supply of what is generally known as **credit money**. The latter is the money that mostly sits in our checking and savings accounts rather than the money that comes in the form of **currency**—that is, money in the form of coins and notes. 

Commercial banks affect the money supply primarily through their lending activities. Central banks attempt to steer the supply of credit money in banks indirectly through **monetary policy**. Government bodies and central banks also seek to steer the credit money supply through the regulatory regime, particularly through capital and liquidity requirements on commercial banks. 

Credit money is often used synonymously with **bank money**, that is, money which is specifically issued by banks. But the two concepts are not entirely equivalent. 

First, while banks have a practical monopoly over credit money, it is not a complete monopoly. Credit money can also be used outside of banks, properly speaking, such as within **community time banks**, where participants receive time credits for their labor, which can, in turn, be used to purchase goods and services within the community. Second, bank money can also include currency, particularly paper currency. While the practice is rare nowadays, some places in the world such as Scotland still allow banks to issue currency. 

We will delve into the details of the role of commercial banks with regards to the money supply in **Chapter 4**. For now, however, we can proffer some intuition on how commercial banks affect the money supply through the history of the **goldsmith bankers** in England. 

During the Medieval Period in England, first the Jewish community (until they were expelled by Edward I in 1290) and, somewhat later, particularly Italian merchants were formidable financial intermediaries. But the evidence strongly suggests that they acted predominantly as lenders, and did not, for instance, accept deposits on any substantial scale.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup> By the sixteenth century, various professionals were recognizable financial intermediaries, including English and foreign merchants, scriveners, and goldsmiths. But only within the Elizabethan period (1558–1603) is it the case that particularly goldsmiths seemed to have significantly turned to accepting deposits and the issue of bank money, a role that came to prominence in the seventeenth century.<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup>

The transition would have proceeded roughly as follows. Next to their competence in forging jewelry and other objects out of gold and silver, goldsmiths began to commonly store for people, in addition to other valuables, their gold and silver in the form of jewelry, specie, and bullion. By **specie** here we mean gold or silver coins issued by a government or some private institution. By **bullion** we mean gold and silver in the shape of bars and ingots. The demand for the private custodial services from goldsmiths seems to have grown particularly in the seventeenth century after the seizing of the mint by King Charles I in 1640. 

Goldsmiths would issue receipts against the gold and silver deposited with them. These were essentially **promissory notes**: a note that promises to pay a sum of money at some date or upon demand, potentially with interest. In the case of the goldsmiths, these notes generally promised to pay some sum of gold or silver upon demand.

The promissory notes were handwritten and were of various types. In some cases, they would be made out to a particular person or to the order of someone endorsed by that person. In this manner, they could be passed from person to person. In other cases, they were made out to some person or the **bearer** of the note—that is, the person in physical possession of it. Generally, however, these notes were **negotiable**: they could be transferred to others. In this way, they became as a medium of exchange and were known as **goldsmiths’ notes**.

According to Davies,<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> our earliest evidence of a goldsmith note is from 1633, and was issued by Lawrence Hoare. The note seems to indicate a deposit for the English treasury in exchange for a license to levy fines on behalf of Henry Coghill. The earliest reference to a note made out to the bearer, according to Davies,<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> comes from the diary of Samuel Pepys who was a naval administrator and politician in the seventeenth century, remembered particularly for the extensive diary he left behind. His entry of February 29 in 1668 mentions the transfer of a note from the goldsmith Colvill of the value of 600 pounds to his father.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup>

Goldsmiths also loaned out their deposits. At some point, the goldsmiths would have realized that they could extend their loans in goldsmiths’ notes and, shortly after, that they could issue goldsmiths’ notes that represented *more value* in gold and silver than they actually had on deposit. Customers, after all, would not come and collect all their gold and silver at the same time, so they (mistakenly) assumed. By issuing more notes than the specie and bullion in their vaults warranted, the goldsmiths were, in fact, not just *issuing money*, but *creating money*. 

The goldsmiths’ notes were hardly the only paper financial instruments that served as a medium of exchange in seventeenth century England.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup>Bills of exchange—which we will discuss in detail in *Section 4*—already been used as a medium of exchange for centuries and were recognized as negotiable by law at least by 1602.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup> The concept of paper currency was also much older. Marco Polo was, for example, very much surprised by his encounter with paper money in China during the Yuan Dynasty at the end of the thirteenth century.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup> That all said, the goldsmiths’ notes that were made out to a bearer really became the precursor to modern English banknotes. 

While the process of how banks create and destroy money is somewhat more abstract today than in the case of the goldsmith bankers and essentially confined to credit money, the main principle behind it is the same: the money supply is increased whenever commercial banks grant loans and reduced when those loans are paid off. 

So, in sum, characteristically, commercial banks are incorporated for-profit financial institutions which (1) manage deposits from consumers and businesses, including within accounts that offer withdrawals without notice and various payment products; (2) extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses; (3) require special approval from the government to operate and are subject to restrictive laws and regulations; and (4) affect the money supply primarily through their lending activities.

Importantly, the characterization of commercial banks offered here pertains to what we commonly call “commercial banks” in everyday language. It is not intended to have prescriptive implications. Some, for example, might balk at the suggestion that commercial banks can affect the money supply, or that they need special approval from the government to operate and are heavily regulated. The purpose here is not to express a strong opinion on those matters, but to merely to provide a baseline conceptualization of a commercial bank that allows for further discussion.


## 2. The legal foundations of commercial banks

To develop a good understanding of commercial banks, as well as banks and banking more broadly, we require some basic background on their legal foundations. We will start our discussion with the claim in the previous section that commercial banks are typically “incorporated.” What exactly does that mean?  

To start, in practically every country, there are various types of **legal structures**, which businesses and organizations can adopt upon their instantiation. These are very broad templates that describe the basic nature of the organization and have certain legal and regulatory implications. As legal structures are only broad templates, two businesses or organizations with the same legal structure can still vary greatly with regards to their business or organizational model, governance and management arrangements, operational details, and so on.

In the United States, the creation of businesses and organization is largely the prerogative of the state governments in which they will come to reside. For businesses, the main legal structures are proprietorships, general partnerships, limited liability partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability companies, S corporations, and C corporations. Nonprofit corporations and unincorporated associations are common legal structures for non-profit organizations. Sometimes collective activities are also managed via trust deeds, in which case no specific legal entity is created. Instead, the trust deed is just a contract that governs the relationship between existing legal entities. Businesses and organizations typically default to certain types of legal structures if no paperwork is filed (such as proprietorships, general partnerships, and unincorporated associations). 

We must carefully distinguish between the foundational legal structure of a business or organization and other legal distinctions that might be applied to it in the context of laws and regulations. For instance, a **venture capital fund**—broadly speaking, a company which invests money pooled from shareholders into startup companies—is a distinct type of investment company within the laws and regulations that are relevant to the work of the **US Securities and Exchange Commission** (**SEC**).<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup> However, it is not a foundational legal structure in our sense discussed here. Rather, the legal structure of a a venture capital fund is typically a limited partnership. 

The US state governments typically offer three main corporate structures: two for-profit corporate structures—the **S corporation** and the **C corporation**—and the **nonprofit corporation**. All three structures have a key document to help in its legal establishment, which provides its main aims and rights. The document is known by various names, but we will refer to it generally as the **articles of association** (also **articles of incorporation** or the **corporate charter**). You might view the articles of association as the constitution of a corporation. A key principle of any corporation is that it is a separate legal person, distinct from the owners, executives, and employees. 

In both C and S corporations, ownership is determined by the relative ownership of **shares**, and the holders of these shares are called **shareholders**. The C Corporation is available in all 50 states, though Delaware is the most popular choice due to well-developed and business-friendly corporate laws. The S Corporation is also available in most states. S Corporations are a less common choice than C Corporations by for-profit corporations because of various restrictions on S Corporations, including a limit of 100 shareholders. Shareholders of corporations only risk the capital they place within the corporation, not their other personal assets (except in extreme cases such as fraud). Corporations, in other words, are governed by the principle of **limited liability**. 

C corporations can be both **privately** and **publicly held companies**. In the former case, the shares are not traded on a public stock exchange and the companies are not required to disclose extensive information to the public about the company. Selling shares typically comes with restrictions, such as the right of first refusal (which dictates that any shares for sale should be offered to existent shareholders first). In a public company, shares are traded on public exchanges and the company has to file extensive information publicly with the SEC. Note that, as S corporations can only have a maximum of 100 owners, it is not practical for them to go public.

Just as a C and S corporation, a non-profit corporation is also considered a legal person. It does not, however, have shares or pay dividends to owners. Essentially, there are no owners. Any profits cannot be distributed and are only to be used by the organization to promote their mission. The organization is led by a board of directors who monitor an executive board that conducts day-to-day operations. Public charities, foundations, and other types of nonprofit organizations are often legally set up as nonprofit corporations. 

Jurisdictions outside of the United States (certainly with developed economies) will also offer their citizens a palette of legal structures for starting a business or organization. There are certainly important differences between jurisdictions. That said, any jurisdiction will typically have one or more legal structures for setting up corporations, or at least legal structures that are very similar in nature. We will not split hairs here about the exact meaning of the term corporation. Instead, we can understand that, for a business or organization to be **incorporated**, in broad lines, has three implications.   

* It means that the business or organization has its own legal identity, one that is separate from the owners, directors, and employees. The expression to “incorporate” pertains, thus, to the creation of a new legal identity. This is often emphasized with the expression “Corporations are people”. 
* It means there is a clear legal distinction between those who own the company—a group that can include individuals as well as other legal entities—and those who direct it. 
* Finally, incorporation means that the owners’ personal assets are protected from the debts and legal issues a corporation faces (except in certain extreme circumstances such as fraud). The owners only risk the money they have invested. They, in other words, have limited liability.  

While the details of legal structures vary within jurisdictions, most will have one or more legal structures that broadly align with incorporation as described above. And commercial banks, as stated, are typically limited to for-profit corporate structures. This is certainly the case in the United Sates. 

In the United States, commercial banks can receive their charters—a concept which we will explain shortly—from either a state or the federal government.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup> Commercial banks that operate under a state charter are designated as **state banks**. Those that are members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by it and the relevant state authorities. State banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by the FDIC and the relevant state authorities. In September 2024, roughly 20% of state banks were also members of the Federal Reserve.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup> Most states require their banks to also be members of the FDIC. A state bank must obtain a branch charter from any state other than the one of its incorporation if it seeks to have operations there.  

State banks are often organized as C corporations or S corporations with the rights to operate as a bank (via a charter). Additionally, certain states also allow commercial banks to be structured as limited liability companies (LLCs). State chartered commercial banks which are structured as an LLC can also participate in the deposit insurance program from the FDIC, as long as they have four corporate characteristics: perpetual succession, centralized management, limited liability, and free transferability of interests.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup> In some cases, an entity might have already been operating with different purposes before the organizers of the commercial bank seek to establish it as one. In other cases, the corporate entity will be created upon the founding of the commercial bank. 

State banks can only operate legally if they have a bank charter. But what exactly is a bank charter? Is it just a license?

Roughly speaking, a bank charter is a license. But strictly speaking, a bank charter is more encompassing. The expression “to charter a corporation” means to establish its legal existence with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. To “charter a bank” similarly means to establish the bank with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. The key documents which contain these goals and rights—the articles of association and sometimes additional certificates—are known as the **bank charter**, at least once approved by the proper government or legislative authorities. A physical certificate is commonly extended that serves as evidence of the bank charter.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup>

A license can be extended to a business or organization without requiring fundamental changes in its aims and rights. Hence, that commercial banks are chartered in the United States, really indicates that a new sort of business is established, regardless of whether it concerns an entirely new corporation or an existing corporation that is granted a bank charter. The bank chartering process is extensive. Any state corporation that is granted a charter becomes subject to a whole new set of regulatory and legal requirements—that is, the state’s and certain federal banking laws—next to those that apply merely to corporations.  

Commercial banks can also be granted charters at the federal level, more specifically from the **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency** (**OCC**), an independent bureau within the US Department of the Treasury. Commercial banks that are chartered and regulated by the OCC are called **national banks**. They are easily recognizable because they must include in their name the designation “national”, “national association”, or “N.A.” Commercial banks with a national bank charter are required to be members of the FDIC and the Federal Reserve. They are allowed to operate anywhere in the United States without requiring additional branch charters.

The national chartering process for commercial banks contains two main stages: the preliminary approval stage and the organization phase. The former is basically an approval of the main plans behind the new national bank, while the latter is the phase in which the national bank is actually organized (e.g., establish articles of association and an organization certificate, raise capital, develop policies and procedures, hire a number of executive officers). The OCC grants or rejects final approval at the end of the organization phase.<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup>

An intriguing aspect to national banks is that they are chartered in the deepest sense of the word. National banks are not just state-established corporations with a federal license. They are actually a special type of federal corporation.<sup>[18](#footnote18)</sup> While they have a lot of similarities to the C corporations that we see at the state level, they are their own unique entity with special rules and regulations. Therefore, unlike with a state bank, you cannot not just take an existing corporation created at the state level and turn it into a national bank. You need to create a new type of legal entity at the federal level.

Besides commercial banks, the OCC can also charter, in the full sense of the term, several other related types of for-profit corporations (including savings associations, industrial loan companies, special purpose banks, and others). Some of these entities are also officially called “national banks”. These chartering powers for the OCC are fairly extraordinary. 

To start, the legal establishment of businesses and organizations is largely the prerogative of the individual states. While many types of companies and organizations are *licensed* at a federal level—such as stock exchanges and investment companies—they are not usually “chartered”, in the full sense of the word, at the federal level. Additionally, while the federal government does occasionally charter, in the full sense of the word, for-profit enterprises, this occurs by special legislative acts in all other cases than those cases that fall under the scope of the OCC. The OCC is, thus, the only federal agency that has the prerogative to legally create for-profit enterprises.<sup>[19](#footnote19</sup>

National banks are primarily governed by the federal law on national banks and administrative rulings by the OCC, the foundation of which rests with the **National Banking Act of 1864**.<sup>[20](#footnote20)</sup> That said, many of the laws and regulations mimic state practices in corporate law. National banks are also subject to federal judicial rulings, though at the end of the 20th century, the US Supreme Court decided to defer to the states on the general standard of care for the directors of national banks.<sup>[21](#footnote21)</sup>

There are various reasons for why commercial banks should be incorporated, whether prescribed by law or not. But at least one prominent reason is the assurance of limited liability. Without limited liability, running a bank would be an extremely risky venture. In the United States, besides legal requirements, this automatically eliminates proprietorships, general partnerships, and limited partnerships, all of which come with unlimited personal liability for the debts and obligations of the enterprise for all or some of the owners. While a limited liability partnership does offer limited liability for all partners (and only for their own debts and obligations, not those of their partners), they are only allowed for certain professions like doctors, architects, and accountants.

A key aspect to modern banking is that many commercial banks are actually legal entities within more complex legal organizational structures. 

These complex structures typically consist of both **operational** and **holding companies**. An operational company is just what we would standardly understand as a company, one that actively engages in the production, distribution, and sale of goods and services. We might describe a holding company as follows:

* It does not have any real business operations of its own. Instead it holds stakes in other companies. These are frequently majority stakes, but they can also be significant minority stakes. 
* The holding company is not usually involved in the day-to-day operations of the companies they own. That is the responsibility of the company’s executives and employees. However, due to its significant stock ownership, the holding company will typically be able to exert a lot of influence through the company’s board of directors and at shareholder meetings. The influence here is mainly on a strategic level. 
* The holding company’s main goal is to profit from their holdings of companies through dividends, and companies are usually owned for a very long time. The holding company will, of course, also hold assets other than stocks in companies, such as bonds, cash, and so on. 

When an operating or holding company has a majority stake in a particular company, the latter is usually called a **subsidiary**. The owner is then often called the **parent company**. A company that is owned entirely by another operating or holding company is sometimes also called a **full subsidiary**. 

Most of the well-known brands in banking are not represented by single companies, but by complex legal organizational structures that include various holding and operational companies. The existence of complex legal organizational structures within banking necessitates additional laws and regulations. These govern, for instance, the types of activities and interactions that can take place between a commercial bank and other entities within the same legal organizational structure.<sup>[22](#footnote22)</sup>

Often the organizational legal structures behind banking brands facilitate the management of regional operations. In this manner, for instance, HSBC holdings plc—a holding company based in the United Kingdom—ultimately owns a variety of companies involved in banking and financial services, including HSBC Bank Mexico, HSBC Bank USA, HSBC Securities USA, HSBC Bank Egypt, and so on. For another example, when we refer to Citibank in the United States, we normally mean Citibank, N.A., the national bank, headquartered in New York City. This national bank is actually owned by a holding company called Citigroup. Next to Citibank, N.A., Citigroup ultimately owns the shares of many other operational and holding companies within the financial services industry.


## 3. Banking 

Commercial banks are the core modern institutions that engage in **banking**, which we here understand as the provision of four core activities: the management of deposits, the extension of credit, the issuance of bank money, and the expansion and contraction of the money supply. That said, there are various other types of institutions which also engage in all four activities. 

In the United States, the two main other institutions to know are **savings associations** (also known as **savings banks**, **thrifts**, or **savings and loans**) and **credit unions**.

Savings associations are very similar to prototypical commercial banks, but at least traditionally focus much more actively on products and services for savings, and not as much on checking and payment products. Historically, they have also focused mostly on extending retail mortgages. Many other countries have financial institutions that are similarly focused, at least historically, on offering products and services for savings, such as **building societies** in the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand; and **sparkassen** and **spaarbanken** in Germany and the Netherlands, respectively.

Many savings associations in the United States are just standard for-profit corporations owned by shareholders. Sometimes, however, savings associations are for-profit corporations that are **mutually owned**, meaning that shares can only be owned by those involved with the savings association (e.g., as account holders, borrowers). Savings associations can also have a hybrid model of ownership. While savings associations do receive their own charters—either nationally by the OCC or from a state agency—they are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks.<sup>[23](#footnote23)</sup>

Next, **credit unions** offer similar services to commercial banks and savings associations, but are formed as non-profit corporations. That is, profits are not distributed to shareholders that own the bank, but instead are utilized to improve or decrease the costs of the products and services offered. Typically these institutions are also mutually owned, as is the case with many savings associations. Rather than owning shares, however, they become a member with voting and other governance rights. These rights are typically independent of the financial stakes involved. Credit unions are typically focused on retail and small business clients. 

While savings association are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks on the national level, credit unions are actually regulated and chartered by the **National Credit Union Administration** (**NCUA**). At the state level, credit unions are sometimes chartered and regulated by the same agency as banks and savings associations. Sometimes, however, they also have their own regulatory agency. Credit unions cannot be FDIC-insured. Instead, the NCUA manages a special insurance fund for credit unions called the **National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund**.

As with savings associations, we can find institutions that are similar to the American credit unions in other countries. The building societies of the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand that were mentioned earlier are actually very much like credit unions as they are also non-profit. In Ireland, the same types of institutions are standardly called **mutual banks**. So-called **kredietbanken** in the Netherlands tend to be non-profit institutions, mutually owned by entrepreneurs which offer bank services and loans to entrepreneurs. 

Many common distinctions further clarify the nature of commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions. With a **community bank**, for example, we typically mean a commercial bank that has a local focus, and we similarly often speak of a **community credit union**. For another example, any commercial bank that only services other commercial banks and savings associations as well as their officers, directors, and employees is commonly known as a **banker’s bank**. A **corporate credit union** (also a **central credit union**) is, in turn, one that only similarly services other credit unions. 

Besides commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions, there are some more exotic entities that seem to engage in all the four core activities of banking in the United States: the management of deposits, the extension of credit, the issuance of bank money, and the expansion and contraction of the money supply.  

An **industrial loan company** (**ILC**), for example, can offer really all the same services as a commercial bank, including checking and savings accounts, though they cannot guarantee that deposits will be made available within seven days (even if in practice funds are often available sooner). They can only be chartered by certain states—and not at the federal level—and are subject to different regulatory regimes than commercial banks and savings associations. For example, ILCs can also be subsidiaries of nonfinancial companies. In this way, for example, both Toyota and BMW operate banks in the United States called the Toyota Financial Savings Bank and the BMW Bank of North America.<sup>[24](#footnote24</sup>

In sum, the main institutions in the United States that are involved in the four core activities of banking— the management of deposits, the extension of credit, the issuance of bank money, and the expansion and contraction of the money supply—are commercial banks. However, there are various other, much less prominent, types of institutions that also offer all these services. Collectively, we can somewhat loosely call them **banks and credit unions**,<sup>[25](#footnote25)</sup> even though various of those institutions, like savings associations and ILCs, are, in a legal sense, technically not always defined as banks.

Many institutions in the United States might carry the label “bank” in the name, but are not really banks in the sense of the types of institutions just described. In fact, they sometimes are fairly far removed from that notion. 

Consider, for instance, **investment banks**. These are institutions which have among their core activities helping companies and governments with raising capital (through stock and bond issuances); providing advice on mergers, acquisitions, and corporate restructurings; and helping manage the investment portfolios of clients such as pension funds, hedge funds, family offices, and wealthy individuals. Typically, these institutions would not provide commercial banking services, even though they may be part of a larger brand that also includes one or more entities occupied with commercial banking. 

In some cases, making sense of the label “bank” will be a fairly complicated exercise. A common distinction that can bring such complications is that of a **private bank**. 

Sometimes an entity called a private bank is just as commercial bank that is privately owned (i.e., the stock is not traded on a public exchange). Often, however, the term also refers to for-profit companies that are specialized in offering wealth management services to high net-worth individuals and families, a service known as **private banking**. 

The possible services that are provided by private banks in the latter sense can vary widely and may include checking and savings account services, advice on investments and taxes, estate planning, custody services, high-value mortgages, and so on. In some cases, such private banks are just specialized commercial banks. But in other cases, these “banks” are really somewhat different types of institutions. 

To avoid confusion about what is meant by banking, one sometimes might see the term deposit banking employed, particularly in historical discussions. By **deposit banking** is generally meant the provision of all the four core services under discussion here, or at least the acceptance of deposits, the extension of credit, and the issuance of, typically credit, money.   

Lastly, many other countries and regions will have banking institutions that show similarities to those in the United States, particularly in the developed economies. We have already made some comparisons throughout this section. It should be re-iterated, of course, that all countries and regions will have their own idiosyncrasies and features. And in some cases, the landscape can look substantially different. 

The four large banks that, for example, dominate China—the Bank of China, the China Construction Bank, the Agricultural Bank of China, and the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China—are predominantly state-owned. We can hardly call them commercial banks. While developed economies might also have banks that are partially state-owned, such state ownership will be much less pronounced. So, in this regard, there is a marked difference between China and the United States as well as other developed economies.


## 4. Banking, money, and payments in the colonial era

Banking and finance have a long history.<sup>[26](#footnote26)</sup>

A contract between two parties which obliges a buyer to purchase a specific commodity or financial asset at a predetermined price from the seller at some point in the future is either a **forward contract** or a **futures contract**.<sup>[27](#footnote27)</sup> The former in case the contract is customizable and privately negotiated; the latter in case the contract is standardized and traded on an organized exchange. 

These contracts are usually settled in cash, rather than in the underlying commodity or financial asset. For instance, suppose that party A agrees to buy from party B one barrel of oil for $100 on December 31, but the contract stipulates that it should be settled in cash. Suppose that a barrel on December 31 is $120. Rather than sell a barrel of oil for $100 to party A, party B just pays party A $20. The monetary benefit of the contract is essentially the same for party A, as if she would have been given a barrel of oil for $100 (at least if we assume good liquidity of the barrel of oil). 

Chinese rice producers and merchants were apparently already using forward contracts over 4,000 years ago for rice sales.<sup>[28](#footnote28)</sup> That is long before forward contracts for rice emerged in Osaka in the seventeenth century, and the establishment of the **Dojima Exchange** for futures trading in the early eighteenth century.<sup>[29](#footnote29)</sup> At roughly the same time as the ancient Chinese rice producers and merchants, several thousands of miles away, a business man in Ancient Mesopotamia made a forward agreement with three farmers to pay a certain amount of money for various crops upon harvest or at the conclusion of their business.<sup>[30](#footnote30)</sup> In the fourth century BC, Aristotle recounts the story of Thales the Milesian, who successfully speculated on the olive harvest by locking in low prices for the use of olive presses:

> He was reproached for his poverty, which was supposed to show that philosophy was of no use. According to the story, he knew by his skill in the stars while it was yet winter that there would be a great harvest of olives in the coming year; so, having a little money, he gave deposits for the use of all the olive-presses in Chios and Miletus, which he hired at a low price because no one bid against him. When the harvest-time came, and many were wanted all at once and of a sudden, he let them out at any rate which he pleased, and made a quantity of money.<sup>[31](#footnote31)</sup>

Greco-Roman maritime loans, even if not purely insurance contracts, certainly had an important insurance function. These loans typically extended to merchants—and sometimes other involved parties such as ship owners or captains—were only repaid upon the safe arrival of the goods at the destination port and, sometimes, also on the condition of the safe return of the vessel. If the journey was negatively affected due to general perils of navigation (e.g., storms, pirates), the loan did not have to be repaid under certain circumstances. The interest rates for these loans could also be freely determined, unlike for standard loans.<sup>[32](#footnote32)</sup>

This insurance function within a loan is an institution that potentially far precedes the Greco-Roman period. The **Code of Hammurabi** indicates that the ancient Babylonians legally mandated certain guarantees within debt contracts to facilitate commerce. According to law 48 of the Code, for example, “If a man has incurred a debt and a storm has flooded his field or carried away the crop, or the corn has not grown because of drought, in that year he shall not pay his creditor. Further he shall post-date his bond and shall not pay interest for that year”.<sup>[33](#footnote33)</sup> Similarly, law 102 states that, if an agent has accepted money from a merchant for a trade expedition “and an enemy has forced him to give up some of the goods he was carrying, then the agent shall specify the amount on oath and shall be acquitted.”<sup>[34](#footnote34)</sup> These legal protections might have been calculated within the interest paid on loans. 

An **annuity** is the term for a variety of financial agreements in which a, typically fixed, sum of money is payable at a regular interval to a recipient. There is widespread evidence for annuities in Roman times. Citizens and soldiers would make a lump sum or a series of payments to assure, in turn, that at a later time they would receive a series of payments for a fixed term or until the end of their life. Domitius Ulpanius was already drawing up life expectancy tables for the purpose of selling annuities around 225 B.C. Fairly sophisticated annuities existed in thirteenth century Belgium that accounted for purchasing power fluctuations. Most of the annuities in medieval Europe were coupled to land and property until the fifteenth century—meaning they were merely payments owed to some institution, family, or person that accompanied land ownership. In the early fifteenth century, however, annuities properly became a form of insurance.<sup>[35](#footnote35)</sup>

As these historical snippets with regards to forwards, futures, insurance, and annuities show, many financial instruments and services have a long history, probably at a higher level of sophistication than many might expect. It should, then, hardly be unsurprising that deposit banking practices already existed in the Ancient World, thousands of years ago, such as in Babylon, Greece, and Rome.<sup>[36](#footnote36)</sup>

After the fall of the Roman Empire, the first significant development of note in the history of banking involves the **Knights Templar**. By the thirteenth century, they were a formidable force in Europe. They are most well-known for being a Christian military order that protected pilgrims on their way to Jerusalem as well as defended the four Crusader states. In addition, however, the Knights Templar oversaw sophisticated logistical, commercial, and banking activities. With regards to deposit banking, it actually seems that the Knights Templar “established the first bank-like institutional organization in history.”<sup>[37](#footnote37)</sup>

Around Europe and in the Holy land, the Knights Templar were responsible for storing gold, silver, jewelry, and other valuables for royalty, popes, merchants, and anyone else with a need for it. They extended loans to, amongst others, English and French Kings, supported tax collection efforts for royals and popes, and were a key actor in transporting money and valuables around Europe. The Knights Templar also facilitated payments by various methods, including through straightforward credit transfers, accepting deposits to the order of third parties, and by processing **drafts**—official letters that authorized a creditor to make a withdrawal on the basis of a debtor’s deposits.<sup>[38](#footnote38)</sup>

Our next main development of note in the history of deposit banking takes place in northern and central Italy. In the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the region saw an explosive revival in the town and city life that had also characterized it until the fall of the Roman Empire. The region was marked by population growth, the birth of new towns and cities, migration to towns and cities, and a sharp increase in the volume and complexity of commercial activities.<sup>[39](#footnote39)</sup> At the expense of ecclesiastical and imperial powers, many of these towns and cities developed into partially independent communes and even fully independent Republican city states, some three hundred by the end of the twelfth century,<sup>[40](#footnote40)</sup> with their own foreign policy, fiscal independence, and so on. The most famous of these city states were, by far, Venice and Florence. 

Private deposit banking naturally emerged in the region during this time and had a significant role in fostering the growth of its commercial activities.<sup>[41](#footnote41)</sup> It clearly developed primarily out of the services offered by money exchangers. Evidence suggests that bankers were accepting deposits and enabling credit transfers in Bologna by 1245.<sup>[42](#footnote42)</sup> According to Dunbar,<sup>[43](#footnote43)</sup> the evidence suggests that deposit banking was practiced in Venice at least by 1318. The practice would surely have started earlier. Many deposit banks in various towns and cities would have been controlled by small, local operators; sometimes they would have been controlled by the international banking partnerships, typically centered around families.<sup>[44](#footnote44)</sup>

From the communes and city states of northern and central Italy, deposit banking practices spread to other parts of the continent, such as Bruges, Ghent, Barcelona, and Valencia. 

Within the Anglo-Saxon tradition, the goldsmith bankers are often attributed significant ingenuity with regards to **fractional reserve banking**—the practice of loaning out more value than the base of deposits it owns, whether specie or other money. Withers, for example, in *The Meaning of Money*, states that 

> The original goldsmith's note was a receipt for metal deposited. It took the form of a promise to pay metal, and so passed as currency. Some ingenious goldsmith conceived the epoch-making notion of giving notes, not only to those who had deposited metal, but to those who came to borrow it, and so founded modern banking.<sup>[45](#footnote45)</sup>

But it should be clear that accepting deposits and creating money through loans, typically credit money, were hardly a new invention at the time. In fact, the goldsmith bankers were fairly late with these practices, which had already left their mark on northern and central Italy as well as elsewhere on the continent for centuries. The Knights Templar and perhaps even ancient societies would have potentially also engaged already in the creation of money through lending.

The settlement and growth of Colonial America occurred against the backdrop of this long history of banking and finance. While the first permanent settlement was already achieved by 1607, the first institutional banks really only started appearing in the late eighteenth century with some, not particularly successful, exceptions.<sup>[46](#footnote46)</sup> In the absence of institutional banks, how did the colonists pay each other? <sup>[47](#footnote47)</sup>

To start, as to be expected, payments were sometimes made in specie. While some native coin minting efforts were indeed undertaken by the colonists, the amount of specie from these initiatives was economically irrelevant. There were no significant silver or gold deposits in the colonies on which these efforts could rely. It would take until the nineteenth century before substantial silver and gold deposits were discovered. Consequently, practically all the specie that circulated within the colonies was of foreign origin. While the Spanish dollar was the predominant coin in circulation at the time, the colonists would have encountered many other foreign coins, often of Spanish or Portuguese origin.

Next to specie, the colonists also made payments using paper currency that was issued by the provincial governments. These paper currencies were emitted from the late seventeenth century onwards, and generally known as **bills of credit**. A notable proponent of paper currencies at the time was Benjamin Franklin. He argued that in an environment in which specie was scarce, emissions of paper currency would stimulate production and improve the balance of trade between the colonies and England as well as the rest of Europe.<sup>[48](#footnote48)</sup>

> On the other Hand, Those who are Lovers of Trade, and delight to see Manufactures encouraged, will be for having a large Addition to our Currency: For they very well know, that People will have little Heart to advance Money in Trade, when what they can get is scarce sufficient to purchase Necessaries, and supply their Families with Provision. Much less will they lay it out in advancing new Manufactures; nor is it possible new Manufactures should turn to any Account, where there is not Money to pay the Workmen, who are discouraged by being paid in Goods, because it is a great Disadvantage to them.

The paper currency of the colonies—or bills of credit as they are typically called—generally came in two forms. 

First, bills of credit were issued by the provincial governments to pay for goods and services. Typically, the holders of these bills could, then, use them to pay taxes to be levied at a later point in time. These bills were also typically exchangeable for specie, but rarely was there a legal guarantee of such an exchange. Second, bills of credit were issued through loans collateralized against property, including land, farms, houses, and so on. Provincial governments set up specialized institutions to make these loans from the early eighteenth century onwards. They were called **land banks**. The borrower would receive bills of credit representing the loan, and would have to pay back the land bank with interest, either in specie or with paper currency.<sup>[49](#footnote49)</sup>

The colonial paper currencies were denominated in pounds, shillings, and pence. Typically they were issued to support financing for war. Given that these paper currencies would fluctuate against one another, managing payments in them could get fairly complicated. Some of these bills of credit would pay interest. Often these bills of credit were made legal tender. While they were bearer notes, these bills often were still endorsed via signatures to combat substantial counterfeiting.<sup>[50](#footnote50)</sup> Following is an example of a bill of credit issued by Massachusetts in 1713:

> THIS INDENTED TEN SHILLING BILL: Due from yProvince of Massachusetts Bay in New England *to the Possessor therereof* shall in value equal to Money and Shall be accordingly accepted by yTreasurer and Receivers subordinate to him in all Publick Payments; and for any Stock at any time in the Treasury.<sup>[51](#footnote51)</sup>

Besides various types of foreign specie and provincial bills of credit, the colonists had a further variety of methods for making payments. To obtain a well-rounded view on payments in the colonial economy, the accounts of large merchants from the time are particularly valuable to us. A great study in this regard is William Baxter’s *The House of Hancock*. It chronicles the commercial ventures of Thomas Hancock (1704–1764) and his nephew and successor John Hancock (1737–1793). 

Thomas started the family’s business from fairly humble beginnings as a book seller and stationer. But eventually he was able to build a vast family business—at least by the standards of the time<sup>[52](#footnote52)</sup>—around the import and export of goods as well as other ventures. While Thomas was a retail seller of goods as well, he would have typically acted more like a wholesaler. 

John Hancock, of course, is better known for his contributions to the American Revolution. Arguably he was the first citizen of standing to lead an act of physical resistance against the Crown when he forcibly removed two custom officers who had ventured below deck of his ship the *Lydia* without his permission.<sup>[53](#footnote53)</sup> John presided over the family business until it essentially closed in 1775, not in the least because John was a better politician than merchant.

According to Baxter’s account, Thomas (and John) accepted and made, as to be expected, payments in both specie and colonial paper currency.<sup>[54](#footnote54)</sup> But as his record keeping accounts show, while Thomas valued what he bought and sold in terms of money, specifically the native Massachusetts currency, he “seems to have handled money itself remarkably seldom”.<sup>[55](#footnote55)</sup> At least, he did not handle what we would standardly consider “money” very often in his accounts. 

Accounts such as those of the Hancocks might be biased against cash payments. It may be the case that such transactions often did not get recorded.<sup>[56](#footnote56)</sup> Nevertheless, it is clear from Baxter’s account as well as elsewhere that payments were often made via other means than specie and bills of credit. Rather than “being primitive and simple, Thomas’ books seem impossibly complicated and topsy-turvy to the accountant who has grown used to the more sedate methods of today”.<sup>[57](#footnote57)</sup>

So, what rather unusual types of exchanges were made by merchants like Thomas and John Hancock?

To start, on occasion they would have just directly exchanged goods of one type for goods of another type on the spot. This type of economic exchange is known as **barter**. 

For instance, suppose that Paul, a local grocer, wants to purchase a bible from Thomas. At least one way he could have arranged that exchange at the time without specie or paper currency is by offering Thomas some goods he would want in return. If Thomas happened to want some onions for personal consumption or to resell, the two could agree on some suitable terms, say 12 onions for one book, and successfully make the exchange.

While some direct exchanges for goods might have indeed been standard barter, more often they probably would have involved goods that merchants like Thomas and John would not use for personal consumption or for resale in the standard sense. Instead, many of these direct exchanges for goods would have involved certain commodities that were commonly known to be very easy to dispose of. So these easily-exchangeable commodities acted as a somewhat primitive form of money.<sup>[58](#footnote58)</sup> We commonly refer to this type of money as **commodity money**.

Some examples of commodity money from the Hancock books are fish, pickled pork, corn, and molasses.<sup>[59](#footnote59)</sup> But many other types of commodity moneys existed within the colonies, or at least in certain parts. Tobacco, for example, was extensively used in the southern colonies as commodity money. Supposedly Virginians even bought wives from England in 1620 with tobacco.<sup>[60](#footnote60)</sup> Corn was another common type of commodity money. 

Commodity money was not merely an informal market phenomenon. The provinces would often legally recognize commodity money. In this manner, for example, corn was made legal tender in Massachusetts in 1631, while in 1643, a Massachusetts court ruled that **wampum**—beads made from shells that were traditionally used for decorative purposes and as money by certain native North American tribes—was acceptable money for the payment of debts up to forty shillings. Maryland made tobacco legal tender in 1732. The Carolinas passed a law in 1715 that allowed payment of commodities for quit rents, including various animal skins, cheese, butter, wheat, tobacco, and so on.<sup>[61](#footnote61)</sup>

The provincial governments often attempted to fix the exchange rates with regards to specie, colonial paper currencies, and commodity monies. These kinds of measures typically had adverse consequences. 

The 1631 law that made corn legal tender in Massachusetts, for instance, fixed the exchange rate at six shillings per bushel of corn on payments for public dues. The same system was later employed by Connecticut and Newhaven. This led to a double price system, where the corn fetched higher prices in the marketplace than for the payment of public dues.<sup>[62](#footnote62)</sup> Another example of such a failed policy is Virginia’s Two Penny Act of 1758, which allowed any obligation in tobacco to be paid at a rate of two pence per pound. Tobacco prices subsequently raised sharply. Rather than paying debts in tobacco, someone could sell the tobacco at a higher price and pay their creditors at a lower rate set by the act.<sup>[63](#footnote63)</sup>

The gold and silver coins that were accepted as money in seventeenth and eighteenth century Colonial America were in some sense a very sophisticated form of commodity money. Gold and silver also have other uses than as money (e.g., jewelry). Since they had various other desirable properties such as durability, divisibility, and transportability, it makes sense why they would have generally become the most desirable form of commodity money at the time. Nevertheless, the gold and silver coins in usage were typically also backed by a sovereign power. So it’s arguably inaccurate to think of them as merely sophisticated commodity money. 

Just as we saw with gold and silver deposits with the goldsmiths in England, these commodities would often be centrally stored and notes against ownership rights would circulate as a means of payment. Tobacco notes were very common, but there were also other types of notes such as for gunpowder. The Hancock accounts show plenty of examples of these types of notes in circulation.  

All the various exchanges described so far—standard barter, direct exchanges with commodity money, and direct exchanges with notes—are all spot exchanges. While they were certainly part and parcel of economic life in the colonies, the majority of transactions were probably on the basis of **book credit**—where loans are merely just recorded as debts in an accounting book. While in some cases these debts would have just been paid in specie and colonial paper currency, much more frequently they would be paid in commodities or commodity notes.<sup>[64](#footnote64)</sup>

According to the most simple exchange within this system, Thomas would sell (or buy) some goods on account and later buy (or sell) some goods in return on account. 

For instance, suppose Thomas had sold several books to a local shoemaker worth 1 Massachusetts pound in total. Rather than collect specie, paper currency, commodity money, or some note of deposit, Thomas might just note in his records that he had sold books to the shoemaker. Probably he would have stated that the shoemaker owes him 1 pound worth of shoes, belts, or other items valued at the market rate whenever he makes some counter purchase. 

At some point down the line, the debt of the shoemaker for 1 pound could, then, be partially or fully settled by Thomas making a counter purchase of some shoes or other wares from the shoemaker. Or, he might have bought more than the shoemaker actually owed to him, and in the debtor-creditor relationship would have reversed. If Thomas and the shoemaker knew each other well, they would have probably had long-standing accounts against each other that would have facilitated many two-way exchanges without much need for settlement. 

The whole system was probably fairly self-reinforcing. As Baxter notes,<sup>[65](#footnote65)</sup> “[t]here must have been a marked tendency to buy from a man simply because he was one’s debtor, and possibly the best way to get orders from an unfriendly merchant was to run deep into his debt”.

Importantly, note that many of these transactions were typically valued in currency. It would not have been typical in the example above for Thomas to have agreed with the shoemaker that he owes him two pairs of shoes at a later point, as in a standard barter transaction. That makes some sense in an economy where credit and debit balances would often facilitate many exchanges over a long period of time, so that individual exchanges never had to be exactly settled. It would have exposed such outstanding credit, however, to currency depreciation. And many debtors accordingly attempted to stretch repayment of the debt as long as possible.

Many times the flow of goods in these transactions on credit would not just be two-way, but triangular.<sup>[66](#footnote66)</sup> If some debtor to Thomas offered to make payment in goods that were not desired directly by him, he would often attempt to find one of his creditors that might want them in order to (partially) settle his debt. The debtor could, then, pay that creditor directly.  Thomas would also be involved at the endpoints of these triangular arrangements. Apparently, they were very commonplace. As noted by Baxter, “The records teem with references to them, and show that they were developed to a high pitch of complexity”.<sup>[67](#footnote67)</sup>

In some cases, the exchange could even become more complex. In one note, for instance, John is asked to deliver two barrels of Pork to Mr. Gault on behalf of Mr. Dawes, or to someone else desired by Mr. Gault. Perhaps John had a debt to Mr. Dawes who wanted to settle his own debt with Mr. Gault. But rather than delivering the barrels to Mr. Gault, the latter could also direct John to send the barrels to satisfy one of Mr. Gault’s creditors.<sup>[68](#footnote68)</sup>

Book credit as just described was, thus, vital to Thomas’ dealings with other colonists. Thomas’ customers included other merchants of varying sizes in Boston and elsewhere. And they all “expected Thomas to be excessively liberal in the matter of credit. Seldom seeing money and having no banks, they looked to their storekeeper for financial aid. He had to give it or be content with poor trade”.<sup>[69](#footnote69)</sup> Commonly credit was extended for 6 months to a year, or even longer.<sup>[70](#footnote70)</sup> While interest rates for credit are often not mention, they may have been an implicit charge within the price of the goods.<sup>[71](#footnote71)</sup>

An expansive credit system was not just used with other colonial merchants. Instead, it also “formed the basis of most transatlantic business until bankers entered the picture during the next century, and it certainly was the key to Thomas’ dealings with England”.<sup>[72](#footnote72)</sup> To maintain a system where imports and exports happen via a credit system, goods will have to flow both ways. But unlike the Middle and Southern Colonies, New England had much less to offer directly to English merchants. Hence, much of the Hancocks’ energies were spent on developing various additional trade routes to ensure a continued two-way flow of goods with England. Those new trade routes, of course, would also have to rely on a two-way flow of goods.

This experience was hardly unique for Thomas and John. While it is difficult to measure exactly as many cash transactions seemed to have not been recorded at the time, it is clear that everywhere throughout colonial America, a sophisticated web of book credit was essential to greasing the wheels of commerce.<sup>[73](#footnote73)</sup>

Many of the triangular or more complex contracts would have been facilitated by bills of exchange or more primitive contracts and letters that performed the same function. We have already mentioned bills of exchange in *Section 3*, but it is useful to explain in more detail here. Specifically, a **bill of exchange** is a type of financial contract which instructs one party, the drawee, to make a payment to another party, the payee, by a certain date. The party that created the bill of exchange is known as the drawer. A common way to use this financial contract is in international trade. 

Suppose that company B in New York imports coats from company A in London. And that company C in Canada imports shoes from company B in New York. The amount C owes to B is less than the amount B owes to A. Rather than C paying B, B paying A, B can create a bill of exchange. In the bill, C is asked to transfer to A the amount owed to B. In this case, B is the drawer, C is the drawee, and A is the payee. The remainder of the amount B owes to A can be settled at a later date or by some additional payment from B to A. Particularly in the past when money was difficult to move around, there would have been a lot of efficiency gains from these types of contracts. 

Many of the complicated flows of goods within the accounts of merchants like the Hancocks would have been facilitated by bills of exchange. But as bills of exchange could also be endorsed to others, they would have often just been accepted as a means of payment directly: rather than make a claim on the money or goods offered by the bill of exchange, the bill itself could be utilized as a means of payment to others without ever collecting on it. This is similar to how commodity notes were utilized. 

In sum, the colonial American experience with payments was very heterogeneous. While colonial Americans would have indeed made payments in various types of foreign specie and provincial bills of credit, they would have more commonly made payments using commodities, commodity notes, and other paper instruments such as bills of exchange. Typically, these exchanges would not have been spot exchanges. Instead, they would have occurred within the context of an expansive system of book credit. 

In this environment without institutional deposit banking, particularly merchants fulfilled standard banking functions.  

To start, many larger merchants facilitated straightforward transfers of credits between their business relationships. Often the times the instructions for these transfers were made fairly informally. In this way, for instance, correspondents with Hancock wrote simple notes that requested to “discount with Mr. Sameul Gray fourty pounds Lawful Money for Fish I received from him.”<sup>[74](#footnote74)</sup> As stated by Baxter:

[T]he Hancocks and their contemporaries were experts arranging straight-forward transfers of credit. Such transfers were similar in principle to modern banking or bill-of-exchange transactions; but there were no banks and seldom any documents that the modern lawyer would call bills. Any merchant who had a sufficient number of accounts in his ledger would play the part of banker, by putting cross-entries through his books, while the most informal requests take the place of our stereotyped checks and bills as the signals for these credit transfers.<sup>[75](#footnote75)</sup>

Smaller local merchants would have also offered such credit transfers within their communities. Frictions may have arisen in those cases where the merchant held a much lesser opinion of the relationship seeking to assume more debt.<sup>[76](#footnote76)</sup> Particularly larger merchants would also have facilitated other types of payments, including cash withdrawals or remittances to Europe. 

Merchants also held and collected money for people, and even made investments on their behalf. Baxter’s account mention, for example, a Mr. Kilby for which Thomas collected a debt from the Massachusetts treasury and invested it partially in government bonds.<sup>[77](#footnote77)</sup> Certainly in many of these cases, the merchants would have issued and created “money” in the form of credit or notes. According to Markham, for example, a Boston merchant, Nathaniel Wheelwright, had begun acting as a banker in Boston. He had issued interest-bearing notes on deposits, which were circulating as currency. When he went bankrupt, however, there was a panic in Boston regarding the value of these notes.<sup>[78](#footnote78)</sup>
