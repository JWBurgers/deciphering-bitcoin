# Chapter 1: Banks and banking

Banks are at the core of money and payments. Like it or not, they are
some of the most fundamental institutions that make our society run (and
sometimes come to a screeching halt). To understand anything about money
and payments, we, therefore, require a background on banks and banking.

We start in *Section 1* by characterizing, specifically, commercial
banks. They are by far the most important type of bank in the United
States. After setting out a broad characterization of commercial banks,
we examine their legal foundations in *Section 2*, including their legal
structure—the corporation—and their legal and regulatory regime. In
*Section 3*, we complete our description of the banking landscape in the
United States with a discussion of savings associations, credit unions,
and other types of deposit banks.

## 1. Commercial banks

We can distinguish between various types of banks. But by the far the
most important type of bank in the global financial landscape is what in
the United States is called the commercial bank.

Commercial banks are key credit providers to consumers and businesses.
They offer many products and services for payments, savings, and
investments. They employ a great number of people. And a somewhat less
well-known fact, commercial banks play a crucial role in determining the
overall money supply. Unsurprisingly, their activities and financial
performance have a massive impact on the overall state of an economy.

In the United States, the commercial banking sector is largely
controlled by a few household names such as Citibank, JPMorgan Chase,
Wells Fargo, and the Bank of America. Most other countries across the
world are similarly dominated by a handful of commercial banks.

- In France, the behemoths include BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, and
  Société Générale, and in Spain they include Banco Santander, BBVA, and
  CaixaBank.

- The United Kingdom is dominated by Lloyd's, Barclays, HSBC, and
  NatWest. These institutions are often also called **high-street
  banks** because they are commercial banks that maintain an elaborate
  network of physical branches.

- Australian commercial banking is largely controlled by the
  Commonwealth Bank, ANZ, WestPac, and NAB.

- Japan's banking landscape is dominated by several megabanks, including
  MUFG Bank, SMBC, and Mizuho Bank. Because these large commercial banks
  are active primarily in urban areas, they are more specifically called
  **city banks**. While the name "commercial bank" is common in France,
  Spain, Australia, and the United Kingdom, commercial banks in Japan
  are, instead, generally known as **ordinary banks** (*futsu ginko*).

Besides these well-known banking giants, there are thousands of smaller
commercial banks that operate in the world's financial landscape.

Most commercial banks in the United States are legally required to be
members of the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** (**FDIC**).
This is a government-owned corporation which insures the money that
consumers and businesses keep in banks, at least up to a certain amount.
Each quarter, the FDIC publishes a report on the financial performance
of its member banks. According to the quarterly report from September
2024,[^1] the United States counted a whopping 3,966 commercial banks
with over \$23 Trillion in combined assets. Most of these were smaller
community and regional banks, often in rural areas.

A similar picture emerges in Europe. While the number of credit
institutions—mostly consisting of commercial banks—has been
consolidating in recent years, the twenty-seven members of the European
Union still counted more than 4,614 credit institutions that were
natively incorporated in 2020. Some member states show much more variety
than others. Germany, for example, has a much greater number of credit
institutions than France and Spain, even accounting for differences in
population size.[^2]

So what exactly is a commercial bank? And what distinguishes it from
other types of banks and financial institutions?

While commercial banks offer a wide variety of services and,
particularly the larger ones, are often complex organizations, a fairly
basic characterization suffices. Specifically, **commercial banks** are
*for-profit* financial institutions that are marked by the following
five characteristics.

1.  They provide bank accounts to consumers and businesses that have a
    balance in credit money, in particular accounts that offer
    withdrawals without notice. The latter type of account is standardly
    utilized for making and receiving payments.

2.  They extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses.

3.  They are virtually always legally structured as corporations.

4.  They require special approval from the government to operate and are
    subject to restrictive laws and regulations.

5.  They affect the money supply, primarily through their lending
    activities.

First, the money that sits in the checking and savings accounts of
consumers and businesses is normally called **credit money**. The other
main form of money for the general public is called **currency**, which
is official money in the form of coins and notes.

In most jurisdictions, official coins and notes exclusively have the
status of **legal tender**. The main implication is that they must be
accepted by creditors to discharge of a private debt, unless that debt
was contracted with a specification of alternative means of payment
(such as a bank transfer).[^3] Contrary to some popular misconceptions,
coins and notes are usually not required to be accepted for the delivery
of goods and services because of their legal tender status. The credit
money in private checking and savings accounts is not legal tender, as
such money is not formally issued by governments. However, credit money
is commonly used to settle debts, more commonly in practice than
official currency.

The balance of credit money in a bank account is determined by deposits,
withdrawals, and, if applicable, interest payments and fees.

Account owners can increase their balance of credit money through
deposits. A **deposit** here is just an amount of credit money that is
added to a bank account as a result of a transfer in credit money from
another account—say a payment for goods or services, a gift, a tax
return, or a monthly salary—or an exchange of cash with the bank. A
credit money balance can also be increased through interest payments
from the bank.

Commercial banks notably offer bank accounts to consumers and businesses
from which money can be *drawn without notice*. The main type of account
here is what is typically called a **checking account** in the United
States and a **current account** in the United Kingdom.

Checking accounts, as we will call them, are those that consumers and
businesses use on a day-to-day basis for receiving and making payments,
typically through mobile or web-based software applications, or via
physical or virtual payment cards. Money in these accounts can normally
also be withdrawn in cash via a bank teller or more commonly from an
**automated teller machine** (**ATM**). Usually checking accounts come
with fees and receive relatively little or no interest.

Besides checking accounts, commercial banks commonly provide various
types of other accounts. Certain very restrictive accounts only allow a
single deposit when you open them, and do not allow withdrawals until
after several months or even years after the deposit has been made
(except perhaps at a penalty). These accounts pay relatively high
interest rates. In the English language, we see a variety of terms that
pertain to such accounts, including **time deposit accounts**, **term
deposit accounts**, and **certificate of deposit accounts**. The latter
is a particularly common term in the United States.[^4]

Other types of restrictive accounts offered by commercial banks are
those specifically geared towards retirement. These accounts typically
offer a means to save, invest, or a combination of both. In some cases,
these **retirement accounts** are organized by employers or unions.
Retirement accounts also exist that are organized by individuals
independently, typically called **individual retirement accounts**
(**IRAs**) in the United States and **personal pension accounts** in the
United Kingdom. Retirement accounts come with various degrees of
influence regarding savings and investment decisions. There are normally
restrictive conditions on withdrawal until a certain age or working-life
milestone has been reached.

Both time deposit and savings-oriented retirement accounts are generally
considered to be special, restrictive types of **savings accounts**.
There are many different account products that travel by this name, but
a more standard savings account might look as follows:

- Money has to be withdrawn to a checking account, either at the same
  bank or a different bank, before it can be used. Sometimes this
  process is quick and without real limitations. In other cases, the
  process is somewhat more restricted.

- The account normally comes without payment cards or check books.

- The account usually enjoys a positive interest rate, at least in
  fairly normal economic times. Checking accounts, because they are less
  restrictive, will often have no interest, or at least a lower rate of
  interest.

Another common type of account offered by commercial banks is the
**investment account**, where deposited funds can be used to buy stocks,
bonds, and other types of investment products. These accounts sometimes
also offer interest rates on the credit money balance, and the funds
have to be withdrawn to a checking account before they can be utilized
more generally. Investment-oriented retirement accounts are essentially
just restrictive investment accounts.

The second main characteristic of commercial banks is that they are key
institutions for lending to businesses and consumers. With respect to
consumers, commercial banks lend on a running basis through overdrafts
on checking accounts and through credit cards. They also furnish formal
loans for, amongst other things, education, cars, and, most importantly,
property purchases and renovations. With respect to businesses, many
have a generous line of credit through overdrafts on their bank accounts
and company credit cards. Commercial banks also give formal loans that
can be used for large investments, working capital, debt refinancing,
and other matters. For very large loans, commercial banks and other
financial institutions often join forces in extending them. Such loans
are called **syndicated loans**.

You will commonly hear the practice of lending referred to as "the
extension of credit." The term **credit** in this expression just means
a loaned sum of money. In Latin, "credit" means "one believes." So the
expression "the extension of credit" emphasizes the faith the lender has
in the borrower to pay back the loan. A person or institution lending
money is often called a **creditor**. Commercial banks extend credit
through formal loans as well as through overdrafts on accounts and
credit cards.

The term \"credit\" is polysemous, taking on various interrelated
meanings in finance beyond its basic sense of a loaned sum of money. A
person or institution's **credit**, **credit rating**, or **credit
score**, for instance, is an indication of their general trustworthiness
with regards to paying back loans, as in the expression "That person has
good credit." To **credit** someone's account means just to add money to
the balance of their account. And as we have already mentioned, credit
money is money that exists as an accounting entry, particularly in our
checking and savings accounts.

Next, commercial banks are standardly corporations that require special
approval from the government to operate and subject to extensive laws
and regulations. Most certainly in developed economies, operating as a
commercial bank normally comes with strict regulatory and legal
requirements that are much more extensive than those for other types of
companies. The body of laws that applies to commercial banks as well as
certain other financial institutions is usually known as a
jurisdiction's **banking laws**. So-called **compliance departments** as
well as legal departments typically ensure that a commercial bank meets
all these requirements.

The costs of compliance on modern commercial banks are normally very
high. Many would argue that these costs are too high and that they
hamper product development, customer service, and real innovation. A
particularly salient target of criticism here are the laws and
regulations which aim to prevent **money laundering**: that is, the
introduction of money obtained from criminal activities into the
commercial and financial system, typically with significant efforts to
conceal the criminal origins of that money. Financial crime expert
Ronald Pol, for example, has recently suggested that the global
anti-money laundering regime might be the "world's least effective
policy experiment."[^5]

Finally, commercial banks affect the money supply. The most important
mechanism in this regard is their lending activities, which impacts the
overall supply of credit money in our checking and savings accounts.
Central banks attempt to steer lending activities from banks through
**monetary policy**. Government bodies and central banks also seek to
steer the behavior of commercial banks through the regulatory regime,
particularly through capital and liquidity requirements.

We should, at this point, make a small clarification on the term credit
money. It is often used synonymously with **bank money**, that is, money
which is specifically issued by banks. But the two concepts are not
entirely equivalent.

First, while banks have a practical monopoly over credit money—as it
mostly sits in our checking and savings accounts—it is not a complete
monopoly. Credit "money" can arguably also exist outside of banks,
properly speaking. For instance, in **community time banks**
participants receive time credits for their labor, which can then be
used to purchase goods and services within the community. Second, while
banks can indeed issue credit money, primarily by extending new loans,
they can also issue paper currency. Although this practice is rare
nowadays, some jurisdictions, such as Scotland and Hong Kong, still
allow banks to issue paper currency. By contrast to the issuance of
credit money which is usually newly created, the issuance of paper money
by commercial banks is standardly the product of the transformation from
one form of money into another.

So, in sum, characteristically, commercial banks are for-profit
financial institutions which (1) provide accounts to consumers and
businesses, notably checking accounts; (2) extend credit extensively to
consumers and businesses; (3) are almost invariably corporations; (4)
require special approval from the government to operate and are subject
to restrictive laws and regulations; and (5) substantially affect the
money supply, primarily through their lending activities.

The characterization of commercial banks offered here pertains to what
we commonly call "commercial banks" in everyday language. It is
important to realize that the terms "banks", "banking", and "bankers"
are used with a fairly wide variety of broadly interrelated meanings in
everyday language. An investment bank, for example, is quite a different
type of institution than a commercial bank, even though they are
commonly called "banks".

For clarity, therefore, we sometimes speak of **deposit banks**, which
is the collective name for institutions that accept cash and credit
deposits from the public; facilitate payments with credit money; make
loans to consumers and businesses; and affect the money supply,
primarily through lending. A commercial bank as described above is
really the most important type of deposit bank in the modern world.

Of the five characteristics of commercial banks provided above, the
first two should be fairly intuitive to anyone with a passing
familiarity of those institutions. The last three characteristics are
surely more obscure to laymen. The fact that commercial banks play such
a substantial role within our money supply probably sounds rather juicy,
or even scandalous, to some. Given that it is such an encompassing
topic, however, we must defer discussion of it to later chapters.
Instead, we will for now only turn to two more mundane, though no less
important, characteristics of commercial banks: that they are almost
invariably corporations and subject to restrictive laws and regulations.

## 2. Legal foundations

To develop a good understanding of commercial banks, as well as banks
and banking more broadly, we require some background on their legal
foundations. We will start our discussion with the claim in *Section 1*
that commercial banks are almost invariably corporations. What exactly
does that mean?

In practically every country, there are various types of **legal
structures**, which businesses and organizations can adopt upon their
instantiation. These are broad templates that describe the basic nature
of the organization and have important legal and regulatory
implications. As legal structures are only broad templates, two
businesses or organizations with the same legal structure can still vary
greatly with regards to their business or organizational model, specific
governance and management arrangements, operational details, and so on.

In the United States, the establishment of companies and organizations
is largely the prerogative of the state governments in which they will
come to reside. The available legal structures vary somewhat by state.
But businesses commonly come in the form of proprietorships, general
partnerships, limited liability partnerships, limited partnerships,
limited liability companies, S corporations, and C corporations. They
typically default to certain types of legal structures if no paperwork
is filed (such as proprietorships and general partnerships).

We must carefully distinguish between the foundational legal structure
of a business and other legal distinctions that might be applied to it
in the context of laws and regulations. For instance, a **venture
capital fund**—broadly speaking, a company which invests money pooled
from shareholders into startup companies—is a distinct type of
investment company within the laws and regulations that are relevant to
the work of the **US Securities and Exchange Commission** (**SEC**).[^6]
However, it is not a foundational legal structure in our sense discussed
here. Rather, the foundational legal structure of a venture capital fund
is normally a limited partnership.[^7]

A key principle of any corporation is that it is a separate legal
person, distinct from the owners, executives, and employees. We can say
that it has **legal personhood**. This attribute has a profound number
of implications, including that the corporation has the ability to sue
and be sued in its own name. The distinct legal personality of a
corporation is often captured by the phrase "corporations are people".

One might use the term "corporation" in a thin sense where all it means
is that an entity has legal personhood. However, with the statement that
banks are "almost invariably corporations" we mean more specifically
that they are standardly corporations in the more common sense of that
term. That is, they are for-profit enterprises that are also marked by
the following attributes in their legal structure: limited liability,
entity shielding, a distinction between owners and management, and the
transferability of shares.

In corporations, ownership is determined by the relative ownership of
**shares**, and the holders of these shares are called **shareholders**.
Shareholders are also often referred to as **stockholders**, though
originally the term "stock" would have referred to the company's
capital, goods, or both. The owners of the corporation are legally
distinct from the management, employees, and other associates of the
company. Particularly in smaller companies, however, executives commonly
also have an ownership position.

The rights and duties of owners and management are typically laid out in
various legal documents. In the U.S., one key document is often known as
the **articles of association** (also **articles of incorporation** or
the **corporate charter**). It supports the legal establishment of the
corporation and describes its main aims and the high-level rights of
shareholders. You might view the articles of association as the
constitution of a U.S. corporation. Further rights and duties,
particularly for management, are often captured by the corporation's
**bylaws**. There is substantial variety in how exactly corporations are
governed across and within legal jurisdictions. While the United States,
for example, relies on a **one-tier board model**—where both
executives and non-executives sit on a single board—European countries
typically rely on a **two-tier board model**—where a clear distinction
exists between a management board and a supervisory board.

Corporations in the U.S. can be both **privately** and **publicly held
companies**. In the former case, the shares are not traded on any public
stock exchange. Selling shares in a privately held company typically
comes with restrictions, such as the right of first refusal (which
dictates that any shares for sale should be offered to existent
shareholders first). With a public company, shares are instead traded on
one or more **public stock exchanges** such as the New York Stock
Exchange or the Nasdaq Exchange. Share owners can buy and sell shares
largely as they please, as long as there are willing counterparties to
the trade. Public companies are subject to much stricter registration
and information requirements with the SEC than private companies. The
distinction between public and private corporations typically also
exists in legal jurisdictions elsewhere than the United States.

Corporations can issue new shares to raise capital. The market for the
sale of such shares for public companies is often known as the **primary
market**. Any shares bought on the primary market can, then, be bought
and sold with various degrees of restrictions in the **secondary
market**. Occasionally, public corporations purchase and sell their own
shares on the secondary market. Sometimes these terms—primary market
and secondary market—are also used in the context of private
companies.

The transfer in shares—certainly in public corporations, but also in
private companies—is facilitated by the fact that corporations have
what Hansmann, Kraakman, and Squire call "strong entity shielding": that
is, corporations are protected from attempts by shareholders and their
personal creditors to liquidate the company. Only in special
circumstances can a shareholder liquidate a corporation. At a minimum,
it requires the owner hold a majority or supermajority of shares, as is
specified in the articles of association. The importance to the modern
corporation of **entity shielding**—or the protection of it from its
owners and their personal creditors—has often been underappreciated
compared to that of limited liability.[^8]

The latter notion refers to the fact that shareholders of corporations
only risk the capital they utilized to purchase their shares, not their
other personal assets (except in extreme cases such as fraud). The
personal assets of owners are, in other words, shielded from any
creditors to the corporation in the case of bankruptcy. This principle
is commonly known as **limited liability**. To be precise, limited
liability here means *full* *limited liability*. While *partial limited
liability*—where an owner's personal assets are protected beyond a
certain point from creditors—has now practically disappeared from the
corporate landscape, it was a common property of corporations within the
US and the UK in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.[^9]

The US state governments typically offer two main corporate forms to
businesses—the **C corporation** and the **S corporation**. The C
Corporation is available in all 50 states, though Delaware is the most
popular choice due to well-developed and business-friendly corporate
laws. The S Corporation is also available in most states. The latter is
mostly used for private companies. As S corporations can only have a
maximum of 100 owners, it is not practical for them to "go public".

So far we have described corporations in the typical modern sense of
for-profit enterprises with certain attributes such as limited
liability. It should be noted, however, that in the US and elsewhere
certain legal entities closely resemble the modern corporation, but are
intended for non-profit purposes. In the US, this legal structure is
formally **called the non-profit corporation**. Just as a C and S
corporation, a non-profit corporation is also considered a legal person,
and is also marked by a separation between management and owners,
limited liability, and entity shielding. It does not, however, have
shares or pay dividends to owners. Essentially, there are no owners. Any
profits cannot be distributed and are only to be used by the
organization to promote its mission.

In the US, public charities, foundations, and other types of nonprofit
organizations are often legally set up as nonprofit corporations. As we
will see in *Section 3*, credit unions, which engage in similar
activities to commercial banks, are organized as non-profit corporations
in the United States.

So, globally, commercial banks, as stated, are typically corporations in
the modern sense with limited liability, entity shielding, and other
such features. Exceptions do exist, of course. The English banks C.
Hoare & Company and Coutts & Company both trace their origins to
goldsmiths that began offering deposit banking services in the
seventeenth century (1672 and 1692, respectively).[^10] They are both
private unlimited companies. While that means they are still legally
separate entities from their owners, there is no limited liability for
the owners, a key aspect of the modern corporation.

We are unaware of any commercial banks in the United States that are not
legally structured as modern corporations. Admittedly, next to the C and
S corporate form, some states do allow commercial banks to be structured
as limited liability companies. However, these limited liability company
banks generally seem to have most or all of the attributes of modern
corporations. So this group of commercials banks does not really seem to
offer a clear exception.

The ubiquity of corporate commercial banking is, of course, grounded in
American banking laws. But those laws are surely inspired by various
advantages to the modern corporation. Without limited liability, running
a bank appears an extremely risky venture. A bank corporation also seems
to offer more stability and continuity than, say, a partnership. While
those sentiments are probably shared by many experts on banking, they
are not universally shared. Particularly in the past, there was
substantial skepticism about banking with full limited liability.

Before the collapse of the **City of Glasgow Bank** in 1878 and several
other banks in its wake, for example, there was a strong conviction in
the United Kingdom that unlimited liability for shareholders in a bank
constrained excessive risk taking. Most banks were, in fact, structured
as unlimited liability companies, even though regimes of partial or full
liability were available.[^11] Goodhart and Postel-Vinay argue that
partial limited liability for insider shareholders was dismissed too
quickly at the time and should be reconsidered.[^12]

Lets now move to banking laws and regulations. In the US, commercial
banks can receive their charters—a concept which we will explain
shortly—from either a particular state or the federal government.[^13]

Commercial banks that operate under a state charter are designated as
**state banks**. Those that are members of the Federal Reserve are
regulated by it and the relevant state authorities. State banks that are
not members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by the FDIC and the
relevant state authorities. In September 2024, roughly 20% of state
banks were also members of the Federal Reserve.[^14] Most states require
their banks to also be members of the FDIC. A state bank must obtain a
branch charter from any state other than the one of its incorporation if
it seeks to have operations there.

State banks are often organized as C corporations or S corporations with
the rights to operate as a bank (via a charter). Larger banks are mostly
structured as C Corporations. Smaller community banks are also
frequently structured as S corporations.[^15] As already mentioned,
certain states also allow commercial banks to be structured as limited
liability companies. State chartered commercial banks which are
structured in that way can also participate in the deposit insurance
program from the FDIC, as long as they have four corporate
characteristics: perpetual succession, centralized management, limited
liability, and free transferability of interests.[^16] In some cases, an
entity might have already been operating with different purposes before
the organizers of the commercial bank seek to establish it as one. In
other cases, the corporate entity will be created upon the founding of
the commercial bank.

State banks can only operate legally if they have a bank charter. But
what exactly is a bank charter? Is it just a license?

Roughly speaking, a bank charter is a license. But strictly speaking, a
bank charter is more encompassing. The expression "to charter a bank"
means to establish its legal existence with various goals and rights via
the state's authority. Even if a corporation long-existed before being
chartered as a bank, it will have to make fundamental changes to its
constitutive legal documents, primarily the articles of association. In
modern times, state approval of a bank proceeds via an extensive
bureaucratic process. Once a formal approval has been granted by the
proper authorities on an application, a formal document or certificate
is commonly extended that serves as evidence of that approval. This is
known as a **bank charter**.

A license, by contrast, can be extended to a business or organization
without requiring fundamental changes in its aims and rights. Hence,
that commercial state banks have to be chartered, really indicates that
a new sort of business is established, even if it is an existing
corporation that is granted a bank charter. The bank chartering process
is extensive. Any state corporation that is granted a charter becomes
subject to a whole new set of regulatory and legal requirements—that
is, the state's and certain federal banking laws and regulations—next
to those that apply merely to corporations.

Commercial banks can also be granted charters at the federal level from
the **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency** (**OCC**), an
independent bureau within the US Department of the Treasury. Commercial
banks that are chartered and regulated by the OCC are called **national
banks**. They are easily recognizable because they must include in their
name the designation "national", "national association", or "N.A."
Commercial banks with a national bank charter are required to be members
of the FDIC and the Federal Reserve. Unlike state banks, national banks
are allowed to operate anywhere in the United States without requiring
additional branch charters.

The national chartering process for commercial banks contains two main
stages: the preliminary approval stage and the organization phase. The
former is basically an approval of the main plans behind the new
national bank, while the latter is the phase in which the national bank
is actually organized. This includes the establishment of articles of
association and an organization certificate, the raising of capital, the
development of policies and procedures, and the hiring of a number of
executive officers. The OCC grants or rejects final approval at the end
of the organization phase.[^17]

An intriguing aspect to national banks is that they are chartered in the
deepest sense of the word. National banks are not just state
corporations that received a federal license. They are actually a
special type of federal corporation.[^18] While their corporate form
resembles that of the C corporation at the level of the states, it is a
unique type of entity with special rules and regulations. Hence,
obtaining is national charter is not just turning an existing
corporation created at the state level into a national bank. You are,
instead, creating an entirely new type of legal entity at the federal
level.

Besides commercial banks, the OCC can also charter, in the full sense of
the term, several other related types of for-profit corporations
(including savings associations, industrial loan companies, special
purpose banks, and others). Some of these entities are also officially
called "national banks". These chartering powers for the OCC are fairly
extraordinary.

To start, the legal establishment of businesses and organizations is
largely the prerogative of the individual states. While many types of
companies and organizations are *licensed* at a federal level—such as
stock exchanges and investment companies—they are not usually
*chartered*, in the full sense of the word, at the federal level.
Additionally, while the federal government does occasionally charter, in
the full sense of the word, for-profit enterprises, this occurs by
special legislative acts in all other cases than those cases that fall
under the scope of the OCC. The OCC is, thus, the only federal agency
that has the prerogative to legally create for-profit enterprises.[^19]

National banks are primarily governed by the federal law on national
banks and administrative rulings by the OCC, the foundation of which
rests with the **National Banking Act of 1864**.[^20] That said, many of
the laws and regulations mimic state practices in corporate law.
National banks are also subject to federal judicial rulings, though at
the end of the twentieth century, the US Supreme Court decided to defer
to the states on the general standard of care for the directors of
national banks.[^21]

A final key aspect to the legal organization of modern banking is that
most commercial banks are actually *not* independent companies. Instead,
they are usually a member of a **banking group**: a collection of banks
and, often, other financial companies that are connected in their
ownership.

The legal organization behind a banking group typically consist of both
operational and holding companies. An **operational company** is just
what we would standardly understand as a company, one that actively
engages in the production, distribution, and sale of goods and services.
We might, in turn, describe a **holding company** as follows:

- It does not have any real business operations of its own. Instead it
  holds stakes in other companies. These are frequently majority stakes,
  but they can also be significant minority stakes.

- The holding company is not usually involved in the day-to-day
  operations of the companies they own. That is the responsibility of
  the company's executives and employees. However, the holding company
  will typically exert a lot of influence through the company's board of
  directors and shareholder meetings. The influence here is mainly on a
  strategic level.

- The holding company's main goal is to profit from their holdings of
  companies through dividends, and companies are usually owned for a
  very long time. The holding company will, of course, also usually hold
  assets other than stocks in companies, such as bonds, cash, and so on.

When an operating or holding company has a majority stake in a
particular company, the latter is usually called a **subsidiary**. The
owner is then often called the **parent company**. A company that is
owned entirely by another operating or holding company is sometimes also
called a **full subsidiary**.

Most commercial banks are legally, thus, but a node in a network of
operating and holding companies. At the top of the network standardly
sits a single holding company. The existence of banking groups
necessitates additional laws and regulations. These govern, for
instance, the types of activities that can take place between a
commercial bank and other banks or financial companies within the same
banking group.[^22]

While banking groups are typically centered around a main brand, they
often contain multiple brands. For example, HSBC holdings—the top
level holding company for the HSBC group—also owns the Hang Seng Bank
in Hong Kong (which, in turn, owns the Hang Seng Bank in China) as well
as the Saudi Awwal Bank. For another example, Citigroup—the top level
holding company which also owns Citibank, N.A. in the United
States—also owns the Banco Nacional de México, one of the largest
banks in Mexico.

While the choices in the legal organizational structure of a banking
group can be motivated by various concerns—including the separation of
business activities and the management of separate brands—it is often
at least partially motivated by geographical and jurisdictional
considerations. In this manner, for instance, HSBC holdings ultimately
owns a variety of companies involved in banking and financial services,
including HSBC Bank Mexico, HSBC Bank USA, HSBC Securities USA, HSBC
Bank Egypt, and so on. Citigroup, in turn, owns, amongst many other
subsidiaries, Citibank Canada, Citibank Japan, and Citibank, N.A., the
U.S. national bank headquartered in New York City.

Organizing banking groups via a top-level holding company is not a
modern invention. As has been well-documented by the Belgian-American
historian Raymond de Roover, the **Medici Company**, already in the
fifteenth century, managed their operations through a network of
subsidiaries with a partnership in Florence sitting at the top.[^23] The
Medici legal-organizational structure was also, in somewhat varying
forms, adopted by the Datini and other Italian merchant companies of the
period as well as in some other sectors.[^24]

The Medici company was a **merchant bank**: a company that besides
engaging in international trade also offered specialized financial
services. Merchant banks were key financial intermediaries in the Europe
of the Late Middle Ages and the early modern period. They were
predominantly owned and operated by northern Italians, at least until
the sixteenth century. Merchant companies were also key players in
providing financial services within the economy of Colonial America.

From the thirteenth century onwards, many merchant companies were
formally constituted through legal entities that had some resemblance,
and were the pre-cursor of, the modern general partnership.[^25] Most
importantly, the partners were subject to **joint and several
liability**. This means that any partner could be sued for the full debt
or obligation of the company (hence, "jointly"), even though each
partner is proportionally liable (hence, "severally"). Importantly, the
partners had unlimited personal liability for debts and obligations of
the merchant company. The high-risk nature of running a merchant
company, particularly a merchant bank, explains why family ties were
typically so central to their organization.

By contrast to the modern general partnership, the partnerships utilized
by the merchant companies were of a temporary nature. They would last,
typically, anywhere from two to twenty years, depending on the
circumstances. Shares in these partnerships were standardly also highly
illiquid. Partnership renewal was precisely often a moment to make some
alteration to the constellation of partners, though changes were usually
fairly minimal. The death of a partner would generally also mean the end
of a partnership. Profits were often retained in the partnership until
it was wound up.

The main model for the general partnership during the Middle Ages and
early modern period originated in Genoa and Tuscany. It was known as the
**compagnia**. The partners of these firms were known as *compagni*.

A major development in the history of merchant banking occurred in the
middle of the fourteenth century. Many of the large Italian merchant
bankers went bankrupt at this time, including the famous Florentine
super-companies: the Bardi, Peruzzi, and Acciaiuoli.[^26] The merchant
banks prominent in the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries were
generally unitary organizations. They did not have legally autonomous
subsidiaries. Branches in other cities and towns were instead just
divisions of the company, managed typically by partners and senior
employees.

We see the emergence of merchant banking companies with more
decentralized styles of legal ownership in the late fourteenth and in
the fifteenth century.[^27] It is commonly argued that this
decentralization was the result of reducing bankruptcy risks. The
transition may have also been a formalization of a principle that had
become common in Italy at the time: creditors to merchants and merchant
houses generally had priority to the local assets of a business in case
of bankruptcy. Other factors may have also played a role in establishing
more decentralized legal structures.[^28]

Most prominent among the companies to display the new decentralized
legal structure was the Medici company. In the fifteenth century, the
Medici company was run by a head office in Florence. In addition, the
company had silk and cloth manufacturing operations, a deposit bank in
Florence known as the Tavola, as well as various branches involved in
merchant banking operations elsewhere such as Avignon, Bruges, Geneva,
London, Milan, and Rome. Rather than manage these business units as
divisions of the managing company in Florence, they were actually set
up, at least until 1455, as legally separate partnerships with the main
company in Florence as the major partner in each.

All the subsidiaries managed by the main Medici company operated as
legally separate entities with their own style, capital, books, and
management. The partnership company based in Florence would hold the
controlling stake in these ventures, but typically relinquish day-to-day
management to a junior partner. These junior partners would typically
receive a share in the profits of the branch. The interaction between
the Medici subsidiaries resembled that of interaction with other
distinct businesses, though culturally the separation would have not
always been strictly maintained. Such was the case, for example, when
the Bruges branch of the company bailed out the London branch in a deal
that made little financial sense.[^29]

While in modern banking groups the top level company would typically be
a corporation, the Medici "holding company" structure certainly
resembled and was motivated by some of the same concerns as modern
arrangements. The recognition of the *accomandita* by Florentine law,
first in 1408, would have made this form of organization particularly
attractive. The *accomandita* legal form recognized the notion of a
"sleeping" partner, one that was not actively involved in managing the
business and only supplied investment capital. And such partners were
only liable for the amount of their investments. The *accomandita* was,
in other words, an early form of the modern limited partnership. It
would have allowed the main Medici holding company to enter into
subsidiaries as "sleeping" partners, limiting their liability and risks
with each venture.

After the middle of the fifteenth century, the main players in the
Medici company typically began to partner *as individuals* in new
ventures. In such a legal structure, there are just many different
companies that have overlapping ownership. But there is no legal entity
at the top of a network of subsidiaries, as in a holding company
structure. This legal structure after the fifteenth century also seems
to have been practiced by other merchant bankers at the time. The model
resembles that of the chain banking structures that developed in the
United States at the end of the nineteenth century.

## 3. Deposit banking

In *Section 1*, we introduced the notion of a deposit bank. We stated
that it is any institution, which accepts cash and credit deposits from
the public; facilitates payments with credit money; makes loans to
consumers and businesses; and affects the money supply, primarily
through lending.

Commercial banks are by far the most important, but not the only,
modern-day institution to practice **deposit banking** or **banking**.
In the United States, the two main other types of deposit banks are
**savings associations** (also known as savings banks, thrifts, or
savings and loans) and **credit unions**.

Savings associations are very similar to prototypical commercial banks,
but at least traditionally focus more actively on products and services
for savings, and not as much on checking and payment products.
Historically, they have also concentrating mostly on extending retail
mortgages, rather than business loans. Many other countries have
financial institutions that are similarly focused, at least
historically, on offering products and services for savings, such as
**building societies** in the United Kingdom, Australia, and New
Zealand; and **sparkassen** and **spaarbanken** in Germany and the
Netherlands, respectively.

Many savings associations in the United States are just standard
for-profit corporations owned by shareholders. Sometimes, however,
savings associations are for-profit corporations that are **mutually
owned**, meaning that shares can only be owned by those involved with
the savings association (e.g., as account holders, borrowers). Savings
associations can also have a hybrid model of ownership. While savings
associations do receive their own charters—either nationally by the
OCC or from a state agency—they are regulated in a similar manner to
commercial banks.[^30]

Next, **credit unions** offer similar services to commercial banks and
savings associations, but are formed as non-profit corporations. That
is, profits are not distributed to shareholders that own the bank, but
instead are utilized to improve or decrease the costs of the products
and services offered. Typically these institutions are mutually owned by
account holders. Rather than owning shares, however, account holders are
members of the non-profit corporation with voting and other governance
rights. These rights are typically independent of the financial stakes
involved. Credit unions are normally focused on retail and small
business clients.

While savings associations are regulated in a similar manner to
commercial banks on the national level, credit unions are actually
regulated and chartered by the **National Credit Union Administration**
(**NCUA**). At the state level, credit unions are sometimes chartered
and regulated by the same agency as banks and savings associations.
Sometimes, however, they also have their own regulatory agency. Credit
unions cannot be FDIC-insured. Instead, the NCUA manages a special
insurance fund for credit unions called the **National Credit Union
Share Insurance Fund**.

As with savings associations, we can find institutions that are similar
to the American credit union in other countries. The building societies
of the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand that were mentioned
earlier are actually very much like credit unions as they are also
non-profit organizations. In Ireland, the same types of institutions are
standardly called mutual banks. So-called kredietbanken in the
Netherlands tend to be non-profit institutions, mutually owned by
entrepreneurs. They extend bank services and loans to other
entrepreneurs.

Many common distinctions further clarify the nature of commercial banks,
savings associations, and credit unions. With a **community** **bank**,
for example, we typically mean a commercial bank that has a local focus,
and we similarly often speak of a **community credit union** or a
**community savings association**. For another example, any commercial
bank that only services other commercial banks and savings associations
as well as their officers, directors, and employees is commonly known as
a **banker's bank**. A **corporate credit union** (also a central credit
union) is, in turn, one that only similarly services other credit
unions.

Besides commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions, there
are some more exotic entities in the United States that meet, or nearly
meet, the characteristics of deposit banks.

An **industrial loan company** (**ILC**), for example, can offer really
all the same services as a commercial bank, including checking and
savings accounts, though they cannot guarantee that deposits will be
made available within seven days (even if in practice funds are often
available sooner). They can only be chartered by certain states—and
not at the federal level—and are subject to different regulatory
regimes than commercial banks and savings associations. For example,
ILCs can also be subsidiaries of nonfinancial companies. In this way,
for example, both Toyota and BMW operate banks in the United States
called the Toyota Financial Savings Bank and the BMW Bank of North
America.[^31]

In sum, the main institutions in the United States that qualify as
deposit banks are commercial banks, savings associations, and credit
unions. However, there are various other, more exotic types of
institutions that meet, or nearly meet, the standard characteristics of
a deposit bank. Importantly, while we loosely refer to these
institutions as deposit banks, banks, or banks and credit unions,[^32]
various of those institutions, like savings associations and ILCs, are,
in a legal sense, technically not always defined as "banks".

Many institutions in the United States might carry the label "bank" in
their name, but are not really deposit banks as just described. In fact,
they sometimes are quite distant cousins in terms of their activities.

Consider, for instance, **investment banks**. These are institutions
which have among their core activities helping companies and governments
with raising capital (through stock and bond issuances); providing
advice on mergers, acquisitions, and corporate restructurings; and
helping manage the investment portfolios of clients such as pension
funds, hedge funds, family offices, and wealthy individuals. Typically,
institutions that travel by this name would not provide checking and
savings accounts or payment products. They also do not impact the money
supply in the way of banks and credit unions. So, investment banks are
not really "banks" in our sense above, even though they may be part of a
larger brand that also includes one or more entities occupied with
commercial banking.

In some cases, making sense of the label "bank" will be a fairly
complicated exercise. A common distinction that can bring such
complications is that of a **private bank**.

Sometimes an entity called a private bank is just as commercial bank
that is privately owned (i.e., the stock is not traded on a public
exchange). Often, however, the term also refers to for-profit companies
that are specialized in offering wealth management services to high
net-worth individuals and families, a service known as **private
banking**.

The possible services that are provided by private banks in the latter
sense can vary widely and may include checking and savings account
services, advice on investments and taxes, estate planning, custody
services, high-value mortgages, and so on. In some cases, such private
banks are just specialized commercial banks. But in other cases, these
"banks" are really somewhat different types of institutions.

Many other countries and regions will have deposit banks that show
similarities to those in the United States, particularly in the
developed economies. We have already shown that most countries have
their own version of commercial banks, and that many countries have
institutions similar to savings associations and credit unions. It
should be re-iterated, however, that all countries and regions will have
their own idiosyncrasies and features. And in some cases, the landscape
can look substantially different.

At least one notable difference with the United States and many other
countries is the role of public banks. Here we do not mean banks that
are publicly held. Instead, in our sense here, a **public bank** is one
where the government owns the majority of the shares. The United States
certainly has such banks, such as the Bank of North Dakota and various
banks owned by native American tribal governments. But they are rare. By
contrast, in European countries, public banks are much more common. The
sparkassen in Germany and the cantonal banks in Switzerland, for
example, play a substantial role in their banking sectors. Even more
extreme is the banking landscape in China. The four large banks that
dominate its financial sector—the Bank of China, the China
Construction Bank, the Agricultural Bank of China, and the Industrial
and Commercial Bank of China—are predominantly state-owned.
