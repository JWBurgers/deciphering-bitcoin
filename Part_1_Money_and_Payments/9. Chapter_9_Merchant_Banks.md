# Chapter 9: Merchant Banks

From the High Middle Ages until into the early modern period, the story
of money, payments, and banking largely revolves around two groups of
financial institutions: merchant banks and deposit banks.

The merchant banks were, at least by the standards of the time, large
international companies. They were owned and operated predominantly by
powerful families from northern Italian cities such as Genoa, Milan,
Venice, and Florence. They processed long-distance payments and extended
commercial and sovereign credit. Deposit banks were, instead, usually
small, locally owned operations, often proprietorships. They had a key
role in facilitating local payments and credit. Unlike the merchant
banks, deposit banks were generally heavily regulated. While merchant
banks indeed sometimes also owned deposit banks or ran such operations,
deposit banking was not really their core business.

In this chapter, we turn first to the merchant banks. We begin in
*Section 1* by describing two types of contracts around trade
expeditions, the *commenda* and the maritime loan. We, then, turn to the
development of merchant companies and merchant banks in the thirteenth
century in *Section 2*. The main business of the merchant bankers is
explained in *Section 3*. In *Section 4*, we briefly offer some
reflections on the Hanseatic League.

# 1. Trade expedition contracts

In the High Middle Ages, various contracts were utilized to reduce the
transportation and commercial risks present in trade expeditions. Rare
twelfth-century notarial records from Genoa clearly suggest that the two
main contracts for reducing such risks at the time were the *commenda*
and the maritime loan.[^1]

The **commenda** was a type of partnership contract utilized for short
commercial ventures, particularly trade expeditions. We have a notarial
act for such a contract from as early as the eleventh century.[^2]
Various earlier contract forms from the Mediterranean region—going
back all the way to the Roman *societas* of labor and capital—probably
influenced the *commenda*'s development.[^3]

The *commenda* came in two basic forms: the unilateral form and the
bilateral form. In law and practice, they were generally treated as two
forms of the same general contract, rather than as separate types of
contracts.[^4] Pryor describes the two standard versions of the
*commenda* as follows:[^5]

- Both forms of the *commenda* had a sedentary investor, known as the
  *commendator* or *stans*, and an active merchant, known as the
  *tractator*.

- In a unilateral *commenda*, the *tractor* did not contribute capital
  to the venture. In a bilateral commenda, the *tractator* contributed
  one-third of the capital and the *commendator* two-thirds of it.

- The *tractator* put the capital to use in a trade expedition with
  varying degrees of support from the *commendator*.

- After the completion of an expedition or the expiration of a certain
  time period, the *tractator* and the *commendator* would divide the
  proceeds from the venture.

- With a unilateral *commenda*, after accounting for costs, the
  *tractator* would receive one-fourth of the profits, while the
  *commendator* would receive three-fourths of it. In case of losses,
  the *commendator* just received the proceeds from the venture. The
  latter, in other words, carried the entire capital loss. There was no
  separate compensation for the labor of the *tractator* in either case.

- In a bilateral *commenda*, after accounting for costs, the *tractator*
  and *commendator* would each receive half of the profits. In case of a
  loss, the remaining proceeds would be allocated according to the
  original two-thirds and one-third ratios of the respective capital
  investments. There was no separate compensation for the labor of the
  *tractator* in either case.

Above describes the two standard forms of the *commenda* in cities such
as Genoa, Venice, Amalfi, Barcelona, and Marseilles. Obviously, any
specific contract could deviate from its standard form, and in some
localities different standards were also maintained. The two forms of
the contract also travelled by various names. In Genoa, the term
"commenda" was reserved strictly for the unilateral form, while the
bilateral form was normally called the "societas maris". In Venice, both
forms of the contract were usually called "collegantia". In Pisa, the
unilateral *commenda* contract was normally called the "compagnia",
while the bilateral form was usually called the "societas".

Sometimes a merchant might enter into a *commenda* contract with only
one *commendator* for an expedition.[^6] Often, however, merchants
entered into multiple *commenda* for an expedition. Sometimes a merchant
traded on his own account next to the honoring of these contracts. Many
remaining records of *commenda* specifically allow for that privilege.
In the twelfth century, the capital contributed in these contracts was
often in the form of goods rather than money. In the thirteenth century,
however, the *commenda* increasingly became an instrument for raising
financial capital.

While a *commendator* was sometimes a retired merchant and an actively
involved investor, the *commendator* was also frequently a passive
investor. Passive investors already had a role in *commenda* contracts
at the end of the twelfth century.[^7] But they would have become
increasingly common as markets developed for the *commenda*,
increasingly turning it into an instrument for raising financial
capital. Highly developed markets for *commenda* contracts existed in
various Mediterranean cities by the end of the thirteenth century,
notably in Venice. And "\[t\]he investors were often non-merchants, and
they came from all walks of life—landed aristocrats, clergy, artisans,
housewives, widows and orphans, and foreign investors".[^8]

Another key contract to reduce the risks of expeditions for merchants in
the High Middle Ages was that of the **maritime loan**.[^9] While not
purely an insurance contract, it certainly had an important insurance
function. Maritime loans are usually described as loans extended to
merchants—and sometimes other involved parties such as ship owners or
captains—that were only repaid upon the safe arrival of the goods or
vessel at the destination port and, sometimes, also on the condition of
the safe return of the vessel. If the journey was negatively affected
due to general perils of navigation (e.g., storms, pirates), the loan
did not have to be repaid or only partially repaid under certain
circumstances.

Due to the risk element, the interest rates for these loans could be
freely determined, unlike for standard loans which were subject to usury
laws. The rates were high. De Roover, for example, notes that rates of
forty to fifty percent were not uncommon for expeditions from Italy or
Constantinople to Alexandria or Syria.[^10] While the lender covered
transportation risks, the commercial risks of an expedition still
technically fell on the borrower. The separation was imperfect in
practice, however, as adverse commercial conditions could also cause a
borrower to default on the maritime loan.

Maritime loans were already popular in the Greco-Roman period. The
concept of wrapping an insurance function into a loan potentially even
far precedes that period. The **Code of Hammurabi** indicates that the
ancient Babylonians, around 2,000 years before Christ,[^11] legally
mandated leniency on repayment of debt contracts in certain
circumstances in order to facilitate commerce. According to law 48 of
the Code, for example, "If a man has incurred a debt and a storm has
flooded his field or carried away the crop, or the corn has not grown
because of drought, in that year he shall not pay his creditor. Further
he shall post-date his bond and shall not pay interest for that
year".[^12] Similarly, law 102 states that, if an agent has accepted
money from a merchant for a trade expedition "and an enemy has forced
him to give up some of the goods he was carrying, then the agent shall
specify the amount on oath and shall be acquitted."[^13] These legal
protections were surely considered in determining the interest rates for
these loans.

Charles Trenerry has actually argued that the Code illustrates that the
Babylonians were already working with a precursor to the maritime loan
for land expeditions. After a review of the available evidence, Trenerry
concludes as follows:

> It is shown that in all essentials the idea of the contract of loans
> on Bottomry was present, *mutatis mutandis*, in the laws governing the
> loans for trading purposes made by the Babylonians from 4000-5000
> years ago, as shown in the *Code of Hammurabi*, dating from *circa*
> 2250 B.C.[^14]

In 1236, Pope Gregory IX condemned the sea loan as usurious.
Subsequently, merchants increasingly shifted to other types of
arrangements for managing transportation risks. One other type of
contract which already existed at the time of the decree, the *cambium
maritimum* (or *cambium* *nauticum*), became increasingly popular.
Essentially, this was just an exchange contract, where the merchant
acted as the taker, and repayment by the merchant or their correspondent
in another location with a different currency was conditional on the
arrival of the vessel or cargo. As with any other exchange contract, the
interest rate was incorporated into the exchange rates.

A big milestone in risk management occurred with the advent of modern
marine insurance contracts in the fourteenth century.[^15] With these
contracts, a merchant or someone else involved in trade would be
promised a payment in case of the materialization of certain
transportation risks against an **insurance premium** paid in advance.
So, unlike the maritime loan and the *cambium maritimum*, these marine
insurance contracts fully separated casualty risks from credit risks, in
a form that is broadly consistent with modern insurance contracts. These
contracts were generally organized by brokers and typically had multiple
underwriters.

Marine insurance contracts developed in northern Italy during the
fourteenth century. The earliest known examples are from Genoa, dated
1343 and 1347.[^16] From northern Italy, the popularity of marine
insurance contracts spread to the rest of the Mediterranean and Western
Europe during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. It continued to be
used mostly by Italians, however, well-into the early modern period. In
the early seventeenth century, marine insurance was still used for no
more than 1% of Dutch shipping and 10% of merchandise.[^17]

# 2. Merchant companies and merchant banks

Despite the short-term contracts that surrounded trade expeditions, most
merchants were operating in an environment of some stability in the High
Middle Ages. Many, of course, participated in guilds and other such
associations that were more enduring. Merchants would have often also
repeatedly entered into trade expeditions with certain other merchants
that were connected via the extended family or from the same city. Most
importantly, however, just as artisan and agricultural operations,
merchant operations were typically structured around family households,
which formed a stable base of operations.[^18]

In some cases a merchant might indeed be "independent" and casually
supported by other members of the household (e.g., his wife, his teenage
son). In other cases, however, one household might include two or more
merchants (e.g., two brothers or a father and son). A very large
merchant household in the thirteenth century might have even included
half a dozen merchants or more, normally bound by nuclear or extended
family ties, living in a compound or palace. Two or more households
might have also combined to form a stable base of operations; standardly
any such alliances would also have been family-based.

These stable units of merchant operations based around households and
family ties are usually referred to as **merchant houses**.
Traditionally, merchant houses were not really organized formally as a
legal business entity. Starting in the thirteenth century, however,
particularly the Italian merchant houses were increasingly formally
organized as **merchant companies**.[^19] In Venice that organization
was in the form of the *fraterna*. In Genoa and Tuscany that legal
organization was through the *compagnia (*here not to be confused with
the Pisan name for the unilateral *commenda* mentioned above). Whereas
the *fraterna* was a default legal form in Venice, the *compagnia* was
an explicit contractual relationship. As mentioned in *Chapter 1*, the
*compagnia* was an early form of the general partnership, one that
became increasingly replaced by the *accomandita*, a form of limited
partnership, in the fifteenth century.

As the thirteenth century progressed, it was particularly the Italian
merchant companies that were the face of the commercial revolution.
Increasingly, they began operating with permanent branches of their
outfits in important cities for trade and finance, such as Avignon,
Paris, Barcelona, and Bruges.[^20] Branches and outposts of trading
companies outside of Italy were largely populated with Italians, often
sent over by the company's headquarters. Sometimes they were led by one
of the senior partners of the company or a senior representative known
as a **factor** (*fattori*). The Medici and other companies also had
branches that were led by junior partners.

In cities and towns without a branch, a merchant company could leverage
agents on the basis of commission. The branches of the larger Italian
merchant companies often acted as such agents in places like Bruges,
Paris, Barcelona, and London. The advent of the Italian-led commercial
revolution in the thirteenth century did not eliminate the caravan
merchant, or at least the nomadic merchant, entirely from the commercial
landscape of the Late Middle Ages, not even in Italy. Many merchants,
particularly those of lesser importance, would have continued to lead
fairly nomadic lifestyles.[^21] That said, even independent merchants
utilized commission-based agents to conduct their business in a
sedentary fashion.[^22]

So, the commercial revolution was very much an Italian affair. Their
leadership in commerce was supported by advancements in partnership
contracts, accounting, bills of exchange, and other aspects of business
administration in the Late Middle Ages. Their success crowded out
merchants elsewhere, particularly in Western Europe. Many of the towns
and cities that had some commercial and financial prominence during the
Late Middle Ages and early modern period such as Avignon, Paris,
Barcelona, Bruges, and Antwerp became populated with colonies of
predominantly Italian merchants.

During fourteenth and fifteenth century Bruges, for instance, the native
upper class "was not made up of merchants, save for a few exceptions,
but of brokers, inn-keepers, *drapiers*, and commission agents".[^23]
Native merchants were largely absent from the upper class because
international trade through the city was facilitated by companies from
Venice, Genoa, Florence, Lucca, Sienna, and elsewhere in Italy. This was
quite different than the picture at the end of the thirteenth century
when the Flemish merchants still congregated in caravans to sell their
wares at the Fairs of Champagne. For another example, Lyon in 1557
apparently had 183 foreign merchants permanently residing there. Over
eighty percent of them were Italians, over a quarter of the Italian
merchants coming from Florence.[^24]

It is commonly noted that during the commercial revolution partnership
contracts became of increasingly longer duration.[^25] The main
mechanisms behind that observation were probably twofold.

First, certain merchant companies would grown in size and capital. With
increasing representation abroad through partners, factors, or agents,
they increasingly would have financed their own trade. Second, long-term
partnerships formed outside the scope of a single merchant house became
increasingly common. Certain merchant houses fused for all or some of
their activities. Members of a merchant house increasingly also formed
longer-term secondary partnerships outside of it for certain
purposes.[^26] The family would have still remained important, but less
so as we progress from the thirteenth century.

These two trends would have come at the expense of venture contracts
that involved equity capital, notably the *commenda*. The latter would
have also been impacted by other trends, such as the development of
marine insurance.

Many of the merchant companies that marked the European landscape from
the thirteenth century onwards, particularly the larger ones, were not
just engaged in trade, but also in financial services. Those merchant
companies are known as **merchant banks**. Merchant banking first seems
to have appeared around the exchange business that was needed to
facilitate trade at the Fairs of Champagne in the thirteenth century.
The business was controlled mostly by the Siennese throughout the
century, before they gave way to the Florentines who dominated the
fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.[^27]

What exactly warrants the label of merchant "bank"?

Merchants then, as now, would have frequently sold goods on credit to
their clients as well as bought goods on credit from their suppliers.
That extension of commercial credit hardly warrants the label of
"bank".[^28] Instead, the merchant banks were "banks" because they
extended credit beyond their own clients, processed international
payments, and engaged in other types of financial activities. While many
merchants in this period might have occasionally dabbled in financial
services, for the merchant bankers that was truly a line of business in
its own right. Trade remained important to merchant banks. Certainly for
the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, we have no examples of merchant
banks that transformed purely into financial service providers.[^29]

Just as most other merchant companies, the merchant banks were typically
organized around certain powerful families from the north of Italy. Some
of the most well-known companies from the Late Middle Ages and early
modern period include the Bardi, Peruzzi, Acciaiuoli, Alberti, and
Medici from Florence; the Grimaldi and Spinola from Genoa; and the
Borromei from Milan. It was particularly the Florentine firms that
dominated the merchant banking landscape. Merchant banking was not
exclusively Italian, of course. Notably, in the sixteenth century, south
Germany saw the presence of several powerful merchant banking houses
like the Fuggers and the Welsers.

The merchant banks of the Late Middle Ages and early modern period were
nowhere near the size of modern international banks. According to De
Roover,[^30] in the early fourteenth century, the Acciaiuoli, one of the
main merchant banking companies at the time, only employed 41 factors
excluding those in the home office of Florence. The rest of the company
would have consisted of some partners and lower-level employees such as
messengers and apprentices. Kohn mentions that the Peruzzi at their peak
in the fourteenth century had around 15 branches from Western Europe to
the Levant, employing around 120 partners and factors.[^31] The Medici
at their peak in the fifteenth century seem to have employed around 40
to 50 factors, and 10 or 11 branch managers.[^32]

While small operations according to modern standards, these famous
merchant banks certainly would have been impressive in the Late Middle
Ages and early modern period. They would have far exceeded in size and
complexity other types of business operations. Of course, many less
famous merchant banks would have been substantially smaller in size.

# 3. The merchant banking business

The main financial business of the merchant bankers consisted of
commercial lending, sovereign lending, the processing of long distance
payments, and arbitrage and speculation on currency exchange rates. The
key financial instrument to all these activities—other than sovereign
lending—was the exchange contract, first in the form of the *cambium*
contract but mostly in the form of the bill of exchange. Essentially,
the business of the banker was the exchange business. Illustratively,
the Italian expression to "run a bank" (*fare il banco*) was at the time
synonymous to the expression "to deal in exchange" (*fare il
cambio*),[^33] while in Elizabethan England, the term "banker" was
synymous with the term "exchanger".[^34]

To obtain an impression of the exchange business, lets first explore how
exchange contracts were utilized to extend commercial loans. We utilize
for that purpose an example from the Datini archives.[^35]

In the name of God, amen.

December 12, 1399

> Pay at usance by this first of exchange to Domenico Sancio six hundred
> écus at 10s. 5d. \[barcelonese\] per ▽, which ▽600 at 10s. 5d. per écu
> are for the value received \[here\] from Jacopo Gosco, and charge them
> to our account. God be with you.
>
> Giovanni Orlandini and Piero Benizi & Co.
>
> In Bruges

On the backside of the note was written the name of the firm "Francesco
da Prato & Co. in Barcelona". This would have referred to a company of
the famous merchant banker, Francesco di Marco Datini (1335–1410). The
downward triangle on the note is an editorial convention to replace a
mark denoting the écu.

The bill was written on December 12, 1399. It specifies that, in Bruges,
Jacopo Gosco (the deliverer) paid a sum of money to the company of
Orlandini and Benizi (the taker). The amount was 600 écus. The écu here
did not refer to the famous French gold coin, but was purely a unit of
account that was equivalent to 22 Flemish silver pence. Many places in
southern and western Europe, including in Barcelona and Bruges, used the
system of account mentioned earlier, where a pound was equivalent to 20
shillings, and each shilling was equivalent to 12 pence. So the sum of
money delivered, 600 écus, was equal to £55 of Flemish pence.

The company of Orlandini and Benizi orders the Datini company in
Barcelona to make a payment to Domenico Sancio. The exchange rate is
listed as 10s 5d Barcelonese per écu, or 125 Barcelonese per écu. The
600 écus would, thus, have been worth 75,000 Barcelonese, or £312 10s
Barcelonese. The bill was accepted on January 11, 1400 and the payment
was made on February 11, 1400, in line with the usance of 60 days
between Bruges and Barcelona.

Note that the firm of Orlandini and Benizi requests that payment of 600
écus to Domenico Sancio be made by "charging them to our account". The
Datini company in Barcelona would have likely also credited the amount
of the bill to Domenico Sancio's account. As already mentioned in
*Section 2*, physical money often did not change hands with the two
payments required for an exchange contract. As stated by De Roover,[^36]

> It would be a mistake to believe that bills were usually paid in
> specie. The records of the Datini branch in Barcelona disclose that
> such was not the case and that bills of exchange were often paid by
> assignment in bank or by book transfer of one kind or another.

What was the commercial context to the bill of exchange above?

The firm of Orlandini and Benizi was a merchant company. It was in the
business of buying Flemish wool and selling it in places like Barcelona.
Likely, they used the 600 écus to purchase wool, which they then
transported to Barcelona. It may, then, have been sold by the Datini
company or perhaps another commercial agent. The funds from the sale,
after any commissions, would have, then, been deposited with the account
of Orlandini and Benizi's company with the Datini Company. That deposit
would have covered the upcoming payment due to Domenico Sancio from the
bill of exchange. In case the timing of the deposit was too late, the
Datini company would have likely extended credit to Orlandini and
Benizi's company.

Jacopo Gosco, the deliverer of the 600 écus, was a Venetian merchant
banker. Essentially, he lent funds to the company of Orlandini and
Benizi to buy inventory. The repayment of the loan occurs two months
later in Barcelona. However, the repayment has been made to Domenico
Sancio, another merchant banker and a correspondent of Jacopo Gosco. How
does Gosco now obtain his profits from the loan?

One common practice would have been for Sancio to buy a second bill of
exchange and that is exactly what happened. A translation of that bill
is seen below.[^37]

In the name of God, amen 12^th^ of February 1400.

> Pay at usance by this first letter of exchange to Jacopo Goscio \[the
> sum of\] 625 écus at 10s 0d Barcelonese per écu, which 625 écus at 10s
> 0d per écu are for the value received here from Domenico Sancio; and
> charge this amount to our account.
>
> God be with you.
>
> Francesco del Tovaglia and Co. in Barcelona

On the backside of the note was written "Giuliano Zaccheria and Co. in
Bruges".

Sancio used the services of a merchant bank in Barcelona, the Tovaglia
company, to send Gosco's £312 10s Barcelonese pence, converted into
Flemish money, back to Bruges. Given that the exchange rate in Barcelona
was 10s 0d Barcelonese per écu, or 120 Barcelonese per écu, the total
paid out in Bruges was 625 écus. That payment was honored by the
Zaccheria company, a merchant bank that was a correspondent of the
Tovaglia company. The bill was accepted by the Zaccheria company on
March 11, 1400 and paid out on April 11, 1400.

In about four months, Gosco made a return of 25 écus on his original
loan of 600 écus to Orlandini and Benizi's company. Considered annually,
the interest rate on the loan is 12.5 percent. The interest on the loan
stems from the differences in the two exchange rates between Barcelonese
and écus. In the original bill, the exchange rate was 10s 5d Barcelonese
per écu; in the return bill, the exchange rate was 10s 0d Barcelonese
per écu. In other words, Gosco received more Barcelonese per écu from
the first bill, then he was required to pay for reverting back into écus
in the second bill. The presence of an interest rate on Gosco's loan
was, thus, the product of Barcelonese being relatively less valuable in
Bruges than in Barcelona.

None of this was a happy accident. In order for bankers like Gosco to
make money by short-term loans, there had to be a financial incentive
through interest. And that interest was captured through exchange rate
spreads, precisely by currencies being relatively more valued in their
home base. That is, a healthy lending market required that francs were
relatively more desirable in Paris, groats relatively more desirable in
Bruges, ducats relatively more desirable in Venice, and so on. The
larger the favorable exchange spread between two places, the larger the
interest rate on exchange contracts. Evidence on medieval interest rates
suggests that this supposition about market equilibrium was indeed
correct.[^38]

Exchange rates, of course, could be negatively affected due to currency
devaluations, wars, and other types of events that shocked the market.
So the merchant bankers did not always make a profit on the extension of
loans through exchange contracts. But overall, the profits of that
activity outweighed the losses. Note that it was precisely these risks
to exchange rates that largely placed exchange dealings outside of the
scope of usury laws.

The example above involving Jacopo Gosco as the lender seems to have
been a fairly common way to extend commercial credit using bills of
exchange.[^39] The exact mechanics for extending commercial credit,
however, could vary substantially. Kohn,[^40] for example, suggests that
Lucchese silk merchants in the thirteenth century utilized commercial
credit via the following mechanism:

- They would purchase silk from Genoa by selling exchange contracts to
  Genoese merchant bankers. These contracts would be due at a fair in
  Champagne.

- Once they sold the silk in Lucca, the merchants would, in turn, buy
  exchange contracts due at the same fair in Champagne with the
  proceeds. They could even buy these contracts from the same merchant
  bankers as to which they sold contracts earlier in Genoa.

- At the next fair in Champagne, the agents of the Lucchese merchants
  would, then, use the proceeds from the bills they had purchased in
  Lucca to settle the bills they sold in Genoa. As mentioned in *Section
  3*, these contracts were probably settled during the clearing and
  settlement phase of the fair.

The types of arrangements for utilizing commercial credit as described
above worked well for merchant companies that had correspondents abroad.
But without such correspondents, it was difficult to sell a bill of
exchange and loan money. The purpose of **dry exchange** (*cambio
secco*) was precisely to make credit available to such merchants. A dry
exchange was just the extension of a local loan disguised through two
exchange transactions.[^41] It was called "dry exchange" because no
trade was palpably involved.[^42]

To understand dry exchange, lets consider an easy to follow hypothetical
example. Suppose that Paolo, a merchant, needs 100 florins in Florence,
and that the Medici bank decides to help him through a dry exchange with
its subsidiary in Venice. The exchange rates are 20 florins per ducat in
Florence and 21 florins per ducat in Venice, as required for a healthy
lending market. One common method for dry exchange known as "exchange
and rechange" (*cambium et recambium*) would have roughly proceeded as
follows:

- Paolo accepts 100 florins and sells a bill of exchange in which the
  Medici bank in Venice is both the payer and the payee. The payment due
  in Venice is for 5 ducats given the exchange rate of 20 florins per
  Ducat in Florence.

- The Medici bank in Venice honors the bill at its due date by crediting
  the Medici bank in Florence with 5 ducats on its permanent account. It
  temporarily opens an account for Paolo and debits the account for 5
  ducats.

- Next, the Medici bank in Venice writes a second bill, where Paolo is
  the payer and the Medici bank in Florence the payee. The amount of the
  bill is for 5 ducats worth of florins, or 105 florins given the
  exchange rate of 21 florins per ducat. The Medici Bank in Venice
  credits 5 ducats to Paolo's account and closes it. It also debits 5
  ducats to the running account of the Medici bank in Florence.

- At the due date, Paolo pays the sum of 105 florins to the Medici Bank
  in Florence.

At the end of this two-way exchange, the Medici bank in Venice is in the
same position as before. Paolo receives his loan of 100 florins, and the
Medici bank in Florence receives 5% interest over the loan (which
according to usance would have been repaid after 20 days). No actual
commerce has visibly taken place. There were various other methods for
dry exchange than that illustrated above.[^43] In a "fictitious
exchange" (*cambio fittizio*), the first bill was not even sent to the
other city, and the deliverer relied on the exchange rate information
relayed by correspondents to draw up a second bill.

In many cases of dry exchange, such as in the example above, exchange
rate risks were still present. Some cases exist, however, in which the
rate for the second bill of exchange was agreed upon beforehand. Such
dry exchanges would have eliminated any exchange rate risks
entirely.[^44] While many dry exchanges did not differ in substance with
regards to exchange rate risks from regular multi-exchange transactions
involving commerce, dry exchange was generally frowned upon much more
with respect to usury laws.[^45] The absence of any palpable commercial
transaction at the second location would have surely increased
suspicions. Recent evidence presented by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli on
account ledgers from the Borromei of Milan suggests that dry exchange
was, probably as to be expected given these considerations,
uncommon.[^46]

Exchange contracts originated as instruments for making international
payments and certainly continued to be used that way throughout the Late
Middle Ages and early modern period. In those situations, the merchant
banker would act as a taker, rather than a deliverer. While merchant
bankers sometimes also charged fees for processing bills of exchange,
this seems to have been a small part of their overall business model.
So, how would a merchant banker make money on processing an
international payment, particularly given our discussion on exchange
rate spreads between different towns and cities above?

Consider again the initial exchange contract involving Jacopo Gosco, the
Venetian merchant banker, and the Orlandini and Benizi company in
Bruges. On December 12, 1399, Gosco lent a sum of money of 600 écus.
While Gosco's exchange rate for any return bill was still uncertain, the
first bill utilized an exchange rate of 10s 5d Barcelonese per écu.
Suppose now that earlier in the day Gosco was requested to play
precisely the opposite role, namely that of a taker for a bill on
Barcelona of precisely 600 écus. The deliverer could have been another
merchant, an aristocrat, a church official, and so on who needed to make
an international payment. Suppose that for this transaction, Gosco also
used his correspondent, Domenico Sancio, but as a payer.

If Gosco played his cards right, he would have arranged a rate of
Barcelonese per écu that was lower on the earlier bill. It's easy to see
why. Suppose that, indeed, Gosco had played his cards right and had
charged 10d 3s per écu. As a result of this bill for an international
payment, Gosco would have owned 600 écus in Bruges. In two months time,
he would also have had a debt to Sancio of £307 10s Barcelonese when the
bill was due in Barcelona. While the 600 écus would have been lent to
Orlandini and Benizi's firm later in the day, that transaction would
have also resulted in a credit of £312 10s with Sancio two months later.
As both bills would have been due on the same day two months later,
Gosco would have ended up with a positive balance of £5 Barcelonese on
his account with Sancio in Barcelona.

Once both exchange contracts were completed, Gosco might have asked
Sancio to remit the £5 Barcelonese profit back to him at the rate of 10s
0d per écu, as we know from the actual record was the exchange rate at
that time. This bill would have yielded him a total of 10 écus profit in
Bruges two months later. While Gosco now seemingly has less profit than
in the original exposition of the actual exchange, where the profit was
25 écus, it adds some realism to the situation. In order to lend out 600
écus in the first place, Gosco would have needed to obtain them from
somewhere. At least one common method was to do what any good banker
does: borrow at a low rate and lend out at a high rate.

There are, of course, various other ways in which Gosco could have had
600 écus at his disposal to lend that led to the actual exchange in the
records. Capital for lending was partially supplied by the partners
through the initial required investment, or the *corpo*. That was,
however, generally only a fraction of the available capital.

A normally much larger amount was known as the *sopracorpo* and
consisted of three sources of funds: (1) time deposits from the partners
that were renumerated at a fixed interest rate; (2) time deposits from
outsiders, including other merchants, aristocrats, and clergymen, but
also small investors, typically also Italian;[^47] and retained
earnings, which were generally not withdrawn until the end of the
partnership. Time deposits generally circumvented usury laws by making
the interest payment voluntary in the form of a gift, though such
payments were surely essential to maintaining credibility.[^48] The
*sopracorpo* as well as borrowing at favorable rates through processing
international payments would have constituted the most significant
sources of capital for lending.[^49]

The role played by the merchant bankers in any particular bill of
exchange market, such as in Bruges or Barcelona, was that of a **market
maker**. They ensured that any particular market for exchange contracts
had liquidity, by being ready to both buy bills and sell them.
Obviously, however, the merchant bankers were only willing to play
market makers because they could maintain a favorable exchange rate
spread. More specifically, the merchant bankers would buy bills, or lend
money, at an exchange rate that valued the countercurrency as relatively
less than when they would sell bills of exchange, or borrow money.

To sum it up. Bankers also profited from fees. But it is the spread in
exchange rates that seems to have driven the market for exchange
contracts. The merchant bankers maintained a spread in exchange rates
locally, where the countercurrency was valued relatively less for
lending than for borrowing activities. Additionally, across cities and
towns, equilibrium required for any currency to be relatively more
valuable in its place of use. While the spread in exchange rates between
cities and towns could temporarily be disrupted by devaluations, wars,
and so on, the need for international payments and credit would have
eventually readjusted market conditions to a favorable spread.

Markets where securities are bought and sold that have a maturity of
less than one year are commonly called **money markets**. Into the early
modern period, bills of exchange could only be bought and sold at their
initial issue, and they were not tradeable. That is in sharp contrast
to, say, modern stocks or bonds which are tradeable on exchanges. The
exchangeability of bills of exchange only really developed in early
seventeenth century Antwerp,[^50] before it spread as a general feature
of bills of exchange throughout Europe. That said, we can still call all
the cities, towns, and fairs where markets developed for exchange
contracts as "money markets".

As already mentioned, highly developed money markets for exchange
contracts really only existed in a select number of towns and fairs into
the early modern period. The towns and cities were known as banking
places.[^51] They included, to varying degrees at varying times, places
like Florence, Venice, Rome, Naples, Bruges, Barcelona, Antwerp, and
London. Prominent money markets were also seen at international fairs
such as those at Champagne, Geneva, Lyon, and Besançon-Piacenza. Given
the concentration of merchant bankers in these locations, the
competition and pressure on exchange rate spreads in them was relatively
high. Each market was typically facilitated by brokers that served as
intermediaries between buyers and sellers for exchange contracts on
commission. The competitive pressures would have often made indirect
payment routes more efficient than direct transactions.[^52]

Outside of a major money market, transactions involving exchange
contracts would have been impossible, or in any case more difficult and
costly. It presented a major obstacle, for example, to the Papacy in
collecting payments throughout Europe. In the words of De Roover,[^53]

> In outposts, such as Scandinavia or Poland, or even relatively
> backward countries, such as Germany where there were no banking
> facilities, papal transfers suffered long delays and were attended
> with great risks and difficulties. Often papal agents would have to
> rely on casual menas such as traveling merchants, pilgrims, or
> students who would undertake to carry money or goods to the nearest
> banking center. Transfers from Poland sometimes took six months or
> more, whereas funds received in Bruges or London were made available
> in Rome within a month or less by a simple letter of advice. The
> bankers provided expeditious and efficient service. The smooth
> operation of this system depended, of course, on the existence of a
> regular money market, and this was found only in a few trading centers
> where the volume of business was large enough to make it worth while
> for the Italian banking houses to maintain branch offices.

Besides their role in payment and credit operations, exchange contracts
were also used in arbitrage and speculative activities. A merchant in
Bruges might, for instance, move funds to Barcelona purely in
anticipation of a favorable shock to exchange rates there, or for use in
relatively high-interest time deposits. The mechanics and business
incentives behind all the different payment, credit, speculative, and
arbitrage activities could fill an entire book. As noted by Bolton and
Guidi-Bruscoli,[^54] "as much paper and ink has probably been expended
on discussing the functions of bills of exchange as was used in the
writing of the hundreds of thousands of these brief documents in the
later middle ages". Our purpose here has been merely to offer an
impression of how the instrument was used for payments and credit
operations.

The last activity of the merchant bankers that deserves attention here
is sovereign lending. The merchant bankers were for centuries an
important source of capital to municipal, territorial, and national
rulers. During the Middle Ages and into the modern period, those loans
were largely personal. It is only during the early modern period that
loans increasingly became the responsibility of a government rather than
a ruler.[^55] These loans were, just as commercial loans, short-term,
but frequently rolled over.

Sometimes sovereign lending was also arranged via exchange contracts. A
notable example is the sixteenth century system of *asientos* loans to
the Spanish crown from primarily Genoese merchant bankers.[^56]
Typically, however, sovereign loans were organized via promissory notes,
not exchange contracts. These loans would have also been influenced by
usury laws. So, often, rather than charging interest, such loans would
be extended without interest and in exchange for monopoly rights on
trade. It is in this manner, for example, how certain Italian merchant
banks came to control the grain trade in Naples and Sicily and the wool
trade in England.[^57] Another popular method of extending sovereign
loans was through the promise of future revenues from taxes and duties.
Given the administrative knack of the Italian merchant banks, they,
hence, often acted as **tax farmers** for sovereigns in these
arrangements.

Lending to sovereigns was an extremely risky businesses, one that the
Medici, for example, as a consequence tried to avoid. As noted by De
Roover,[^58]

> Once engaged in making advances to princes, it was impossible to pull
> out and to retrench without courting disaster. Inevitably the lender
> was sucked deeper and deeper into the whirlpool, as the Medici found
> out to their detriment. And a banker who had been pressed dry was
> mercilessly dropped like an orange whose juice has been squeezed out.

## 4. The Hanseatic League

The Italians dominated trade and finance in the Mediterranean region and
Western Europe. In northern Europe, trade was dominated by Germans
through the Hanseatic League.[^59] The term **hansa** referred to
certain associations of travelling merchants doing business in foreign
cities. In the High Middle Ages, increasing cooperation between these
associations eventually grew into a federation of towns known as the
**Hanseatic League**. The League lasted into the early modern period,
but its peak occurred during the Late Middle Ages.

There were various degrees of membership and the pool of member towns
obviously changed throughout the league's existence. In general,
however, members were in northern Germany and the rest of the baltic
region, as well as in Western Germany and the Netherlands. Core members
were towns such as Lübeck, Rostock, Hamburg, and Cologne. Most of the
merchants and associates that operated within the league were,
naturally, German. The league also had trading outposts in many towns
that were not members. These were called *kontors*, and included such
places as London, Bruges, Bergen, and Novgorod. Often the merchants
operated within particular areas of the city, and they would have formed
colonies similar to the Italians. Particularly in some of the *kontors*,
trading activities would have been conducted under fairly risky
circumstances.

The merchants of the Hanseatic League were in many regards not quite as
developed as their Italian counterparts. While trade indeed became more
sedentary over time, that transformation was less developed than among
the Italian merchants. Large trading companies did not arise. In
general, the business techniques of the Hanseatic merchants also
remained behind those of the Italians, at least until the sixteenth
century. Notably, banking and money markets in the Baltic region were
much less developed than in the Mediterranean and much of Western
Europe. As a consequence, moving funds within and outside of the
Hanseatic region often occurred through shipments of specie.
