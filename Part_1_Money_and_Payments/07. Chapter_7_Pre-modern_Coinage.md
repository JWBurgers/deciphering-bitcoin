# 7. Pre-modern coinage

Modern physical money generally consists of coins and paper bills. But
at least until the seventeenth century, anything resembling modern paper
money did not exist in Europe. Coins, however, have a much longer
history, one that dates back to the seventh century BC. In this chapter,
we lay out some of the key developments and aspects of pre-modern
coinage: that is, the systems and practices surrounding the production
and use of coins.

In *Section 1*, we begin with a discussion of the first European coins.
It allows us to introduce a number of key notions for the subsequent
discussion. *Section 2* traces the development of coins from ancient
Greece to the introduction of the Carolingian penny. *Section 3*
describes Europe's transition to a money economy in the High and Late
Middle Ages. In *Section 4*, we, then, complete the historical overview
of coinage until the modern era. We conclude in *Sections 4* and *5* by
discussing the pre-modern bimetallist standard and some technological
aspects of pre-modern coinage.[^1]

## 1. The first coins

As metallic objects were increasingly valued during the transition from
the Stone Age to the Bronze Age, it is unsurprising that they took on a
monetary role.

According to our archaeological records, the world's oldest urban
centers were the Ancient Sumerian city-states which developed in the
second half of the fourth millennium BC. Economic life in these cities
was centralized around temples and palaces, often massive operations
involving hundreds or even thousands of people. To increase transparency
and efficiency, these ancient Sumerian temples and palaces began
expressing the value of economic transactions such as the extension of a
loan and the payment of a tax in certain select commodities. While
various commodities were utilized—including barley, wool, copper, and
iron—silver eventually became the most common standard of value.
Naturally, pieces of silver in various shapes and sizes became a common
medium of exchange to settle transactions.[^2]

The adoption of monetary properties by metallic objects also spread
elsewhere. Towards the end of the third millennium BC, particularly
pieces of silver of various shapes and sizes were widespread
standardized objects of value in the Near East and Mediterranean,
normally under the support of authorities. Though much later, in Ancient
China bronze cowry shells, spade-shaped, and knife-shaped moneys became
popular around the middle of the first millennium BC.[^3]

In Europe, what we now recognize as coins are generally thought to have
originated in Lydia and Ionia—regions that are now part of western
Turkey—during the seventh century BC. It seems that India invented
punch-marked coins, mostly made of silver, at around the same time.[^4]
Round bronze coins with square and round holes emerged in China somewhat
later during the fourth century BC.[^5] These innovations probably
happened largely independently, as the European, Indian, and Chinese
coins had substantial differences between them with regards to their
physical characteristics and methods of production.

The Lydian and Ionian coins were made from precious metals.
Specifically, they were made from electrum, a mixture of gold and silver
that occurred naturally in the region. These first European coins were
also hammered. A blank, round piece of metal—often called a
flan—would be placed in between two engraved dies. The minter would,
then, strike the top die with a hammer to create round pieces of roughly
the same shape and size embellished by engravings on each side. The
Ionians and Lydians melted the metal and then poured it into molds for
obtaining the flans. This made for very thick coins. In the middle of
the eighth century, the Carolingian Franks, then, adopting the practice
from Umayyad Spain, began hammering metal into much thinner sheets
before cutting them into blanks and hammering the blanks into coins.
This hammered minting process essentially persisted into the early
modern period, though some variations on it existed.[^6]

From Lydia and Ionia, the usage of coins quickly spread throughout the
Greek world. A common claim is that coins made from only gold or
silver—at least to the extent possible at the time given the primitive
purification techniques—were first minted in the middle of the sixth
century BC by King Croesus, the last ruler of the Kingdom of Lydia.
These coins called croeseids had the foreparts of a bull and lion on one
side and two squares pressed into the other side. While the gold
croeseids were, indeed, probably the first gold coins, the earliest
silver coins may also date back slightly earlier to the Greek island of
Aegina.[^7] Electrum coins continued to be popular, but were eventually
abandoned in Classical Greece (480 BC – 323 BC).

At this moment, we pause our history of coins and introduce some key
terminology on the basis of the discussion above. We start with the
difference between precious and base metals.

Gold and silver are precious metals, that is, metals with a very high
economic value.[^8] Such metals typically resist oxidation and
corrosion, though silver less so than gold. While other precious metals
exist such as platinum, palladium, and rhodium, only gold and silver
have historically been important for coinage. Until the nineteenth
century, certainly the most important coins were made from gold or
silver. Base metals, by contrast, commonly occur in nature and are of
lower economic value. They were also used for making coins before the
modern era, particularly copper. Often copper was used as an alloy in
gold and silver coins. Coins with copper as the dominant ingredient also
existed. Since the end of the twentieth century our coins are
exclusively made from base metals such as copper, zinc, nickel, and
aluminum.

Objects and materials made out of gold and silver (and any other metal)
can be assessed according to their purity or fineness. With modern
technology, we can make coins that consist almost exclusively out of
gold and silver, that is, with a fineness of 99.99% or even more.
Historical techniques did not allow for achieving quite such high
degrees of fineness. Additionally, until modern times, the precious
metal content of coins or other objects was difficult to determine with
a high degree of precision.

The most common pre-modern tool for assessing the gold and silver purity
of coins was the touchstone. It was a slab of dark, hard stone—most
commonly slate—on which one could rub a coin to produce a streak.
Using assay needles with varying and known degrees of fineness, one
could then make additional streaks on the stone to determine the
fineness of the coin by comparison. The touchstone test is estimated to,
at best, have only been within 2% to 3% accurate during the medieval
period. Various numismatic historians estimate that accuracy even lower,
somewhere between five and ten percent.[^9] In the nineteenth century,
the touchstone was supposedly only about 1% off the mark on average. All
these accuracy indications are only for coins that had a high degree of
fineness. The lower the purity of a coin, namely, the more difficult it
was to estimate its fineness by a touchstone test.[^10]

Fully melting down a coin, assayers could determine its silver or gold
purity to a much higher degree of accuracy. Yet, accuracy was still not
perfect, as small amounts of lead and other trace elements were
difficult to distinguish from the gold or silver content. And, of
course, the coin would be lost in this manner, so it was hardly
practical in the context of commercial exchange.[^11]

We should clearly distinguish between coins and bullion. We can
understand the latter here as objects composed of a high concentration
of gold, silver, or another, typically precious metal with basic,
standardized shapes. Bullion is intended for trade, storage, or
industrial use, and usually comes in the form of bars, but also as
sheets, plates, strings, pellets, or dust. Larger shapes cast from
molten metal, especially bars, are sometimes also called ingots. Bullion
can be melted, shaped, or sometimes used directly for creating more
refined products such as jewelry, tableware, plates, and, of course,
coins. These finished objects can, in turn, be molten down and returned
into bullion. Modern bullion, particularly bars, normally come with
stamps indicating weight and fineness. While such inscriptions existed
on metallic objects in earlier times, its modern introduction for
bullion bars probably began in northern Italy during the thirteenth
century.[^12]

Gold and silver, particularly in bullion or coin form, are commonly
known as commodity moneys. The concept of a commodity money is somewhat
tricky. But essentially, it denotes any money that also has substantial
commercial value outside of its role as money. Well-known historical
examples include wheat, barley, tobacco, dried fish, pepper, corn, and
cattle. For hundreds of years, hemp and silk textiles had a monetary
function in Ancient China.[^13] Cigarettes have historically functioned
as currency in prisons and POW camps.[^14] More recently that role has
been assumed by packets of ramen noodles in American prisons.[^15]

Commodities are basic, fairly uniform goods or raw materials. Ramen
noodles and cigarettes are much more refined products and not typically
called commodities. But throughout history, conventional commodities
have been most commonly adopted as more primitive forms of money. Hence,
any money with substantial commercial value outside of its monetary role
typically receives the designation of "commodity money", even if it is
not always a commodity strictly speaking. Gold and silver are
historically the most significant forms of commodity money. That surely
has various reasons, but in any case is certainly related to properties
such as their natural scarcity, portability, divisibility, and
durability. Given the historical prominence of gold and silver a
commodity money, the standardization of these metals into coins and
bullion was extensive.

Commodity money exchanges should be carefully distinguished from barter
exchanges, as the two types are commonly confused. In a paradigmatic
barter exchange, all the goods exchanged are desired by the respective
recipients for personal consumption or at least for consumption within
their direct family and social circles. Within a commodity money
exchange, however, certain goods are involved in the exchange, not with
a direct eye on consumption, but because they are easily exchangeable
with others. Paying for a new cow with barley in Ancient Mesopotamia
was, in fact, not really barter. Instead, barley was commonly accepted
as money, so the party receiving barley often could have passed it on to
someone else in an exchange rather than use it for direct consumption.

Many primitive moneys are probably best described as commodity moneys.
But there are certainly exceptions. The Spartans, for example, treated
iron slab money with vinegar, so as to make it useless for anything but
money.[^16] The most well-known example, however, comes from Yap, a
Pacific island about a 1000 kilometers east of the Philippines.[^17]

In the nineteenth century, European visitors of Yap increasingly noted
the existence of circular disks with round holes made of crystalline
limestone. These are commonly known as rai stones. The limestone was not
native to Yap, but sourced from quarries on other islands in the
Pacific, mainly Palau, nearly five hundred kilometers away. One account
from 1882 reported about 400 Yapese men working in the Palauan quarries,
roughly ten percent of the Yapese population at the time. According to
legend, the practice began five to six hundred years ago and the round
shape of the stones mimics the full moon.

Until the end of the nineteenth century, the rai stones were transported
across the open ocean by canoes and rafts. These stones were sometimes
quite large, and could reportedly be even more than a meter in diameter.
At that point Europeans also began transporting the stones to Yap with
larger and more modern ships. Some of these stones were incredibly
large. The largest known stone from this period is reportedly on the
island of Rumung measuring 3.6 meters in diameter and 0.5 meters thick.
It weighs four tons.

While Yap also had various commodity moneys, the rai stones were
commonly used as money for certain types of exchanges. During the
nineteenth century, they were used to pay for pigs, canoes, doctors,
sorcerers, tattooers, and the use of fishing waters. They were also used
in dowries, funerals, war reparations, and the formation of alliances.
In modern-day, the practice of exchanging rai stones is still continued
in ceremonial settings.

While size mattered, the value of the stones was also dependent on other
factors. This included factors such as the rarity and beauty of the
stone as well as the craftmanship of the carving. But a stone's value
was also affected by factors such as whether anyone carrying the stone
was injured or killed. The mode of transport also mattered, and the
European stones were generally of much lower value despite their larger
sizes. Interestingly, the disks often did not move during exchanges, but
ownership was just socially re-assigned. In one case, a disk that had
fallen into the ocean was still used in exchange as everyone assumed it
would have remained in place.

Besides their use as money, the stones really did not have any practical
function, at best only a ceremonial one. So they were not really a
commodity money, but actually much more akin to a modern fiat money: a
money with negligible intrinsic value that is socially accepted, often
by official decree. Rather than resembling coin or paper notes, the rai
stones are actually more akin to the fiat money entries found in an
account book. This is why it mattered so little that the stones remained
in place, even at the bottom of the ocean, during many exchanges. As
suggested by Bryan,[^18] the value of the rai stones were seemingly
dependent on the marginal costs of production. This would have limited
the quantity of value in the Yapese economy, even as Europeans began
transporting stones of enormous size.

The distinction between commodity moneys and the more fiat-like
primitive moneys like the Yapese rai stones and Spartan iron slab money
is a graded one. Perhaps an example that is less clear-cut is that of
the cowrie shell. These shells have been used across various cultures
and times as money, including in India, China, Africa, the Pacific, and
North America.[^19] They were, for instance, common in ancient China
during the second and first millennium BC. The non-monetary use of the
natural cowrie shells was generally limited to decorative and ceremonial
purposes, a function heavily intertwined with their role as money. While
perhaps less like fiat money than rai stones or the Spartan iron slabs,
these cowrie shells were not quite as paradigmatically a case of
commodity money.

Interestingly, around the middle of the first millennium, the Chinese
began to use cowrie shells made from bronze.[^20] By contrast to the
natural cowrie shell, the bronze cowrie shell was, in fact, a commodity
money given the value of its bronze content.

## 2. The Carolingian penny

The Greek knowledge on coinage was naturally adopted within the Roman
world. By the fall of the Western Roman Empire, it relied largely on
gold coins—the solidus, semissis, and tremissis or trientes—and
bronze coins—the follis and nummis. Some silver coins were also
produced in the fifth century. Other than in Britain, the coin systems
persisted in formerly Roman Europe during the downfall of the Empire in
the fifth century. Over time, however, both the quantity and quality of
coinage decreased. Standards for copper and silver coins deteriorated
quickly, and they were no longer minted by the end of the sixth century.
Copper coins would not be re-introduced into Europe until the late
fifteenth century. By the eighth century, gold coin minting also seems
to have ceased almost entirely.

Various factors were at play in the deterioration of the coin systems
Europe inherited from the Roman world. Most importantly, the fiscal and
commercial needs for coins had mostly been lost by the seventh century.
The tax and administrative systems inherited from the Romans had largely
broken down. Soldiers increasingly were paid in land, rather than coin.
Local and regional markets were in decline and land rents were also
increasingly paid with produce or labor. By the seventh century, only
gold coins really remained in limited use such as for gift exchanges,
ceremonies, compensation payments, fines, marriages, and funerals. The
deterioration in gold coins specifically was compounded by the lack of
European gold mining, the use of gold for trade with the Near East, and
increased hoarding.

After a cessation in the minting of silver coins for about a hundred
years, a key event in the history of European coinage occurs towards the
end of the seventh century. At this time, the Frisians and Merovingian
Franks began minting the denarius, a silver coin named after a Roman
coin that was common from the third century BC until the third century
AD. The medieval version became very popular. Within a few decades, it
was also minted in Anglo-Saxon England. Minting of the denarius, then,
spread to other parts of Western and central Europe throughout the
eighth century via the Carolingian Franks.

The Carolingian Franks did much to standardize the production of the
denarius. By the eighth century, Europe had, despite the low production
of coins, a great number of mints. These were, of course, very basic
operations. While minting had largely been understood as a royal
prerogative, these mints were mainly under the control of local rulers
and ecclesiastical authorities by this time. The Franks, however,
re-exerted control over the mints in their territory. Pepin the Short
introduced thinner, broader flan for the silver denarius, a format that
was indirectly adopted from the Persian silver Dirham through the Muslim
Arabs. Charlemagne notably set out a common standard for the denarius at
the end of the eighth century. Surviving denarii from the period suggest
that they were minted at around 2 cm in diameter and at slightly less
than 2 grams with a silver purity of around 90%.[^21] The denarius was
also minted in Anglo-Saxon England with roughly the same weight and
fineness standards.

The silver denarius was essentially the only type of coin used in Europe
until the thirteenth century. As the Carolingian Empire disintegrated
during the early ninth century, however, mint control again
decentralized and increasingly came into the hands of the nobility and
ecclesiastical authorities. In this way, the standards for the denarius
started to vary in terms of their weight, shape, design, and fineness.
In many areas on the continent, the denarius was debased—that is,
reduced in its precious metal content—because of intense competition
between mints to attract silver bullion.[^22] This type of competition
became particularly intense between France, England, and surrounding
states in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and has subsequently
been labeled a monetary war. It started at the end of thirteenth century
when Philip the Fair (1268–1314) began heavily debasing the French
silver coins to raise revenues primarily for managing the Gascon War and
conflicts in Flanders.[^23]

The Latin name for the coin, denarius, received vernacular adaptations
across Europe. It came to be called the *denier* in Frankia, the
*denaro* in northern Italy, the penny in Anglo-Saxon England, the
*pfennig* in the Holy Roman Empire, and the *penning* in the Low
Countries.

At least by the early eighth century, a system of counting coins had
also developed in Europe using units called *libra* and *solidii*. Each
*solidus* was equivalent to twelve coins and each *libra* equivalent to
twelve *solidii*. For instance, in the twelfth century some account book
entry for English pennies might have been recorded as "3 li. 6 s. 10
d.", where the "li" here stands for "libra", the "s" for "solidus", and
the "d" for "denarius", as these books were standardly kept in Latin.
The total number of pennies received according to this account entry
would, then, have been 850. The *£* symbol commonly came to denote the
*libra* across Europe, and notably persists in modern times as the
symbol for the English pound sterling.[^24] Even though the libra
counting system was the most popular, other coin counting systems also
developed in Europe before the modern era. These counting systems
obviously offered convenience for account books when larger payments
were recorded.[^25]

While the Roman-inspired solidus had long disappeared as a gold coin by
the time of the Carolingian Franks, it thus came to denote a counting
unit. The "shilling" was eventually the English name of the Latin
solidus. In Frankia the solidus became known as the *sou*, in northern
Italy as the *soldo*, and often as the *schilling* in the Holy Roman
Empire and the *schelling* in the Low Countries. The "pound" was
eventually the English name for the Latin *libra*. It became known as
the *livre* in Frankia, the *libra* (eventually *lira*) in northern
Italy, the *pfund* in the Holy Roman Empire, and the *pond* in the Low
Countries.

During his reign, Charles the Great also standardized the pound weight
system, a system of measurement that, like the denarius, also traces its
origins to the Ancient Romans. A commonly cited weight for the
Carolingian pound is 489.51 grams,[^26] though some discussion remains
on that subject. After the disintegration of the Carolingian Empire,
however, various systems for weights re-emerged in Europe, including the
tower pound system, the troy pound system, the avoirdupois pound system,
various mark systems, and so on.

Before the modern era, a minting policy sometimes relied on a
straightforward correspondence between the pound counting system and a
pound weight system. Consider, for instance, the issue of the famous
tealby penny—also known as the cross-and-crosslet—in England under
the reign of Henry II (b. 1133–1189).

Initially, from each troy pound of silver provided by a customer—or
roughly 367.5 grams at the time—the King's mints supposedly produced
252 pennies. A total of 12 pennies were, then, retained by the mints to
cover production costs, known as the brassage, and the monetary
authority's profit, known as the seigniorage (which went to the monarchy
in this case). The customer then received 240 pennies of which the pure
silver weight was equivalent to one tower pound, or roughly 350 grams.
In 1180, the silver weight of the tealby penny seems to have been
slightly reduced, so that each tower pound of silver yielded 246
pennies: a pound, or 240 coins, for the customer and 6 coins for the
mint to cover brassage and seigniorage.[^27]

Supposedly, Charlemagne's mints had also minted 240 pennies from a
Carolingian pound of silver or by minting more had returned exactly 240
pennies to its customers. Despite such occasional historical
correspondences, the pound counting system is analytically distinct from
the various pound weight systems. Often a pound of coins, meaning 240 of
them, had no logically following total weight or silver weight within
some pound weight system. While a pound of English pennies, for
instance, still contained close to a tower pound of silver in the
thirteenth century, this weight fell to less than half by 1544.[^28]

## 3. The development of the money economy

Despite the advances made by the Carolingian Franks, Europe remained
largely manorial and self-sufficient in the ninth century. There was
little of a real money economy marked by commercial exchange and trade.
With the disintegration of the Carolingian Empire, mint production seems
to have even declined again, a development certainly also influenced by
the raids, migrations, and invasions from Vikings and Magyars. The role
of coin in the last two centuries of the Dark ages was still quite
minimal. Obligations were commonly specified in a non-metal commodity
money, or in both coin and an alternative commodity money.[^29] Only
from the late tenth century onwards is there a substantial revival of
minting. This period, of course, coincides with the start of the long
period of economic growth and development that Europe experienced after
the closing of the Dark Ages.

Throughout the High and Late Middle Ages, Europe, then, transformed from
a largely self-sufficient economy where primitive commodity moneys such
as horses, oxen, sheep, cloths, vases, or grain were commonly used in
exchange,[^30] to a developed commercial economy based on sophisticated
money and credit arrangements. The "money" here includes specie and, to
some degree, bullion. But it also includes the "money" that lived as
account entries, particularly within local deposit banks and
international merchant banks. These entries, as illustrated in
subsequent chapters, were only partially covered by specie or bullion.
The development of deposit banking and merchant banking throughout this
period was, in fact, probably more important for the transition to a
well-developed commercial economy than the growth in the use of specie
and bullion.

The most profound period of change was the long thirteenth century. It
was really during this period that land rents, for example, which had
previously been paid with labor or produce, were increasingly paid in
money. Particularly poorer farmers as a consequence increasingly shifted
to wage work, while local and regional markets drastically expanded. The
shift to money rents increasingly allowed rulers and wealthy landowners
freedom of movement. Increasingly, they permanently or at least
partially established themselves in towns and cities. Those regions with
a strong monarchy, increasingly were dominated by specific capitals such
as London, Paris, Rome, Naples, and Prague.

The long thirteenth century also saw profound fiscal, military, and
political organizational changes. The money economy increasingly enabled
taxation regimes, which grew to a scale that had not been witnessed
since late Antiquity. The money revenues of rulers, cities, and towns
increased tremendously. For instance, the city of Florence's income
transitioned from roughly 25,000 Florentine pounds per year in 1240 to
950,000 Florentine pounds per year in 1343.[^31] The new tax systems
also supported borrowing by rulers. And loans were increasingly
necessary as noble knights and administrative officials were
increasingly paid with money instead of land. While mercenaries were
hardly new in this era, they were also hired on unforeseen scales within
the new money economy.

One way in which increasing streams of income supported loans to rulers
was through the potential for collateralized bespoke loans. The new
taxation systems were, however, also accompanied by the first movements
towards long-term, structured public debt, in lieu of short-term,
private loans. This transformation was largely confined to towns and
cities, which had the best credit standing, and many of them came to
have permanent public debts during this period.

In the north of Italy, standardized public debt securities were first
issued in the long thirteenth century, particularly among the cities and
towns with a strong Republican tradition.[^32] First among them was
probably Genoa in the second half of the twelfth century,[^33] soon
followed by Venice and eventually other communes and city-states such as
Florence and Lucca. While these public debt securities resembled modern
bonds, they were commonly issued on the basis of forced loans from the
wealthier citizens who seemed to have preferred them to taxation. In
addition, interest rate and principal payments were generally assured by
granting the bond holders certain rights over specific taxes, rather
than through a general faith in the credit of the government. These
government bonds were notably registered assets, not bearer assets.
While initially many bonds had maturity dates, over time perpetual bonds
became increasingly became common.

Active secondary markets developed for the northern Italian government
bonds where they were typically traded at a discount. Deposit banks and
merchant banks often acted as dealers in these markets and provided
substantial liquidity. In some places, these markets were facilitated by
brokers. The government bonds were popular investments and through
secondary markets diffused from the city's wealthy creditors to the
broader public. According to Pezzolo,[^34] by 1380 about one in six
Florentines was a creditor to the government, while by 1500 about one in
seven Genoese was a government creditor. The government bonds also took
on other financial functions such as collateral for lending.[^35]

Northwestern Europe also saw the rise of long-term structured debt,
though in a different form.[^36] In the early thirteenth century,
Troyes—one of the four towns to host the Fairs of Champagne—began
selling certain standardized contracts to raise capital. These were
called *rentes* in French and *renten* in Dutch. From Troyes, the
practice spread throughout the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries to
other municipalities in northern France, the Low Countries, and Germany.
The *rente* contracts came predominantly in two forms: *rente viagère*
contracts (or *lijfrent* contracts in Dutch) which promised payments
against a human life (typically the buyer), and *rente héritable*
contracts (or *erfelijk rent*, *erfrent*, or *losrent* contracts in
Dutch) which were inheritable and perpetual. The *rente* contracts also
became popular in many Spanish towns and cities from the early
fourteenth century onwards.

The *rente* contracts were annuities. Just like the government bonds in
northern Italy, they were typically backed by entitlements to specific
tax streams. Unlike the Italian bonds, however, the issue of *rente*
contracts was standardly viewed as the sale of a future income stream,
and technically not considered a loan. As with the Italian bonds,
secondary markets developed almost immediately. These were particularly
liquid in the Low Countries. Annuities were popular as a provision for
retirement, but also to provide for widows and orphans. Municipalities
frequently sought measures to restrict secondary trade. In this way, for
instance, Den Briel prohibited the resale of any credit instrument,
including *rentes*, in 1449, while Haarlem prohibited the sale of rente
contracts to people outside the city's jurisdiction in 1505.[^37]

While the availability of specie and bullion were hardly the only factor
in the transformation of the European economy that took place during the
High and Late Middle Ages, they certainly had an impact. Illustratively,
while some of the developments mentioned above began at the end of the
tenth century, they were halted to some extent in the eleventh century.
One main reason was certainly the scarcity of silver due to the drying
up of the Saxon silver deposits in the middle of the century as well as
the start of the crusades a few decades later. Whereas probably roughly
twenty million pennies were circulating in England around the
millennium, for example, this appears to have roughly halved by the
start of Henry II's monetary reforms.[^38]

The long thirteenth century, in turn, was accompanied by a silver mining
boom. The first major deposit discovered was at Freiburg and throughout
the period additional major silver deposits were discovered, primarily
in central Europe. From the mines in central Europe, the silver spread
quickly throughout Europe, particularly westwards and southwards. It was
during the long thirteenth century that we first see bars and other
bullion marked with official stamps indicating their weight and
fineness,[^39] and that mints, at least in some places, scale from small
operations to more substantial enterprises with divisions of labor and
larger-size labor forces. In some periods of production, these
operations apparently had more than a hundred workers, an operational
scale far beyond anything before the long thirteenth century.[^40] Given
that minting first scaled in northern Italy, many of these operations in
Europe, until the early modern era, were run for monetary authorities by
Italian entrepreneurs, particularly Tuscans. Many of these entrepreneurs
probably represented merchant banking companies.[^41]

The economic impact of the availability of specie and bullion can
perhaps most clearly be seen in the period from the late fourteenth and
for much of the fifteenth century, when Europe was marked by an acute
dearth of precious metals, particularly silver. This episode in European
history is commonly called the Great Bullion Famine.[^42] Important
causes were the drying up of Central European silver mines,
interruptions to the supply of gold, and persistent trade imbalances
with the Near East that drained the continent of specie. It has also
been argued that the switch to a silver standard in China was an
important cause.[^43] All these problems were compounded by the loss of
coins, the fearful hoarding of coins and bullion, and natural wear and
tear particularly on silver coins. While the recoinage of Edward I in
1278–80, for example, drew in one hundred tons of silver, the recoinage
of Henry IV in 1412–14 was only able to draw in two tons of silver, the
lack of silver partially compensated for by drawing in seventy tons of
gold.[^44]

During the Great Bullion Famine, people naturally turned to commodity
moneys. In various places such as Genoa, for example, pepper was
commonly employed as a medium of exchange in this period.[^45] But given
the consistent trade imbalances between Europe and the Near East,
commodity moneys were unable to fill the gap in trade settlement. Fears
over repayment also significantly impacted the extension of commercial
credit and caused substantial pressure on the leverage of deposit banks.
As to be expected, interest rates were much higher during the Great
Bullion Famine. These pressures only began easing in the 1460s,
primarily due to the production of larger silver deposits in the Alps
and the Ore Mountains.

Most of the Late Middle Ages was, thus, clearly a less thriving period
for the European economy than the long thirteenth century, even if the
interruptions to economic progress were perhaps not quite as severe as
originally imagined by many historians.[^46] Various factors contributed
to the general malaise, including wars, famines, and epidemics. This
period, for example, saw the start of the brutal Hundred Years War
between England and France (1337–1453), which had started when the
first King of the House of Valois, Philip VI (1293–1350), invaded the
English-held continental territory of Gascony and Edward III
(1312–1377) laid claim to the French crown. It also saw the first and
worst outbreak of the plague in Europe, the **Black Death**
(1347–1351), which probably killed between a quarter to half of
Europe's population. The Great Bullion Famine nevertheless certainly
played some role in the general malaise of the period.

## 4. Coinage until the modern era

What coins were used after the Carolingian era?

By the start of the thirteenth century, the uniform standards that were
set for the Carolingian and English pennies had devolved into a
scattering of widely divergent coins. The tournois, the parisis, the
provinois, the barcelonese, the genovese, the sterling, and the Flemish,
Brabantine, Hainaut, and Florentine penny all varied in their weight and
fineness standards. According to Kohn,[^47] by 1200, the weights of
pennies ranged from that of the English sterling at 1.46 grams with 1.35
grams of fine silver to the Venetian denaro at 0.25 grams with 0.05
grams of fine silver.

Many of the various pennies had by this time, more specifically, become
billon coins.[^48] This label typically indicates that a coin has a
silver fineness of 50% or less. Until the pre-modern era such coins were
standardly alloyed with copper, though sometimes other base metals such
as tin were also used.[^49] The main exception was, of course, the
English penny. The English rulers would, a few brief interruptions
notwithstanding, rigidly adhere to high standards for their silver coins
into the modern era.

The divergence in standards for the penny was in large part the result
of a decentralization in authority over minting after the disintegration
of the Carolingian Empire. In Frankia, the devolution into a feudal
system in the ninth and tenth centuries meant that mints largely came
under the control of counts and other feudal authorities, and sometimes
in the hands of bishops or abbeys. In much of the Holy Roman Empire,
many of the mints came into the hands of ecclesiastical authorities in
the tenth and eleventh centuries. In northern Italy, minting remained
under control of the Holy Roman Emperor at first, though
decentralization also started in the twelfth century. Competition
between the multitude of mints had led to the debasement of the penny in
many parts of the continent.[^50]

A notable exception to the fragmentation in control over minting was
England. As the Anglo-Saxons reconquered territory from the Vikings
during the late ninth and tenth centuries, England increasingly had a
uniform coinage, notwithstanding some small regional variations. In 930,
Athelstan first promulgated a law that only royally issued coins should
circulate the realm, a principle that has endured into modern times, but
now for the entire United Kingdom. From the end of the tenth century
until at least the early twelfth century, coins in England were
withdrawn from circulation and reminted every few years. This system
seems to have been maintained even during the North Sea Empire
(1016–1042) and persisted after the Norman conquest (1066), at least
until Henry II's introduction of the tealby penny in the twelfth
century.[^51]

Perhaps most interestingly, from the end of the tenth century until the
time of Henry II, the English pennies were at least partially on a fiat
standard: their economic value was in substantial part based on the word
of the king.[^52] Unlike as in the rest of Europe, that is, these
pennies were worth substantially more in the marketplace than their
(supposed) precious metal content. As stated by Spufford,[^53]

> These pennies fluctuated quite considerably in weight and fineness
> from one issue to the next and yet their value remained constant,
> since it derived not from their intrinsic worth, but from the word of
> the king. Elsewhere in western Europe the value of the denier
> generally depended on its silver content or its supposed silver
> content. Coin certainly had a premium over uncoined silver, but this
> premium did little more than cover minting-costs and give a very
> modest profit to the ruler.

All these aspects to the English penny were made possible by strong,
centralized royal control over the coinage. England had many mints
throughout much of the period, but they were operating in the service of
the king. After a brief interruption to the stable centralized control
over the coinage during a period of civil war known as the Anarchy
(1135–1153), it was re-established by king Henry II.[^54]
Interestingly, the famous tealby penny was, like its continental
counterparts, primarily valued both legally and in the marketplace on
the basis of its actual or supposed precious metal content.

In the early thirteenth century, in the midst of the silver boom, larger
silver coins started to make an appearance in Europe. The most important
of these early larger silver coins was known as the **groat**, commonly
known as the *grosso* in Italy, the *gros* in France, the *groot* in the
Low Countries, and the *groschen* in Germany and surrounding areas. In
most European countries, the literal translation of the name of the coin
was roughly "the large". The English name, groat, is instead just a
phonetic adaptation from the Dutch "groot".

The earliest groats were struck in Italy. The Venetian groat, also known
as the matapan, was probably minted from 1201 onwards. Other northern
Italian cities soon followed such as Genoa, Verona, Pisa, Florence, and
Lucca.[^55] Outside of Italy, the groat was first struck in Marseille
and somewhat later in 1266 at the famous mint at Tours by Louis IX. The
latter's famous version of the coin became known as the **gros
tournois**. Towards the end of the thirteenth century, rulers in various
regions of the Low Countries began minting groats. The minting of the
famous Flemish groat seems to have started in the first years of
fourteenth century.

The sizes and weights of different groats varied substantially from the
outset. The gros tournois, for example, was first struck at 4.22 grams
while most of the northern Italian groats were initially 1.7 grams such
as in Verona, Siena, Pisa, and Florence. All of them, however, had a
high degree of fineness. The relative value of these various groats to
the regional penny very much depended on the locality. In Venice, the
denaro had been debased so much that the matapan initially had a value
at least twenty-four times larger.[^56] In Genoa, however, the grosso
was only worth four times the genoese initially.[^57]

According to Spufford,[^58] the primary impetus for the minting of these
new coins was the growth of urban and military money wages. With growth
in the number and size of these wages within the development of the
money economy, the minting of larger coins became attractive when the
payment of them in the regional pennies became sufficiently
inconvenient. Obviously, the value of the regional penny was critical to
that transformation. While initially motivated by the payment of wages,
the larger coins would have also been used for other large local
payments such as for rents, cattle, and taxes.

Pennies were, thus, increasingly utilized for smaller purchases in the
thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. Depending on the local
circumstances, other silver coins were also minted as necessary. In
various Italian cities, coins that were worth fractions of the penny
became common. In England, the new money economy motivated the minting
of wastels (half-pennies) and farthings (quarter-pennies) in 1279 and
1280. In earlier times, when demand for coin presumably was not as
large, pennies were also commonly cut in halves and quarters to provide
for smaller change.[^59]

Other than in England where the standards remained high, pennies and
smaller coins were commonly became billon coins. Copper coins were
introduced again to Europe probably by Naples in 1472, after an absence
of nearly a millennium. By the middle of the sixteenth century, copper
coins had also become common in the Low Countries. They only gained real
importance in France and England in the second half of the seventeenth
century. To serve very small purchases, England may have also had lead
tokens by end of the fifteenth century.[^60]

The larger silver coins that first appeared in the thirteenth century
were not primarily intended for international and interregional trade.
While silver coins were certainly also utilized in this context, silver
bullion, particularly in the form of bars or plates, had long been the
main form of silver money for trade. Silver bullion, particularly if
weight and fineness are known, is obviously much more convenient to
accept than counting out thousands of coins. It is also easier to
transport. Silver bullion received upon payment was sometimes minted
into coins, but often also just re-used for international and
interregional trade. In most places—England being a notable
exception[^61]—silver bullion was also commonly used in very large
local economic transactions.

Importantly, silver bullion was genuinely utilized as a commodity money
until the fourteenth century, just as silver coins. The receiving party
knew that the silver bullion could be utilized in subsequent
transactions as money and that they could also bring it to the mint for
obtaining coins. Any party accepting silver in a commercial exchange was
not doing so because there was an immediate consumption use for the
silver in their immediate social sphere.

While the use of silver bullion as a commodity money in European trade
was still common at the start of the fourteenth century, its role was
largely replaced by gold coins towards the end of it.

As noted already, Europe only had silver mines throughout the High
Middle Ages. Africa, by contrast, predominantly had gold mines. This
made gold relatively more valuable in Europe and silver relatively more
valuable in Africa. This discrepancy incentivized intercontinental flows
of the metals, silver from Europe to Africa and gold from Africa to
Europe. But while the gold that flowed through Africa and the Middle
East indeed somewhat overflowed into Christian European lands through
trade, tributes, and conquests, it was not until the middle of the
thirteenth century that sufficient gold had flowed into Europe for the
minting of native coins.

The first major European gold coins were minted in 1252 by Genoa and
Florence. These coins were called the genovino and the fiorino d'oro
(also gold florin or Florentine florin) respectively. Venice followed by
first minting the ducat in 1284 (from 1562 onwards called the sequine or
*zecchino*).[^62] All of these coins were around 3.5 grams of near pure
gold. The florin and ducat had lasting importance as European-wide
currencies into the early modern era. The second half of the thirteenth
century and the fourteenth century also saw the production of gold coins
with more of a regional importance such as the gold penny and noble in
England, the Rhineland gulden or florin in Germany, and the écu and écu
à la chaise in France.

Gold coins began to spread throughout Europe in the second half of the
thirteenth century. They became increasingly common at the Fairs of
Champagne. During the first half of the fourteenth century, gold coins
increasingly became the primary trade currency in the core commercial
areas of Europe. A big driver in this regard was the Hundred Year War
where both the English and French borrowed heavily in gold coin from
Italian merchant bankers.[^63] The spread of the use of gold coin was
also spurred on by the intensive mining of major gold deposits in
Hungary. From the core commercial areas of Europe, gold coins spread to
other areas of Europe, including the peripheries, throughout the
fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Gold coins came to dominate as a currency in trade. To offer the same
economic value, gold coins obviously required much less in weight than
silver. Due to the higher density of gold, the reduction on the volume
required for some economic value would have been even greater. In the
early fourteenth century, right before Hungarian gold flooded Europe,
the necessary weight in silver for some economic value was fourteen
times that of gold. Due to the lower density of silver, its volume would
have required twenty-six times the volume of gold for the same economic
value.[^64]

All this obviously made gold coins much more practical than silver
bullion in international and regional trade and in the context of other
large purchases. The use of gold coins was also spurred on by laws and
regulations. In many European realms, the trade in or export of bullion
was severely restricted from the late thirteenth and early fourteenth
century onwards, often with fairly severe punishments in place. With
some exceptions, bullion generally had to be delivered to the ruler's
mints to support the coinage. Early modern Holland and various northern
Italian city-states with extensive bullion trade were notable
exceptions. Often restrictions on the domestic usage of foreign coins
were also in place; those coins not exempt were commonly understood to
be "bullion". Domestic and foreign coins could, however, commonly still
be used to pay for imports. In England, notably, these types of laws
took a particularly harsh form. From January 1364 until May 1663, it had
a general prohibition on the export of gold and silver in any form. The
circulation of all foreign coins was also banned for practically the
entire period.

All these laws and regulations generally had the intention of attracting
bullion to the ruler's mints—as most rulers had to rely solely on the
import of precious metals[^65]—and preventing competition from foreign
coins. They were largely motivated by the circulation of debased foreign
coins, sometimes imitations of domestic coins, and the Great Bullion
famine. Eventually, the general spirit of these laws and motivations
made their way into the writings of economic thinkers known as
mercantilists, the most prominent of which did their writing between the
early sixteenth and the late eighteenth centuries. While the
mercantilists differed in their exact ideas, they all commonly promoted
the idea that national wealth and power was to a large extent dependent
on a nation's stock of gold and silver, a specific idea also known as
bullionism. Mercantilists commonly, therefore, promoted exports over
imports as a way to increase the stock of precious metals.[^66]

With the rise of the gold coin for trade, by the end of the Middle Ages,
Europeans, thus, utilized three main forms of coin: gold coins, good
silver coins, and black money. Gold coins were for very large payments,
typically within the domain of noblemen, merchants, and government
officials. The rest of the population generally dealt with larger silver
coins for wages and other large local payments, while black money was
utilized for smaller, everyday purchases. In many cases, the role of
black money was fulfilled by low-grade silver coins, also known as
billon coins. Copper coins also had a role as black money.

A key economic event in the early modern era was the flooding of world
markets with silver from the New World.[^67] This was spurred on not
just by the vastness of its silver deposits, but also the application of
a new production technique in the sixteenth century known as
amalgamation. The technique consisted of mixing pulverized precious
metal ore with quicksilver and, then, washing it out with salted water
to extract silver or gold. While the New World also increased global
gold supplies, about fifty times more silver was produced in the New
World than gold during the early modern era.[^68] Unsurprisingly, gold
became relatively more valuable. It has been estimated that the European
silver to gold ratio increased from 10.75 in 1500 to 15.61 in 1800.[^69]

The discovery of silver in the New World had substantial impact on
global trade. While regional European trade largely operated on the
basis of bills of exchange and gold for settlement in the early modern
era, global trade was dominated by silver. In contrast to the medieval
period, however, this silver largely came in the form of coins, notably
the Spanish dollar, also known as the piece of eight or peso. The
Spanish dollar came to dominate payments in international trade. It was
also the most common coin in Colonial America. The paper money produced
by the Continental Congress during the Revolutionary War was, in
principle, redeemable for Spanish dollars. The *Coinage Act of 1792*
created the US dollar system and pegged the U.S. dollar against the
Spanish dollar.[^70] According to *Section 9* of the act,

> Dollars or units—each to be of the value of a Spanish milled dollar
> as the same is now current, and to contain three hundred and
> seventy-one grains and four sixteenth parts of a grain of pure, or
> four hundred and sixteen grains of standard silver.

The Spanish dollars, minted in the New World, were used to buy imports
from Europe. The Europeans, then, utilized these Spanish dollars to
purchase imports from the Near East and Asia. The silver coins were
desired in those regions for the production of their own currencies or,
in the case of China, even to directly function as currency. The
Europeans were, thus, taking advantage of the relatively low valuation
of silver in the New World and the relatively high valuation of it in
the Near East and Asia. While within Europe the bill of exchange and to
some degree gold coins would have continued within intracontinental
trade, international trade became dominated by silver coins even though
it may have been less practical.

While bullion and paper notes had a role as money in the early modern
era, physical money, thus, consisted predominantly of gold and silver
coins until the 1800s.

## 5. The bimetallic standard

Gold, silver, and copper bullion as well as other metals had a certain
value in the pre-modern marketplace just as they do now. We can define
the intrinsic value of a coin merely as the economic value of its metal
content if it were in bullion form. Gold coins, silver coins, and copper
coins are so-called because their intrinsic value is largely defined by
their gold, silver, and copper content respectively. In this way, billon
coins are typically understood as low-fineness silver coins, even though
copper was standardly the main ingredient in them. That is precisely
because the intrinsic value of billon coins was mostly determined by
their silver content rather than their copper content.

In the pre-modern period, coins generally did not have value marks. This
is unlike modern coins. The euro, for example, comes in coin
denominations of one, two, five, ten, and twenty eurocents as well as in
denominations of one or two euros. In the United States, the main coin
denominations are one, five, ten, and twenty-five cents, though rarely
seen fifty cent and one dollar coins are technically still legal tender.
But until the nineteenth century, such numerical indications of value
were largely absent from coins. The only notable exception are the coins
that circulated in the monetary system that ruled late Republican
Rome.[^71]

Despite the absence of value marks, gold and silver coins did receive
official legal values in their native monetary realms. These were
normally expressed in a single standard of value known as a money of
account. Commonly, that money of account was just an important silver
coin. In this manner, for example, the penny was used in early modern
England to define the value of the farthing (a quarter of a penny); the
halfpenny (a half of a penny); the groat (four pennies); the testoon
(twelve pennies); and the crown (sixty pennies). The gold coins had
similar exchange rates expressed in silver pennies. Sometimes foreign
coins also received official values in pre-modern monetary realms,
typically those common to trade such as the Florentine florin and the
Venetian ducat. The official legal value of a coin is commonly also
called its face value—despite the absence of value markings on
pre-modern coins—or its par value—where "par" is Latin for
"standard".

A commonly made distinction is between full-bodied coins and
tokens.[^72] A full-bodied coin is normally characterized as any coin
for which the legal value is roughly equivalent to its intrinsic value.
A token coin, on the other hand, is one where the legal value is
substantially higher than its intrinsic value. The boundary between
"roughly equivalent" and "substantially higher" is, of course, not
precise. But typically any coin with an intrinsic value at least ten to
twenty percent less than its face value is called a token. Given that
official values were commonly expressed in a particular silver coin,
such a coin, in good condition at least, was necessarily full-bodied.

Strictly speaking, a particular coin might shift between a full-bodied
and token status, even multiple times, during the course of its
lifetime. Suppose that a gold coin is issued with a legal value at
twenty-four times that of a silver penny. The price per unit weight of
pure gold is twelve times that of pure silver. In addition, the gold
coin's pure gold weight is precisely twice the silver coin's pure silver
weight. So, the gold coin's official legal value is exactly consistent
with its intrinsic value. Suppose now, however, that the price per unit
weight of pure gold falls from twelve to ten times that of pure silver.
In that case, the intrinsic value of the gold coin is only twenty times
that of the silver penny in the marketplace, even though it's official
value is still twenty-four times that of the silver penny. So the
originally full-bodied gold coin has now transformed more into a token,
one where the intrinsic value is almost twenty percent less than its
face value.

A key question about pre-modern coinage is the influence of a coin's
legal value, intrinsic value, and other factors on it's market value. We
will turn to detailed discussion about the market value of coins in
*Chapter 5*. Suffice to say here that coins did not always circulate at
their official value. So while a coin could *de jure* be a token because
its official value was substantially higher than its intrinsic value, it
could actually circulate in the market close to its intrinsic value and
be *de facto* a full-bodied coin. Our gold coin in the example above,
indeed, likely would have circulated at a discount from its official
value and be *de facto* a full-bodied coin.

Most pre-modern monetary systems, at least from the thirteenth century
onwards, are generally understood to have operated on bimetallic
standards. In the nineteenth century, states then adopted monometallic
standards. While many nations in North America and Europe initially had
a silver standard, they had all transitioned to a gold standard by the
end of the nineteenth century. This classical gold standard lasted until
the start of the first World War. Briefly and partially re-introduced
during the interwar years, most of the Western world then signed onto
the Bretton Woods system from 1944 to 1971. Within this sytem, US
dollars were convertible into gold for foreign central banks—having
ended for everyone else holding dollars in the 1930s[^73]—and all
other currencies had a fixed exchange rate against the US dollar.
Convertibility was completely ended by President Richard Nixon on August
15, 1971 in a televized address to the nation,[^74] after which Western
nations effectively had a full fiat standard.

What exactly is the difference between a monetary system on a
monometallic and on a bimetallic standard?

Pre-modern bimetallic systems were normally based on gold and silver.
Perhaps the main characteristic was that neither metal was utilized with
the sole purpose of only providing for token coins. Indeed, billon coins
(and copper coins) were often just utilized as tokens for small change.
Normally, they were also subsidiary money, only legally recognized for
discharging debts up to a certain amount.[^75] But certainly the fine
silver coins were not intended to be subservient to gold coins in this
manner.

Bimetallic systems also had three other characteristics. First, at least
the more important coins made out of each metal were full legal tender,
meaning they could be legally used to discharge a debt of any size.
Second, the coins of each metal were also officially valued in the same
money of account. This assured that bimetallic systems defined a value
ratio of silver to gold.[^76] Finally, the coins from both metals were
normally minted according to the free coinage principle: members of the
public, often money changers or merchants, could bring bullion or old
coins into the mint and in return receive newly minted coins.[^77]

By contrast, in a monometallic standard, one metal was primary for
defining the monetary standard. While coins of other metals circulated,
they explicitly were intended to function as tokens rather than
full-bodied coins. These token coins were normally minted under
centralized control and often also only partial legal tender.

In 1816, for example, England officially moved to a gold standard.[^78]
All existing silver coins were called up and recoined. All circulating
coins, gold and silver, were defined as the equivalent of a certain
value of gold bullion. While the gold coins remained full-bodied, the
new silver coins had a much lower intrinsic than face value. The free
coinage of silver was also suspended and silver coins were made only
limited legal tender. While initially the English government only sought
to maintain stability in the silver coins by limiting their quantity, in
the 1830s the silver token coins were guaranteed to be convertible at
par value. That guarantee was offered by the Bank of England, which had
become de facto the institution for storing the bullion and coins of the
government and the crown during the eighteenth century.

Just because a monetary system was bimetallic does not mean that coins
of both metals commonly circulated. During the eighteenth century, for
example, England largely operated on the basis of gold coins. Between
1717 and 1774, hundreds of thousands of pounds worth of gold coins were
produced annually; yet, in only six years did the economic value of the
production of silver coins exceed ten thousand pounds.[^79] The scarcity
of silver was the product of the systemic legal overvaluation of gold
coins, which led to the hoarding and export of silver coins rather than
their utilization in commerce. The shortage of silver coins was probably
mainly addressed through credit arrangements and other financial
instruments. When England moved to a gold standard in 1816, it was,
thus, mostly just a legal change that followed the economic realities of
the time.

Gold was substantially overvalued by the official monetary regulations
in eighteenth century England. Hence, gold coins were more like tokens
rather than full-bodied coins. Yet, England's monetary system at the
time should still be viewed as bimetallic. Gold coins were not intended
merely as tokens and to be purely subservient to silver coins. In
addition, the system was marked by other characteristics of bimetallic
systems, including the free coinage of both metals and full legal tender
status of both types of coins.

In the pre-modern era, trimetallic systems were occasionally also
maintained. A somewhat curious instance of such a system existed in
Sweden between 1624 and 1776. Given Sweden's monopoly position in copper
production, it wanted to drive the international copper price upwards.
In addition, there was a shortage of silver money, probably due to its
use in trade and the maintenance of mercenary armies. So, in 1624,
Sweden began producing full-bodied copper plate money, not just to serve
as petty change, but as an alternative to silver money. Given the low
value of copper relative to silver, these plates were inconveniently
large, one type even weighing around 20 kilograms. Though no longer
commonly used, the Swedish words Plåt (plate) and Plåtar (plates) can
still denote money.[^80]

Given our notion of full-bodied and token coins above, we can define a
fiat coin by two characteristics. First, the coins have a negligible
intrinsic value compared to their legal value. They are, in other words,
just an extreme endpoint of the full range of token coins.[^81] Second,
they are not convertible to another type of full-bodied coin. The
English silver coins from the 1830s onwards were fiduciary tokens. While
their intrinsic value was low, they could be exchanged for full-bodied
gold coins. A fiat coin is, however, not convertible to a full-bodied
coin. More broadly, a fiat system is, then, a system where all the forms
of legal tender have low intrinsic value and can, at best, only be
exchanged for new fiat money, not full-bodied coins or other moneys with
a high intrinsic value.

A last feature of the pre-modern bimetallic systems that requires our
full appreciation is how many different coins—both local and
foreign—commonly circulated together in a local and regional economy.
A list that was presumably drawn up in the late thirteenth century
indicates that almost one hundred gold and silver coins already
circulated in France by that time.[^82] In 1577, a bailiff in Limburg
assumed his office with a required security deposit of 300 guilders by
using more than 210 coins of 19 different types from all over the Low
Countries and abroad.[^83] In eighteenth century Milan, supposedly a
total of 22 gold coins and 29 silver coins were in circulation, not even
counting the smaller copper-based coins.[^84] This diversity of coins in
circulation was also transplanted to Colonial America in the seventeenth
century, where it was complemented by a prolific issuance of colonial
paper money, particularly in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

While England had historically long-enjoyed fairly centralized control
over the coinage, monetary control over larger geographic regions only
increasingly became the norm elsewhere in Europe from the fourteenth
century onwards. In France, the monarchy had established a privileged
position over minting by the early fourteenth century.[^85] In the Low
Countries, the Burgundian rulers increasingly helped create uniformity
in monetary standards in the fifteenth century, particularly with
regards to the gold and larger silver coins.[^86] The unification of
Spain enabled substantial centralization in its coinage. Also since the
fourteenth century, actions with regards to the coinage were
increasingly wrested from the direct control of kings and subject to
approval from the nobility. In 1352, for example, at the start of the
Hundred Years War, Edward III sought to dispel concerns about debasement
by promising that the coinage could only be changed from then on by acts
of parliament. The kings of Aragon similarly became subjected to
parliamentary approval for any alterations to the coinage in 1372.[^87]

But while centralized control in combination with its unique geographic
position helped keep the number of foreign coins circulating in England
at bay, the multitude of coins continued in Continental Europe, even in
the face of the sometimes strong restrictions that were instituted from
the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries onwards. Only in the nineteenth
century did many states become capable of preventing the widescale
circulation of foreign coins.

## 6. The technology of pre-modern coins

Commodity coins in the pre-modern era were of very poor quality.

The primitive hammering technique entailed, already at the issue of any
particular type of coin, that the specimens of the same lot were not of
entirely equal shape and size and, most importantly, that their precious
metal content varied. Mints, therefore, allowed for a weight range of
newly minted coins. The official weight was only an approximate
average.[^88] Coins also underwent natural wear and tear in circulation,
which reduced their overall precious metal content. When new and older
coins jointly circulated, natural deterioration, therefore, further
exacerbated the differences within a type of coin.

The natural variation was made worse by intentional human interventions.
People attempted to extract bits of precious metal from coins and then
still pass them off for their legal value. The common techniques
included clipping, where small slivers were cut from the edge of the
coin using a knife or scissors; sweating, where coins were placed in a
bag or barrel, sometimes with abrasives, and shaken; and shaving, where
bits of metal were removed from the edge or surface with a file. In
addition, coins with a suspected high precious metal content were
sometimes culled and melted down into bullion.

Counterfeiting was also a common problem. Given the lack of uniformity
and low costs of the hammering technique, token coins were attractive to
counterfeiters. In mid-eighteenth century England, for example, about
half the copper token coins in circulation were estimated as
counterfeits.[^89] Making coins full-bodied was, in other words, a way
to discourage counterfeiting. However, counterfeiting also extended to
full-bodied coins. Low-fineness silver coins, even if full-bodied, were
particularly susceptible given the low accuracy from touchstone tests
and their low value in exchange. Billon coins were sometimes also
chemically processed to remove copper from the outer layer and make it
appear of higher quality. But even for full-bodied coins with a high
degree of fineness, particularly silver coins, counterfeiting was also
an issue.

Often counterfeits were actually produced by foreign mints.

The English sterling standard was one of the most notable fineness
standards for coinage that was fairly well maintained. It lasted into
the early twentieth century. No later than 1335, the silver purity
standard for pennies was set at 92.5%, the modern-day standard for the
alloy sterling silver, though a high standard certainly existed earlier
perhaps even before the Norman conquest of England (1066).[^90] By the
second half of the thirteenth century, this quality standard and
England's wool exports had helped make the sterling commonly accepted in
Britain, Ireland, and parts of northern Europe. It was also increasingly
becoming accepted in the Low Countries.[^91]

Flemish mints apparently started producing counterfeit English pennies
in the middle of the century when the balance of trade with England
became less favorable and English rent fiefs were halted, both of which
had brought in sterlings.[^92] Astoundingly, in the 1290s, it seems that
around 90 million counterfeit sterlings—commonly classified as
crockards or pollards—were minted in the Low Countries, about ten
times the amount of actual sterlings minted in England during that
decade.[^93]

In many cases, foreign rulers imitated coins with just enough
differences to circumvent the accusation of counterfeiting, but yet with
a decent chance of consumers accepting them at the same face value.
Another famous case was known as the 'war of the gold nobles', where
Philip the Bold, first Duke of Burgundy from the House of
Valois-Burgundy, struck imitations of the English noble between 1388 and
1402. The move came as a response to the Crown's regulation that the
Calais Staplers—an association of wool merchants—only accept the
English gold nobles for wool. The imitations had slightly less gold
content, but it seems that Flemish merchants were able to pass them off
for the same value as the English gold nobles.[^94]

Punishments to curb some of the problems facing the coinage were often
severe. In England, counterfeiters and clippers potentially faced the
death penalty until 1832.[^95] Catherine Murphy was the last English
woman to be burned at the stake in 1789 because she had created
counterfeit coins with her husband (who was hanged). In medieval France,
counterfeiters were apparently boiled alive.[^96] Yet, despite such
legal measures, the problems persisted. We may assume that the chances
of conviction of such crimes was low and the laws only applied
sporadically.

The negative effects of deteriorating and counterfeit coins can be
mitigated by a frequent renewal of the coinage (*renovatio monetae*).
Particularly in the High Middle Ages monetary systems with frequent
renewal were not uncommon in Europe. As already mentioned, such a system
existed in England, at least until Henry II's monetary reforms in the
twelfth century.[^97] Recoinage systems also dominated in northern,
eastern, and central Europe during the High Middle Ages.[^98] Success of
the system required taking the old coinage out of circulation. Fines and
imprisonment for using the old coins were common in recoinage systems.
On the Continent, the discontinued coin supposedly was sometimes also
branded onto an offender's face as a punishment.[^99] Any recoinage
typically had an inherent tax, which in some cases was substantial.

In much of Western Europe—northern Italy, France, Christian Spain, and
the Low Countries—long-lived coinage systems were already dominant in
the High Middle Ages. In these systems, coins would continue to
circulate indefinitely. In the twelfth century, England also switched to
such a system. By the fourteenth century, it was operating in much of
Europe, undoubtedly in part due to the inefficiencies of frequent
recoinages. These long-lived coinage systems generally operated on the
basis of the free coinage principle.

As already noted, members of the public, often money changers or
merchants, could choose to bring bullion or old coins into the mint and
in return would receive newly minted coins. It seems that rulers often
also pro-actively enticed them.[^100] Some of the newly produced coins,
however, were kept by the mint to cover the costs of minting and the
seigniorage. While this seigniorage was at times substantial, it was
generally quite modest. Redish, for example, finds that the total costs
of minting and seigniorage in England and France from 1360 to 1789 were,
notwithstanding occasional extreme interruptions, typically under 5% for
England and, slightly higher, but typically under 10% for France.[^101]
Seigniorage rates were modest because individuals could also often take
their bullion to the mint of a nearby ruler and high seigniorage rates
also encouraged counterfeiters.[^102]

While rulers often set policies that induced bringing in old coins for
reminting—such as the modest seigniorage rate—the deterioration of
the coinage, of course, had a much more substantial impact on long-lived
coinage systems. For the Late Middle Ages, the weight loss of the
English penny—through wear and tear, clipping, shaving, and so
on—has been estimated at around 0.2% to 0.3% per year.[^103] On the
basis of official statistical data on coin weight loss collected in the
nineteenth century as well as data about hoards from the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries, Velde concludes that weight decreases particularly
affected low value coins, sometimes even by a few tenths of a percentage
point per year.[^104]

Such small weight losses can add up over time. Due to a shortage of
silver in the eighteenth and early nineteenth century, most of the
English shillings circulating through England in 1798 were fifty to a
hundred years old. The mints had also ceased production of silver crowns
in 1751. An examination of the currency in 1789 found that the English
crowns and shillings in circulation were, respectively, 3.3% and 25%
lighter on average than at the moment of their issuance.[^105]

We should be weary to let our modern experiences underappreciate how
detrimental all the processes above could be to the coinage. According
to Hermen Bote, a fifteenth-century German chronicler, for instance, an
epidemic of culling and melting destroyed the entire coinage. Whenever a
merchant or burgher "had a good silver penny or a gulden of full weight
solely looked to his advantage, until finally the peasants, too, learned
this, so that no good penny, *groschen* or gulden would stay in
circulation: whenever one appeared it was withdrawn".[^106]

The challenges facing pre-modern coinage were solved only over a long
period of time. One important step was made in the sixteenth century
when a mechanized system of coin production was invented in northern
Italy. Rolling mills flattened out the flans instead of hammering.
Cutting presses, then, replaced shears to make the blanks. A screw press
finally stamped the coins. From northern Italy, the technique spread
throughout Europe, though often in the face of resistance from minters.
It was only permanently established in France and England, for example,
around the middle of the seventeenth century.[^107] The technology was
only adopted in the New World in the 1730s.[^108]

The technique increased the quality of coins. By contrast to the
hammered coins, the edges could now be made much more uniformly round.
Minters would also create ridges and inscriptions on coin edges. Overall
the coins had a much more uniform appearance. So the introduction of
milled coins largely addressed issues with clipping, shaving, and
sweating. Yet, certain problems persisted. As the technique was still
fairly low-cost and simple, it offered few incentives to stop
counterfeiters. In addition, coins still had a natural variation in
their metal content. Issues with counterfeiting were also a risk to bank
notes at the time, hence why Bank of England notes in the eighteenth
century were only of high values. It was thought that at least then
people would inspect them carefully. The remaining problems with coins,
particularly counterfeiting, would only be sufficiently addressed by
nineteenth century advances.[^109]

The technological limitations in pre-modern coinage are a key factor in
explaining why many pre-modern monetary systems were bimetallic or
sometimes trimetallic. Let's first consider the higher value coins .

The public generally believed that the value of coins was determined by
their intrinsic value and had little faith in tokens, particularly for
coins of higher value. The monometallic systems with fiduciary money
that arose in the nineteenth century—where notes and coins with low
intrinsic value represented an amount of gold or silver—were not
possible before the 1800s. Rulers were not sufficiently trusted to
guarantee convertibility. In addition, the technology to prevent
counterfeiting was simply not sufficiently sophisticated for such a
system to work. The key factor was that the costs to produce decent
counterfeits were simply not very high. Any fiat system—which easily
solves any issues with denominations—was not practical for the same
reason.[^110]

So, higher value coins had to be full-bodied. Yet, coins with lower
degrees of fineness were more difficult to assay. They were also more
difficult to identify as counterfeits through an experienced eye. On the
whole, coins of low-fineness were, thus, easier to counterfeit. They
were also more costly to melt down into bullion, particularly a
practical obstacle when reminting. And the different denominations
required by pre-modern economies after the commercial revolution were
far too large to cover only by variations in the size of full-bodied
high-grade silver coins. The same was true, all the more so, for gold
coins and copper coins.

The solution was systems with higher-value coins of a high degree of
fineness in both gold and silver. Gold coins were for international and
interregional trade as well as other very high value transactions. Finer
silver coins were then standardly used for larger local transactions
such as wages and rents. Having fine coins of both metals assured a wide
range of values and helped prevent counterfeiting. Yet, the bimetallic
systems also legally defined an inherent value ratio between silver and
gold which would cause substantial issues for stability given that
market rates between silver and gold bullion shifted. It also was at the
foundation of consistent depreciation in the coinage; that is, the
precious metal content of coins generally decreased over time relative
to their legal value.

Providing for small denominations in bimetallic systems was incredibly
complicated, hence, why Cipolla referred to their provision as the "big
problem of the petty coins". A key factor in these problems was the
relatively high production costs of petty coins. We turn to this subject
more elaborately in the next chapter. Suffice to say here that petty
coins were not often full-bodied coins of yet another metal. Most
commonly, they were full-bodied billon coins or token billon coins.
Sometimes they were also token copper coins. Hence, most pre-modern
monetary systems dealt with petty coins by other means than turning to a
trimetallic standard.
