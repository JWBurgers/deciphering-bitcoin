# Chapter 3: Beyond Bank Payments

Bank payments constitute a core payment method in developed economies. In the United States, bank payments are processed via three main systems: Fedwire, CHIPS, and ACH. Yet, they are hardly the only payment method used by consumers and businesses. In this chapter, we will turn our attention to these other payments methods.

We start in *Section 1* by showing that cash payments, bank payments, and physical card payments are the three key payment methods within developed economies. We have already discussed the first two payment methods, and will turn to physical card payments in *Sections 2*, *3*, and *4*. Our main focus is on understanding the processing of payments, the business models, and the operations of Visa and Mastercard, the leading card companies globally. The results generally illustrate the principles behind physical card payments. 

To complete our survey of the global landcape for payments, we will first briefly discuss some other common payment methods such as checks, digital wallets, and M-Pesa in *Section 5*. We conclude by making a common general distinction between push and pull payments in *Section 6*.  


## 1. Key payment methods

Within the United States, bank payments, physical payment cards, and cash dominate the payments landscape. According to the 2023 Survey and Diary of Consumer Payment Choice,<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> which annually explores the habits and preferences of US consumers with regards to payments, U.S. consumers, on average, made 45.6 payments per month in 2023. These include bill payments, online purchases, and purchases at physical points of sale. 

•	Around two-thirds of those 45.6 monthly payments involved physical debit and credit cards. Most of the remaining transactions consisted of cash payments and online bank payments.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup>  
•	In terms of value, close to 80% of those 45.6 monthly payments was accounted for by online bank payments and card payments. The remaining value was attributed to check payments (9%) and cash payments (6%).
•	While cash constituted a fairly small share of use in payments—both in terms of the number of transactions and in terms of the value of transactions—it is the most widely used payment instrument. More people in the survey reported using cash as one of their payment methods in the last 30 days than any of the other payment methods (87%).<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup>

In other developed economies than the United States, you will definitely see variety in habits and preferences with regards to payment methods.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup> That said, we can safely make a few broad generalizations about all of them. 

First, the empirical evidence suggests that the relative number and relative value of cash payments is generally declining.<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup> That said, cash is still an important payment instrument in most developed economies. Sweden, for example, is generally considered one of the most cashless societies on Earth. Yet, in 2023, the central bank’s survey on payment habits still showed that 10% of surveyees reported their last in-store purchase had been made in cash.<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> There was also an increase in the importance the surveyees attributed to cash, particularly as a backup for when cards and digital methods fail.<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup>

Second, the use of physical payment cards, particularly debit and credit cards, as well as online banks payments are integral to the payments landscape in developed economies generally, not just in the United States. Consider, for example, the findings of the 2022 Study on the Payment Attitudes of Consumers in the Euro Area—the members of the European Union that have adopted the euro.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup>

•	The almost forty thousand respondents completed their recurring payments—such as those to electric and phone companies—predominantly through online bank transfers.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup>
•	While many of their non-recurring transactions at physical points of sale were still made in cash (59%), physical payment cards were also used in over a third of them (34%). The total value of their card transactions was actually higher (46%) than the total value for cash (42%). In addition, more than half of online non-recurring payments in 2022 were made by payment cards.

Third, once you understand the basic architecture behind bank payments and physical payment cards, it is fairly straightforward to understand most other payment methods used in developed economies. To start, many payment solutions are really just wrappers—very useful ones to be sure—on the basic infrastructure for bank payments and payment cards. Venmo, Cashapp, PayPal, Google Wallet, virtual credit cards, Wise, iDEAL, Klarna, and a seemingly ever-increasing list of other digital payment solutions fairly accurately fit that description. But even payment solutions that are somewhat more complicated under the hood, like checks, are fairly easy to grasp with a solid understanding of bank payments and payment cards.

Outside of the developed economies, bank payments and payment cards are often much less important to payments. In some cases, we see really ingenious solutions to facilitating electronic payments in the absence of a developed banking infrastructure. In Kenya, for example, M-Pesa has become the predominant payment method in recent years, and the service has also spread to other parts of Africa and elsewhere. But even these types of solutions are fairly straightforward to understand once you grasp the basic design principles behind payments.   

These observations explain our approach in this chapter as set out in the introduction. We will now turn to the main players, processes, and systems involved in physical payment cards. 


## 2. Card payments

A common payment method for many people across the globe is the **credit card**. The small plastic or sometimes metal cards are swiped millions of times per day at the **payment terminals** of restaurants, hotels, and many other types of businesses. They are also used for cash advances at ATMs. In recent decades, the details on the card have become increasingly used for making payments online. 

Standardly, when a consumer or business makes a payment with a credit card, they are essentially extended a short-term, uncollatoralized loan. The loan is normally interest-free if it is repaid on time. To minimize risks, credit cards generally have spending limits. In some cases, the issuer of the credit card might require collatoral that acts as the spending limit on the card. Such credit cards we typically refer to as **prepaid credit cards**.    

Visa and Mastercard are the two most well-known **credit card companies**.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup> They handle the majority of the world’s credit card transactions. According to the Nilson Report (2023), Visa accounted for 39% of the global market and Mastercard for 24% of the global market in terms of the value of goods and services in 2021. Discounting the Chinese market, somewhere around 90% of the global market is in hands of Visa and Mastercard.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup>

We colloquially still refer to Visa and Mastercard as credit card companies. But that term is somewhat outdated. While much of their core business in indeed still to facilitate credit card transactions, Visa and Mastercard also process millions of transactions per day with other types of **payment cards**. These include the following:

* **Debit cards**. These types of cards are directly connected to a bank account from which the funds are withdrawn. They can be used at payment terminals or at ATMs. In some localities, the card details can also be utilized for online payments, just as with a credit card. 
* **Prepaid cards**. Also sometimes called **prepaid debit cards**, these types of cards are associated with a balance that has already been funded by the user. Prepaid cards can be reloaded. Prepaid (debit) cards should not be confused with prepaid credit cards.  
* **Gift cards**. These types of cards also have an associated balance that has already been funded. By contrast to prepaid cards, gift cards can typically not be reloaded. Many retailers offer gift cards that can only be utilized within their stores and, at best, a limited number of affiliates. But Visa and Mastercard giftcards are useable in many different businesses.  

Hence, instead of referring to Visa and Mastercard as credit card companies, it is more accurate to call them **payment card services companies**. 

At the core of the services offered by Visa and Mastercard is their **card networks**. The company sits at the center of the network and is, in architectural contexts, frequently referred to as the **card scheme operator**. The connecting members are predominantly commercial banks. Any other types of members are also strongly regulated financial institutions. Large **payment processors** such as Adyen, Mollie, PayPal, and Stripe, for example, are technically not considered banks. But they are usually members of both the Mastercard and Visa networks.   

In addition to restricting membership to strongly regulated financial institutions, Visa and Mastercard impose a host of further technical, procedural, and policy guidelines on their members. By restricting membership in this way, Visa and Mastercard minimize the risks in their systems and services.

Why would any financial institution want to become a member of the Visa or Mastercard network? Membership allows them to communicate, via the card scheme operator, with other members of the network for the processing of payment card transactions in various types of settings. We, therefore, commonly refer to these members of a card network as **card processors**. Acting as a card processor for the Visa or Mastercard network can be financially lucrative for a financial institution. 

Credit cards as well as other types of payment cards are held by consumers, businesses, and organizations. They are commonly referred to as **card holders**. Merchants, in turn, accept these cards for payments, via one or more **point of sale channels**. These are predominantly physical locations, web shops, and mobile applications. 

Visa and Mastercard do not issue cards to card holders directly. They also do not deal directly with merchants. Those parts of the business are handled by partners, respectively, called **issuers** and **acquirers**. Due to this architectural feature, we often refer to Visa and Mastercard as **open card networks**. 

You will commonly hear the aforementioned issuers and acquirers referred to as **issuing banks** and **acquiring banks**. That is because they are frequently, indeed, commercial banks. They can, however, also be other types of entities. Airlines and large department stores, for example, also commonly issue credit cards. As an example on the acquisition side, as we will explain in the next section, many businesses accept card payments nowadays by utilizing a payment processor offered via **e-commerce platforms** rather than banks. In these cases, we probably want to say that the e-commerce platform and the payment processor jointly acquire the merchant.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup>

Many of the members in the Visa and Mastercard networks will function as both issuers and acquirers. But not all of them. Some of them are only issuers, some only acquirers, and some are neither. In addition, many issuers and acquirers in the Visa and Mastercard ecosystems are not actually direct members of the card network at all. Instead, they rely on one or more members for the purposes of processing. 

Suppose, for example, that a local bank in a small town wants to offer the ability to accept credit cards and debit cards to their brick-and-mortar merchants. They want to include in the package access to the two major card networks, Visa and Mastercard. But they would also like to process American Express and Discover card transactions. 

The technical and operational requirements for joining these networks are very burdensome. So, instead of joining themselves, it is probably more lucrative for this local bank just to handle the relationship with the merchants and establish a partnership with a larger player that can act as the card processer for all these cards. The idea here is not all that different from smaller banks that rely on bigger banks for access to services like FedWire or CHIPS. Many issuers and acquirers in the Visa and Mastercard networks outsource their processing in this fashion. Sometimes an issuer or an acquirer may even work with multiple processors. 

Within the Visa and Mastercard networks, the processing of all types of card transactions can be separated into two main tasks: (1) **authorization** and (2) **clearing and settlement**. While the details of how these tasks are executed will vary somewhat between the two companies, between the types of transactions, between localities, and so on, we will ignore those details and just boil the two tasks down to their essentials.  

First, authorization is merely the establishment of an approval or disapproval of a transaction by the issuer. Details of a transaction are routed at a point of sale to the in-house facilities or a third-party card processor utilized by the acquirer. This information is, in turn, routed to Visa or Mastercard’s infrastructure. This message is, then, forwarded to the issuer’s in-house processing facilities or to it’s third-party processor. Various checks are conducted along the way. 

At that point the issuer or a third-party processor on its behalf will, then, perform further various checks, including whether the card holder has sufficient funds or credit for the transaction. An approval or disapproval message is, then, returned through the network to the point of sale. While many steps were involved, all of this happened within a few seconds. 

The clearance and settlement will take longer. For credit cards, the merchant will typically experience that indirectly because it can take several days before the amount from a credit card transaction is credited to their business account. Some services, however, do offer near instant payouts on credit and debit card transactions, even though transactions are not yet settled in the background. 

Card scheme operators have a major role in the clearance and settlement process. When any new member joins the Visa or Mastercard network, they are required to open one or more **settlement accounts**. These card services companies work with various banks for ensuring settlement in geographic regions like the United States. Which bank(s) any member should open an account at depends on the currencies in which they want to settle and various other factors. 

Just as deferred net settlement systems like ACH, card scheme operators like Mastercard and Visa will tally the credits and debits over their members periodically. Members that owe money to the rest of the network will, then, transfer that money to a clearing account owned by the card scheme operator. The operator will make payouts from a clearing account to those members who are owed for their activities over the period.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup>

Depending on various factors, including geographic location, payouts to a clearing account by debtors over a certain period can be made in various ways. In the United States, member payouts to a clearing account for Mastercard are typically made automatically via Fedwire, though ACH is also used in certain circumstances. Though we were not able to confirm this fact, we would expect Visa to operate in the same manner. 

Besides Mastercard and Visa, there is one other really major payment card service provider on the global market: UnionPay. This is a Chinese company founded only in 2002. It has been making significant global inroads in recent years. The rest of the global market for payment card services is handled by many smaller companies. Some such as American Express, Discover, Diners Club International, and the Japan Credit Bureau also operate on a global level. Others operate more on a domestic or regional level such as RuPay (India), EFTPOS (Australia and New Zealand), Interac (Canada), and Elo (Brazil). Most countries seem to have domestic or regional card schemes in place.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup>

The basic flow of card payments—consisting of (1) authorization and (2) clearance and settlement—is likely similar in most of these ecosystems. But it is beyond our scope to investigate all those arrangements here. 

Do be aware that there will be, in any case, variety within the details of these ecosystems. By contrast to Visa and Mastercard, for example, American express also directly issues cards directly on-boards merchants. For another marked contrast, American Express offers bank deposit services to consumers and businesses. Mastercard and Visa do not have such in-house facilities, and always rely on third-parties such as CitiBank and Bank of America for holding customer funds. 


## 3. Accepting card payments 

While we have elaborately discussed the players and processes in the payment card system, we have not really offered any light on the practical details of accepting card payments. How exactly can a business accept card payments both online and at physical locations? What are the practical steps they should take?

While there surely isn’t one approach, it is at least very common to leverage **e-commerce solutions** for accepting card payments online. These are technology solutions that can help you create product pages, track inventory, process payments, set up shipping options, and handle all other critical aspects of managing an **e-commerce business**—any business that operates over the Internet, either via a website, an application, or both. 

A well-known e-commerce solution is WordPress in combination with Woocommerce. Wordpress is commonly known as **content management system**. The latter is a software package for creating, adapating, and managing fairly sophisticated websites and applications. At its core, WordPress is focused on the content of your website or application: the style and layout; the creation and storage of content; managing users; and so on. Through many available **plugins**, the features and functionalities achievable with WordPress are extended. Woocommerce is precisely such a plugin, one that specifically allows for the creation and management of stores.

Another popular e-commerce solution is Drupal Commerce. It is an additional package that can be installed with Drupal Core. Just as WordPress, Drupal Core is a basic content management system. But while WordPress and any extensions can be used in a no-code or low-code fashion, Drupal Core and extensions require much more technical know-how.  

Further popular e-commerce solutions are Shopify and Magento. By contrast to Woocommerce and Drupal Commerce, Shopify and Magento can be utilized as standalone platforms to create your entire website or application. They, in other words, have fairly advanced content management capabilities. You can, however, also use Shopify and Magento with a content management system like WordPress and Drupal. 

There are numerous other e-commerce solutions in the market besides the ones just mentioned, including BigCommerce, Wix, Squarespace, and OpenCart. All of these have their advantages and disadvantages. In any case, we can safely say there is no shortage of e-commerce solutions, content management systems, and other technologies to create sophisticated e-commerce businesses. Even people with little technical know-how can get an e-commerce business up and running with these tools fairly quickly.  

Credit cards are very commonly used in e-commerce transactions. To a lesser extent, debit cards, prepaid cards, and gift cards are used. One key step to launching an e-commerce business with such a solution is to select one or more payment gateways to facilitate card transactions as well as other types of payments. 

A **payment gateway** is a really a back-end service that collects all the information about a payment and performs various checks on it. In the context of payment cards, payment gateways perform a similar function to the payment terminals at physical locations. A payment gateway, then, forwards the information about any payment to a card processor which communicates through the card network—commonly Visa or Mastercard—with the card processor for the issuer or the issuer’s in-house facilities. 

E-commerce solutions typically offer a large array of payment gateways. Within Woocommerce, for instance, you can select Stripe, PayPal, Square, Adyen, Mollie, Woocommerce Payments (a native solution), and many other gateways for processing your card payments. The exact list of options will depend on the e-commerce solution and in large part on where you live. You can even choose different options for different types of card payments. These payment gateways will normally have their own in-house card processing facilities, though sometimes they may rely on third-parties. 

Setting up these payment gateways in an e-commerce solution is fairly straightforward. You really just need to open an account with the payment gateway, couple the account to a business account, and obtain API credentials. The API credentials for each gateway can, then, be configured within your e-commerce solution, so that your online store can securely send payment information to the gateway. 

Many of the card processors within e-commerce solutions are refered to more generically as **payment processors**. That is because they normally offer processing facilities for other types of payments as well. They are not banks. But instead offer merchant accounts that temporarily hold funds from payment cards and other payment methods. These funds are periodically payed out to businesses accounts at commercial banks. Stripe, PayPal, Square, Adyen, and Mollie are some of the major companies that operate in this manner. 

Commercial banks are not entirely absent from the e-commerce space. Paymentech, for example, is a payment gateway and processing service offered by JP Morgan Chase. The Bank of America, the National Australia Bank, and Westpac are some other examples of commercial banks that offer payment gateways within these e-commerce solutions. In addition, e-commerce businesses can also create their own gateways and connect these to the payment processing services of banks directly. That all said, much of the payment processing infrastructure is offered by third parties. 

While they are called “e-commerce solutions”, they typically also offer options for accepting payment cards and other payment methods in physical locations. Payment terminals are still a common option for cards. Traditionally these worked by swiping a card’s magnetic strip, but more commonly we employ them with a chip and pin on the card or even in a contactless manner. Payment terminals are often also integrated physically into a general point of sale systems, vending machines, and other equipment. Another popular option for accepting card payments are **mobile card readers** connected to dedicated tablets or a smart phone. We are also increasingly seeing **tap on phone solutions** for processing payment cards. 


## 4. Haddock’s fish and chips

Even foregoing many of the details, the discussion of the processing of payment card transactions in the previous sections contained a lot of information. In addition, we should also understand how money is made within the ecosystem. Let’s turn to an example for these final matters on payment cards. 

Suppose that Joe, a car salesman from Houston, is on a short trip to Boston. He is looking for some lunch and comes across a fish and chips shop called Haddock’s. As Joe doesn’t see too many of those in Houston, he decides to give it a try. Joe intends to pay with his credit credit card issued by Capital One. It is a standard Mastercard that has a limit of $1,000. Within this scenario, Capital One is, therefore, the *issuer* (or *issuing bank*). Mastercard, of course, is a payment card services company that acts as the *card scheme operator* for the credit card. Let’s assume for the example that Capital One is directly connected to the Mastercard network, so operates as a *card processor*. 

Haddock’s, in turn, has both a physical and an online presence. Specifically, they have four physical locations in the Boston area as well as a website that handles deliveries. The deliveries placed on the website are passed through to one of the physical locations on the basis of an algorithm that considers physical distance and workload. Both physically and virtually, Haddock’s can accept credit cards for payments.

While Haddock’s has a main *business account* with Clinton Savings Bank, it does not rely on them for accepting credit cards. Instead, Haddock’s has a website that was built with Wordpress and Woocommerce. For credit cards, Haddock’s selected Stripe to use as a payment gateway and for processing. From Stripe, they received a number of payment terminals to handle payments at their physical locations. All payments come into their Stripe *merchant account*, which periodically makes payouts to their main business account at Clinton Savings Bank. 

It is a little debatable who we actually want to call the acquirer in this scenario. But typically, Stripe would be considered the acquirer as they handle all the main services. 

Suppose now that John’s bill for his lunch is $15. With a Massachusets sales tax of 6.25%, John’s total comes to $15.94, which he will pay for using his Mastercard. The existent architecture must now perform two main tasks: (1) *authorization* and (2) *clearance and settlement*. 

Authorization in our example requires that Capital One is notified of the transaction and sends an approval or disapproval message back. In our particular example, the relevant information about the transaction is assembled at the payment terminal and then routed via Stripe’s card processing facilities and then via Mastercard’s infrastructure to the Mastercard processing infrastructure of Capital One. Various checks are conducted along the way. Capital One will also conduct numerous checks, including whether John has sufficient credit available for the transaction. 

Supposing that John has spent only $200 of his $1,000 limit, Capital One will route a notification of approval to the payment terminal via Mastercard and Stripe. The cashier at the Old Sailor sees the approved message on the payment terminal and thanks John for his business. While many steps were involved, all of this happened within a few seconds. 

The clearance and settlement process will take longer. Typically a merchant like the Old Sailor will experience that indirectly because it can often take one day or longer before the amount from the transaction is credited to their merchant account. That said, Stripe and other payment processors do offer near instant payouts on credit cards in certain circumstances, even though transactions are not yet settled in the background. 

Now when Capital One joined the Mastercard network, it was required to open one or more *settlement accounts*. The exact list of settlement accounts would have depended on the currencies in which Capital One wanted to settle and various other factors. Suppose that, in this fasion, Capital One was instructed to open a Mastercard settlement account denominated in dollars with CitiBank. The same would have been instructed to Stripe for their settlement in dollars. 

John’s payment is, then, included in one of the periodic tallies of the debits and credits of all the members in the Mastercard network. Let’s assume that Capital One one had a net debit position. So it transferred that balance to a clearing account owned by Mastercard. Let’s also assume that Stripe had a net debit position, so that it also makes a payout to a *clearing account* owned by Mastercard. The effect of John’s payment to Haddock’s would have, thus, essentially reduced the net debit position for Stripe during that particular period. 

While there are different types of arrangements possible, it is most likely that the transfers of Capital One and Stripe happened automatically at the order of Mastercard via FedWire. From their clearing accounts, Mastercard makes payments to those members who had a net credit position over the tally period. 

Note that John’s payment could have also been included in a tally where either Capital One or Stripe or both would have held a net credit position. Supposing, for example, that Stripe had held a net credit position. In that case, Stripe would obviously not have transferred any money to a Mastercard’s clearing account for the period. Instead, they would have just received a payout to their settlement account from a Mastercard clearing account. That payment would typically also have been made via FedWire. 

At some point during or after this process, Haddock’s will see the John’s payment added to the balance of their merchant account. Though it is not exactly $15.94. Instead, it will probably be around 20 to 40 cents less. The difference is known as the **merchant discount rate** or **merchant discount**. It typically ranges between one and a few percent of the total amount, as the result of a fixed fee and a percentage fee on credit card transactions. Some small portion of the merchant discount would likely have been withheld by Stripe. Another small portion likely went to Mastercard. But most of that money would have gone to the issuer of the credit card, in this case Capital One, via what is called the **interchange fee**. 

Once the funds from the transaction are in their merchant account, Haddock’s can withdraw the funds to their business account at Clinton Savings Bank. Typically that withdrawal happens as part of a larger payout and automatically. John, in turn, will have to pay Capital One for all the funds he borrowed via his credit card over some period, typically a month, at a later period. That will include his purchase of fish and chips at Haddock’s. Often paying off a credit card debt happens automatically. In this case, it is plausible that John would have a checking account at Capital One from which his credit card payments are automatically deducted each month. 


## 5. Other payment methods

The world of non-cash payment payment methods is a vast and varied one. Besides bank payments and card payments, there are a host of other payment methods. Let’s briefly review some other well-known instruments here.

A **check** is traditionally a small slip of paper issued by a specific bank to one of their customers. The customer can use the check to make payments by filling out a specific sum of money, a recipient, and other details on the check, and then handing or sending it to the recipient. Recipients of checks can deposit them with their own bank, which will then process the payment with the issuing bank. Checks are really special types of bills of exchanges where the drawee, the drawer’s bank, automatically accepts the bill of exchange as long as it meets certain conditions (primarily sufficient account balance). 

To offer the recipient additional guarantees, there are variations on the standard check. In the United States, common examples are **certified checks**—via which the issuing bank guarantees availability of funds in the payer’s account—and **cashier’s checks**—where the issuing bank withdraws the funds from the payer’s account and assumes obligation for honoring the payment. Such variations on checks also exist in other countries.

In recent years, there have been efforts to create digital systems that (partially) replace paper checks. Many banks in the United States, for example, offer customers the ability to write out digital checks and send these to others. 

Nevertheless, the usage of checks as a payment instrument in the developed world has generally been in a substantial decline due to the rise of other cashless payment instruments such as payment cards. This is so both in the number of transactions that use checks as well as the total value of those transactions. In some countries, such as the Netherlands, Namibia, and South Africa, they have been eliminated entirely. That said, checks still enjoy substantial usage in various countries such as the United States, Canada, and France.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup>

In the United States, clearing and settlement of checks on an interbank level is much more fragmented than the processing of electronic funds transfers. The Federal Reserve System offers paper and electronic check clearing facilities that are typically settled through the National Settlement Service. Many checks are also cleared through local systems or through correspondent banking arrangements; sometimes these might be settled through the Federal Reserve, but often this also occurs through correspondent banking arrangements.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup> In other countries, our impression is that clearing and settlement services for checks are typically less fragmented. They are offered by both central banks and private companies and typically take place on a multilateral net settlement basis. 

A **money order** is slightly different than a check. Any money order has an issuer, which can be a bank, credit union, or some other entity like the US Postal Service, Moneygram, or Western Union. When someone purchases a money order at some authorized location, the money immediately transfers to the issuer. A recipient can, then, cash out the money order or deposit the funds into a bank account. The issuer of the money order is responsible for providing the funds.

## 6. Push and pull payments 

In the payments landscape, a distinction is commonly made between push payments and pull payments. The distinction is employed very inconsistently in practice. But we will make it here in a broad way, so as to serve as a useful categorization. 

We will characterize a **push payment** is one in which the transfer of money from a payer to a payee is entered into the payment system by the payer. A **pull payment** we will, in turn, characterize as a transfer of money from a payer to a payee, which is initiated by the payee into the payment system.   

A cash payment is clearly an example of a push payment. If you send your nephew $20 for his birthday via your online banking portal, by placing an order at your local branch, or through a telephone call to the bank’s customer service department, you are also clearly making a push payment. The same is true for any standing order you set up with your bank—that is, any regular payment of a set amount of money towards a payee. 

A long-established payment method is the use of a **pre-funded account** or **tab** such as at a grocery store, gas station, or bar. Suppose that someone pays an advance of $200 at their local grocery store every month. Each time they visit the shop, the storekeeper will just deduct the due amount from their balance. This arrangement might be particularly handy, for example, if customers have kids that also do their shopping for them. The payment made after each visit is really a pull payment. 

The paradigmatic modern-day example of a pull payment is a payment where money is automatically deducted from a bank account by a utility company or other type of service provider in order to settle a bill. By signing a certain type of contractual agreement with a service provider such as a phone or electric company, you can authorize them to deduct bills periodically from your bank account without your approval. This type of contractual arrangement is typically known as a direct debit arrangement. 

Card payments should standardly also be considered pull payments. Typically these are made via a physical terminal or some online payment portal and introduced by the payee into the payment system. A personal check is probably best classified as a pull payment. This may seem a bit odd, as the payer has to write out details on the check and hand it to the payee. But the initiation of the transfer of funds is really only started when the payee sends the check to their bank. So the payee in this case has significant control in the initiation of the payment.  

Note that the distinction between credit transfers and debit transfers is sometimes used in the same way as the distinction between push and pull transfers. Given our characterizations for push and pull payments, however, that is awkward. Instead, we might better see them as follows:

* Credit transfers are any push payments made between bank accounts. That would exclude cash payments or any payments that involve cash at one end, such as the purchase of a cashier’s check with cash. 
* Debit transfers are any pull payments made between bank accounts. The paradigmatic examples are direct debit arrangements and personal checks.
