# Chapter 3: Europe's Economic Rise

Around the turn of the millennium, Europe enjoyed somewhat more order
and stability. It is from here on out that Europe begins a journey of
economic ascendancy, one characterized by an expansion of trade,
advances in financial and business practices, population growth,
increasing urbanization, and the introduction of new technologies. While
the economic and technological progress experienced fluctuations and
interruptions, these trends continued until the end of the Middle Ages.

In the early modern period, Europe then really began to diverge
economically and technologically from much of the rest of the world, a
gap that widened dramatically during the **Industrial Revolution**.
While Great Britain began to fully industrialize in the second half of
the eighteenth century, for many other areas of Europe that process
largely transpired in the nineteenth century. Some European settler
societies—such as the United States, Canada, and
Australia—experienced similar transformations. Japan was the only main
society outside the European sphere of influence that industrialized
before the Second World War, namely in the late nineteenth and early
twentieth century.

Of course, prosperity has not grown evenly and in the same way
throughout Europe over the last millennium. In the early modern period,
the Netherlands—or the Dutch Republic at the time—was Europe's
economic engine with a substantial reliance on international trade and
financial services. This was probably the main reason why it experienced
industrialization later than countries such as England, Germany, and
Belgium, and never to the same extent. Yet, the Netherlands maintained
high levels of prosperity and is today still one of Europe's wealthiest
countries. Southern Italy remained agricultural during the nineteenth
and much of the twentieth century, and only experienced
industrialization after the Second World War. It remains substantially
less prosperous than the north of Italy. Ireland also remained
economically underdeveloped and agricultural until well-into the
twentieth century. Today, the Celtic Tiger is one of Europe's most
prosperous nations.

Eastern Europe was economically much less developed than Western and
Central Europe by the end of the early modern period. In 1917, the
Russian Empire fell in the February Revolution and the Bolshevik
Party—the main communist party in Russia under the leadership of
Vladimir Lenin (1870–1924)—seized power in the October Revolution.
While many regions in the former Russian Empire sought independence such
as Ukraine, Belarus, Georgia, and Armenia, many were reconquered by the
Bolsheviks and included into the Soviet Union, formed in 1922. While the
areas within the Soviet Union had experienced some industrialization
earlier, it is mainly after the formation of the Soviet Union that
extensive industrialization began. Then, after the Second World War, the
Soviet Union's occupation of much of Eastern Europe brought Communist
parties to power and many of these Soviet satellites also saw
substantial industrialization. While industrialization under Communist
rule led to economic growth early on, however, the Soviet Union and its
satellites eventually stunted in their economic development. They were
far behind the rest of Europe at the time of the Fall of the Berlin Wall
(1989).

Hence, the divergence of Europe and some of its settler societies from
much of the rest of the world during the early modern period and the era
of industrialization was hardly an entirely homogeneous and even
process. In recent years, some of the European laggards have seen
remarkable progress in their economic development and levels of
prosperity, including Ireland, Poland, the Czech Republic, and the
Baltic States. While Japan industrialized early on, it only achieved
levels of prosperity comparable to wealthier European countries like
England and the Netherlands in the decades after the Second World War.
Outside of Japan and these European cases, a few other countries and
regions have attained the high levels of prosperity seen in Western and
Central Europe after the Second World War, including Israel, Singapore,
Hong Kong, Taiwan, and South Korea—primarily through rapid
industrialization and development—as well as Brunei, Qatar, and the
United Arab Emirates—whose wealth rests much more on natural resource
extraction.

Our purpose in this chapter is to describe the progression and evolution
of the economic rise of Europe since the turn of the first millennium as
well as some of its settler societies such as Australia, Canada, and the
United States much later. We also explore the causes of the economic and
technological ascendancy of Europe and these settler societies. In
*Section 1*, we turn to Europe's economic expansion during the High and
Late Middle Ages, while *Section 2* explores the early modern and modern
periods. In *Section 3*, we briefly explore Adam Smith's ideas on
economic growth and their influence on his classical and neoclassical
successors. *Section 4* concludes by exploring one of the key drivers
behind the economic rise and divergence of Europe and some of its
offshoots from much of the rest of the world: it's distinctive political
and legal institutions.

## 1. Economic growth in the Middle Ages

The key era of European economic development during the Middle Ages was
probably the late twelfth to the early fourteenth century—also known
as the **long thirteenth century**—after which economic expansion was
interrupted by wars, famines, and epidemics, including notably the Black
Death (1347–1351) and the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) between
England, France, and their allies. While traditionally most of the Late
Middle Ages has been seen as one of abject depression by historians, it
now appears the interruptions to economic progress were not nearly as
severe and long-lasting as originally envisioned.[^1] In fact, though
certainly a grim idea, the decimation of the European population in the
fourteenth century in some cases had a positive impact on long-term
economic growth, particularly in certain parts of Western Europe.

Data on population growth and urbanization can give some sense of the
developments in Europe during the High and Late Middle Ages. A modern
degree of accuracy for such data is beyond our reach. But, indicatively,
it has been estimated that, from 1000 to 1300, the population of Europe
roughly doubled. While around 35 to 40 cities had a population of 20,000
or more in the year 1000, this was probably closer to 100 in the year
1300. While likely only five cities had more than 50,000 inhabitants at
the outset of the new millennium—the main ones being the Islamic
Córdoba and the Byzantine Constantinople—this seems to have tripled by
1300.[^2]

Paris had become a bustling metropolis with an estimated 160,000
inhabitants at the end of the High Middle Ages. Close behind was Islamic
Granada. The Iberian Peninsula was largely Christianized at the end of
the High Middle Ages, including Córdoba, and Al Andalus was confined
within the southeastern corner to the Emirate of Granada. The
populations in Córdoba and Constantinople had decreased significantly by
1300. Italy experienced substantial urban growth during the High Middle
Ages. Venice, Milan, and Genoa, each had around 100,00 inhabitants by
1300.[^3] Other sizeable cities were Florence, Bologna, Rome, Naples,
and Palermo. Elsewhere in Europe, the populations have been estimated to
be around 50,000 for Ghent; 35,000 for Bruges; 35,000 to 45,000 for
London; and 40,000 for Cologne.[^4]

The Black Death was caused by the plague, an infectious bacterial
disease caused by *Yersinia pestis*. The Black Death and other plague
epidemics decimated the European population in the fourteenth century.
Population was also under pressure from various wars, including the
Hundred Years' War. Many towns and regions lost over a quarter of their
population or more to the Black Death alone. Nevertheless, by 1500, the
European population seems to have largely recovered and was somewhere
around seventy million.[^5] The number of really large cities had
increased substantially. Whereas about twelve cities had 50,000 or more
inhabitants in 1300, this had increased to around twenty-one by
1500.[^6]

Bairoch's authoritative study on urbanization for the period only
concludes a fractional increase in the urbanization of Europe from the
turn of the millennium to the end of the Middle Ages, namely an increase
from 9.7% to 10.7%. This is a percentage based on the population of
towns over 5,000 compared to the total population. Besides issues with
data accuracy and the determination of the best threshold, the threshold
approach is at best a rough approximation.[^7] Data from the HYDE
project, by contrast, clearly show increased urbanization in many areas
of Europe. Using modern geographic boundaries as their units of
analysis, the project's data show that, from the turn of the millennium
to the end of the Middle Ages, the urbanization rate roughly doubled in
France and Italy, and more than tripled in Germany. While Poland was
universally rural at the start of the period, it had urban pockets such
as Kraków, Gdańsk, and Poznań by the end the end of it. The Netherlands
and Belgium went from universally rural societies to already fairly
urban ones.[^8]

From descriptive studies of the period, one is also strongly tempted to
conclude that Europe had a more urban character going into the early
modern period, even though some areas such as England and Spain would
have stagnated in their urban development. England, in particular, was a
rural backwater by the start of the early modern period. The most urban
regions around 1300 were northern Italy and the Low Countries,
particularly the southern Low Countries with cities such as Ghent and
Brugge.[^9] By the early sixteenth century, the most urbanized region of
the continent was probably Holland, where it is estimated that almost
fifty percent of the population lived in areas with an urban
character.[^10]

For a long time, many economic historians had a sceptical view of
pre-modern economic growth. It was thought that productivity
improvements were largely absorbed by subsequent increases in the
population. Per capita GDP growth and living standards were, therefore,
thought to have generally increased very little, a phenomenon known as
the **Malthusian trap**, named after the originator of the idea, the
economist and scholar Thomas Malthus (1766–1834).[^11]

In recent decades, however, many economic historians have become more
optimistic and believe that pre-industrial societies also experienced
sustained economic growth, just more moderately. While making estimates
of per capita economic growth rates and overall growth rates before the
nineteenth century are extremely difficult, it is now common practice to
attempt such quantifications. Below we offer some of the findings.
Please note that any mentions of growth rates and per capita growth
rates below are adjusted for inflation. They are, therefore, **real
growth rates**, not **nominal growth rates**.

In Europe, the main candidates for sustained moderate growth before the
Middle Ages are Archaic and Classical Greece; the Western Roman Empire
during the Late Republican and Early Imperial periods; and the Eastern
Roman Empire from the Late Republican period until the fifth or sixth
century.

With regards to Ancient Greece, Morris suggests that per capita
consumption of the Greeks in 300 BC was about 50 to 100 percent greater
than in 800 BC, a growth rate of 0.14 to 0.26 percent per year. This
period experienced substantial population growth, so he estimates the
overall growth rate of the economy around 1.1% to 1.5% per year. While
some of that growth was recovery from the Bronze Age Collapse and the
Greek Dark Ages (c. 1200 BC–c. 800 BC), Classical Greece was certainly
more prosperous than its neighbors. Given the strong beliefs in the
equality of free-born men and Greece's democratic political
institutions, this prosperity was accompanied by a much more egalitarian
pattern of consumption than in other Mediterranean societies.[^12]

With regards to Ancient Rome, Silver conservatively and only
suggestively estimates Rome's per capita consumption growth at 0.13
percent annually from 100 BC to 200 AD.[^13] Political instability,
tribal invasions, inflation, and other factors, then ushered in an
economic decline for the Western Roman Empire into the Early Middle
Ages—with admittedly a partial recovery in the fourth century. In
Byzantium, growth seems to have continued longer, the economy probably
peaking in the era of Justinian I.[^14]

According to Maddisson, Western Europe and Eastern Europe were, then, on
average, economically stagnant in the first millennium. However, both
regions probably experienced moderate overall economic growth between
1000 and 1500: an estimated 0.29 percent on average annually for Western
Europe and 0.19 percent on average annually for Eastern Europe.[^15] The
per capita growth rate for Western Europe is, then, estimated at 0.13
percent annually. This is exactly equal to the estimate for the Late
Republican and early Imperial period given by Silver above and in the
vicinity of the estimate by Morris for Archaic and Classical Greece.
These figures are, of course, quite speculative, but they give a sense
of the economic development of Western Europe during the High and Late
Middle Ages. Eastern Europe lagged behind in per capita growth with only
an average 0.04 percent increase annually from 1000 to 1500.[^16]

The main areas of per capita GDP growth in the High and Late Middle
Areas were probably northern Italy and the Low Countries.

From 1300 until the second half of the sixteenth century, the GDP per
capita in northern and central Italy was undoubtedly high compared to
much of the rest of Europe other than the Low Countries. According to
Malanima,[^17] the purchasing power of the average northern-central
Italian in the period hovered roughly around the purchasing power of
\$1,600 in the United States in 1990.[^18] The region probably
experienced reasonable growth in per capita GDP, particularly from the
late twelfth century onwards, to produce those elevated levels by the
Late Middle Ages.[^19] After the Black Death, northern and central Italy
still show a very moderate increase in per capita GDP from 1360 to 1430,
after which there is a long and slow decline until 1600.

In the Late Middle Ages, it seems that mainly Holland and Flanders had
sustained per capita GDP growth.[^20] Flanders was the core economic
area of northwest Europe in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The
key industry was the textile industry, which relied on wool from
England. Flemish textiles were strongly desired by the northern Italian
Merchants and the key commodity at the famous Fairs of Champagne, which
were the center of international trade from the late twelfth until the
late thirteenth century. By the end of the Middle Ages, Flanders had
prosperity levels similar to those of northern Italy.[^21]

Though data for Flanders is lacking in the Late Middle Ages, Van Zanden
and Van Leeuwen have managed to construct a time series of the economic
growth of Holland from 1347 to 1807.[^22] Throughout the period Holland
experienced sustained, though volatile growth in GDP per capita, at an
annual average of 0.19 percent. In the Late Middle Ages, Holland shows a
jump in per capita GDP in the decades after the Black Death, which
probably is attributable to its developed labor and factor markets at
the time. While there are also some periods of stagnation and possibly
some decline, the underlying trend in per capita GDP was upwards until
the end of the Middle Ages. This was hardly due to population decline,
as Holland's population had already recovered from the Black Death by
the middle of the fifteenth century. Instead, in the 150 years before
the end of the Middle Ages, Holland's economy moved away from
agricultural activities and the region rapidly urbanized. According to
Van Zanden and Van Leeuwen, the shift away from low productivity
agricultural work to industries such as textiles, brewing, shipping, and
peat digging, as well as moderate technological progress explain
Holland's growth in GDP per capita during this period.[^23]

## 2. Economic growth into the modern period

European economic growth seems to have accelerated somewhat during the
early modern period. According to Maddisson, Western Europe grew at an
average annual rate of 0.40 percent, while Eastern Europe grew at an
average annual rate of 0.41 percent (compared to 0.29 percent and 0.19
percent, respectively, during the High and Late Middle Ages).[^24] Per
capita GDP growth continued at roughly the same pace in Western
Europe—namely, at an average of 0.14 percent annually (instead of 0.13
percent during the High and Late Middle Ages)—and also picked up in
eastern Europe at a pace of 0.10 percent annually on average (compared
to 0.04 percent during the High and Late Middle Ages).[^25]

The main regions of per capita GDP growth in this period were surely the
Dutch Republic and England (or the Kingdom of Great Britain after 1707).
Maddisson measures the average annual per capita growth rates for these
regions from 1500 to 1820 as 0.28 percent and 0.27 percent
respectively.[^26] While northern Italy was the economic center of
gravity at the outset of the sixteenth century, it shifted towards
northwestern Europe as the early modern period progressed, particularly
to England (Great Britain after 1707) and the region that would become
the Dutch Republic in 1588. We will briefly turn to the historical
context in which this economic growth occurred.

Middle Francia, which included Holland and Flanders, had quickly
disintegrated after the Treaty of Verdun. During the High Middle Ages,
then, the Low Countries comprised a patchwork of duchies, counties, and
other semi-independent territorial entities, each with their own
institutions, laws, and traditions. At the end of the fourteenth
century, however, a large part of the region came under the control of
the House of Burgundy—a cadet branch of the French royal House of
Valois. Over time, the Burgundian State—the name for the Duchy of
Burgundy and the other territories under its control—was able to
expand its sphere of control over the Low Countries through conquest,
land purchases, diplomacy, and inheritance. The area collectively became
known as the Burgundian Netherlands. While the territories remained
semi-independent, the Burgundians did increase their control over them
with time, for instance, with regards to the coinage.[^27]

Burgundian control of the Low Countries continued until 1482. After the
death of Charles the Bold at the Battle of Nancy (1477), Louis XI
(1423–1483) brought the Duchy of Burgundy, which was technically a fief
of the French King, back under his control. Charles' only child, Mary of
Burgundy (1457–1482), retained possession of the Burgundian
Netherlands. In 1477, she married Maximilian I of Habsburg (1459–1519),
the eldest son of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick III (1415–1493). After
the marriage of Mary and Maximilian, they jointly ruled the Burgundian
Netherlands under the auspices of Frederick III. After Mary's death in a
horse-riding accident in 1482, Maximilian acted as a regent for their
son Philip the Fair (1478–1506), who was still only four years old. The
year 1482, thus, marks the beginning of the Habsburg Netherlands.

Through a felicitous marriage between Philipe and Joanna of Castile in
1496, their oldest son, Charles V (1500–1558), was able to inherit a
vast patchwork of territories in the course of the early sixteenth
century, including the core Habsburg lands; the Habsburg Netherlands;
Castile, Aragon, and Navarre in Spain; Sicily, Sardinia, and Naples; and
various other regions. Mostly as the result of a series of abdications
from 1554 to 1556, the Habsburg Empire permanently split into two
separate branches. Spain, the Italian regions, the overseas colonies,
and the Habsburg Netherlands went to Charles' eldest son, Philip II
(1527–1598). Ferdinand I (1503–1564), Charles' younger brother, had
already governed the core Habsburg lands since the early 1520s. He had
also acquired some possessions in his own right, notably the Kingdom of
Hungary and the Kingdom of Bohemia in 1526, and was elected King of the
Romans—the title given to the appointed heir of the Holy Roman
Emperor—in 1531. In 1556, Charles then abdicated as Holy Roman Emperor
in favor of his brother. This transferred control of the core Austrian
lands and Ferdinand was recognized by the noble electors in 1558 and the
Pope in 1559. The two branches of the Habsburgs evolved separately from
1556 onwards. The region under the control of the Spanish Habsburgs was
known as the Spanish Netherlands from then onwards.

The reign of the Habsburgs under Charles and Phillip II eventually led
to the Eighty Years War (1568–1648), also known as the Dutch Revolt.
The most fundamental issues were the Habsburg attempts to standardize
and centralize rule as well as their efforts to stamp out religious
heresy from their Empire. In the 1520s, for example, the Habsburgs had
instituted an inquisition—a Catholic Church body charged with
suppressing heresy—within the Low Countries. While the infamous
Spanish Inquisition was quite popular domestically, the Dutch were much
more averse to the persecution of heretics, not in the least because
Protestantism had an increasing number of adherents in the region. Such
policies angered both the nobility and the general population. Matters
escalated particularly during the reign of the overzealous and devout
Roman Catholic Philips II. As Calvinism had swept the low countries in
the 1550s and 1560s, the scale of persecutions against protestants
increased dramatically, despite protests from many of the nobles and the
general population. In the 1560s, the Low Countries also experienced
food shortages, particularly in the Winter of 1564 to 1565, and repeated
outbreaks of the plague. Particularly the Spanish Habsburg response to
these events compounded the local dissatisfaction with their rule.

The first acts of real violence and armed rebellion began in 1566. They
included waves of destruction of Catholic Church property—known as the
iconoclastic fury (*beeldenstorm*)—as well as various local uprisings
against Spanish rule. They were committed by lower nobles and elements
of the general population who commonly went by the name the "beggars"
(*geuzen*). Particularly important support for the beggars came from
Calvinistic preachers that typically held their sermons in secret.
Higher nobles generally distanced themselves from the initial acts of
rebellion, at least publicly, including the powerful trio of Lamoral of
Egmont (1522–1568), Philip of Montmorency (1524–1568), and William of
Orange (1533–1584) also known as William the Silent.[^28]

The initial acts of rebellion largely failed by early 1567. Many of the
beggars exiled abroad to places like Cologne in Germany and La Rochelle
in France, a bastion for Huguenots. William of Orange also fled to
Germany, even though he had not publicly supported the rebellion. In
August 1567, regency of the Habsburg Netherlands was taken over by the
notorious Duke of Alba (1507–1582). He came down harsh on the
rebellious Dutch, executing in the thousands and convicting many,
including William of Orange, in their absence. Notably, in September
1567, he also arrested Lamoral of Egmont and Philip of Montmorency,
counts of Egmont and Hoorn, who had publicly opposed the rebellion, and,
in February 1568, ordered the kidnapping of William's son at Leuven
University. Such events were decisive and William of Orange together
with various other nobles—including his own brother Lodewijk van
Nassau (1538–1574)—organized troops consisting of exiled beggars and
mercenaries in Germany. In the Spring of 1568, they began their
campaigns into the Netherlands, officially starting the Eighty Years
War.

Initially, these military campaigns were not in the name of full-blown
independence from the Spanish Crown. Instead, they were against the
presence of Spanish troops, the centralization of rule, and the
persecution of protestants. Over time, not in the least because of the
often counterproductive responses to the rebellious activities by the
Spanish, it morphed into an independence movement. Some regions and
cities made peace with the Spanish in 1579 through the Union of Arras,
predominantly from the southern Low Countries as they were
overwhelmingly Roman Catholic. The Union of Utrecht a few weeks later,
instead, united various provinces and cities, predominantly from the
northern Low Countries, in a continuation of the rebellion against
Spanish rule. Many of these areas, then, formally declared their
independence from the Spanish monarch on July 26, 1581. After a failed
search for a new monarch—the most obvious candidate William of Orange
had been assassinated in 1584—seven provinces formed the Republic of
the Seven Netherlands in 1588, more commonly known as the Dutch
Republic.

The territory of the seven provinces and the various generality lands
that were controlled directly by the States General—the representative
assembly of the Republic—was very roughly equivalent to that of
modern-day Netherlands. This region seems to have experienced
substantial economic growth throughout the turbulent events of the
revolt against the Spanish, at least until the early seventeenth
century. The key economic area was Holland. According to Van Zanden and
Van Leeuwen, Holland's per capita growth continued at a somewhat faster
pace than in the Late Middle Ages until about 1620. A key event was the
siege and fall of Antwerp to the Spanish in 1584–1585, after which many
merchants as well as economic activity shifted north, particularly to
Holland. From the 1620s, GDP per capita growth then stagnated or even
declined for a few decades, even though overall economic growth was
still substantial given the sharp increase in population due to
immigration. In the eighteenth century, GDP per capita growth picked up
again, returning Holland more consistently to its former peak levels.
According to Van Zanden and Van Leeuwen, much of Holland's growth in the
early modern period, particularly until the middle of the seventeenth
century, was driven by technological progress. Factor substitution and
human capital improvements also played a role.[^29]

The southern part of the Low Countries—roughly equivalent to
modern-day Belgium and Luxemburg—remained under control of the Spanish
Habsburgs. From 1701 onwards it was heavily contested in the War of
Spanish Succession (1701–1714)—a conflict over the successor to the
last rightful heir of the main Habsburg line, Charles II
(1661–1700)—eventually being transferred to the Austrian Habsburgs by
the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. It, then, continued as the Austrian
Netherlands. The region, particularly Flanders, had still shown
substantial economic growth in much of the sixteenth century as well as
moderate per capita growth.[^30] At the time, it was still one of the
wealthiest regions in Europe. Yet, much of the population and economic
activities moved to the north at the end of seventeenth century,
particularly after the Fall of Antwerp in 1584–85. While the southern
Low Countries did have a brief recovery around 1600, the long-term
impact of the immigration to the north was probably substantial and the
region's per capita GDP declined until the second half of the eighteenth
century.[^31]

Let's now turn to the other economic powerhouse of the early modern age:
England (or Great Britain after 1707).

After the death of the childless Anglo-Saxon king Edward the Confessor,
multiple claimants to the throne arose. The throne ended up in the hands
of the Duke of Normandy, William the Conqueror (1028–1087), after the
defeat of his most powerful rival, Harold Godwinson, at the Battle of
Hastings (1066). In the twelfth century, the throne then passed to the
Plantagenet Dynasty, the first in line being Henry II (1133–1189). The
last Plantagenet, Richard II (1367–1400), was deposed by another family
branch, the Lancasters, in 1399. Later in the fifteenth century, they
famously fought over control of the monarchy with another branch of the
Plantagenet family, the Yorks, in the so-called Wars of the Roses
(1455–1485). The conflict ended when Henry Tudor—a Lancastrian
claimant—defeated and killed Richard III (1452–1485) at the Battle of
Bosworth (1485). As Henry VII (1457–1509), he then was the first in the
Tudor line of rulers, taking the name from his father's side.

After the passing of the childless Elizabeth I in 1603, the Scottish
King James VI (1566–1625) also ascended to the English throne where he
was crowned James I. Following the male patrilineal line, it marked the
transition from the Tudor to the Stuart dynasty. Wales had by this time
ceased to be a separate kingdom. In the sixteenth century, it had
formally been incorporated and become subject to English legal and
administrative systems. The infamous Henry VIII (1491–1547) had
formally declared himself King of Ireland in 1542. While England,
Ireland, and Scotland remained separate states, James I, however, now
ruled over all three states in what is known as the Union of the Crowns.
In 1625, James I passed away and he was succeeded by his son Charles I
(1600–1649).

Charles' reign saw the British Isles enter into a very turbulent and
violent period. There were various interrelated conflicts that
transpired across the three kingdoms. But at the center stage were the
English Civil Wars, which consisted of three main conflicts: the First
English Civil War (1642–1646), the Second English Civil War
(1648–1649), and the Third English Civil War (1649–1651).

The institution of parliament had spread rapidly through Europe in the
High and Late Middle Ages. In England, we can properly speak of a
parliament certainly by the end of the thirteenth century, and in the
fourteenth century it was the first such institution to meet more than
once every two years.[^32] During the Civil Wars, tensions over the
division of powers between King and parliament came to a head, though
other factors also impacted the conflict, including Charles' style of
rule, government finances, religious practices and institutions, and the
unpopular wars Charles had entered. The main parties in the Civil Wars
were the royalists, known as the cavaliers, and the Parliamentarians,
known as the roundheads. The violence and turbulence of these events
inspired Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) to produce his most famous work,
*Leviathan* (1651), which essentially argued for rational submission to
absolutist rulers given the violence and chaos of the state of nature.

After the Second English Civil War, England was briefly governed as a
republic. It first came under the reigns of the Rump Parliament
(1649–1653), the parliament that remained after it had been cleansed of
royal sympathizers in 1648. Given its unwillingness to hold new
elections, Oliver Cromwell (1599–1658)—a main political and military
figure of the period—dismissed it in 1653. He, then, occupied the role
of Lord Protector from 1653 to 1658, essentially functioning as a
military dictator. After his death, the same title was briefly held by
his son Richard Cromwell until 1659 when he resigned. In 1660, the
sentiments to the republican experiment had shifted, and both the
Parliament and the Monarchy—now with Charles II (1630–1685) as King,
the son of Charles I who had been executed in 1649—were restored with
the support of the army of George Monck.

While Charles II was publicly an adherent of Anglicanism—the English
branch of Protestantism with its roots in Henry VIII's split from the
Roman Catholic Church in 1534—he was a Catholic sympathizer in private
and converted on his deathbed in 1685. His brother who succeeded him,
James II (1633–1701), was openly Catholic. While their father Charles I
had been an Anglican, Charles II and James II's mother, Henrietta Maria
of France, was a devout and open Catholic. And the children had been
raised as Catholics during their exile in France and the Spanish
Netherlands. After the death of James II's first wife, Anne Hyde, in
1671, he married the devout Catholic Mary of Modena. The birth of their
son James in 1688 sparked powerful protestant nobles, merchants, and
others into action.

In 1677, Mary Stuart (1662–1694)—James II's eldest daughter from his
first marriage and a protestant—had married William III of Orange
(1650–1702), a direct descendant of Frederick Henry of Orange
(1584–1647), the son of William of Orange and his fourth wife Louis de
Coligny. Fearing a Catholic Dynasty and James II's absolutist
tendencies, seven prominent Englishmen—with the support of many
nobles, merchants, landowners, and others behind them—sent William an
invitation for an invasion. William III landed with his forces in
Brixham on November 5, 1688. James' forces collapsed quickly and the
fighting was over in a few weeks with little bloodshed. These events
have become known as the, relatively bloodless, Glorious Revolution.
William and Mary were made joint sovereigns by Parliament in February
1689. He occupied this role next to his roles as governor
(*stadthouder*) of most provinces in the Dutch Republic, including
Holland and Zeeland.

As William had little sympathy for the French, his ascension to the
English throne ushered in a long period of wars with the French. It also
brought substantial changes in political and economic institutions.[^33]
Notably, William's influence helped bring about what is known as the
**Financial Revolution**, a revolution in the English system of public
debt. According to Munro, the revolution involved, first of all, the
introduction of perpetual annuities as a source of capital instead of
temporary loans. The issue of this debt was increasingly on the national
and provincial level, not extended on a personal or municipal basis.
Annuities were further sold and honored without coercive elements,
tradeable on secondary markets, and payments were managed by parliaments
and legislative assemblies through specific taxes. The public had gained
substantial confidence in public debt through these measures.[^34] Other
financial innovations were also brought over from the Netherlands,
including the secondary markets for shares and derivatives in joint
stock companies. Dutch experience with the Bank of Amsterdam (1609) was
also important to establishing the Bank of England (1694).

The Act of Settlement in 1701, then, officially barred Catholics from
holding the crown. So, when the childless Queen Anne died in
1714—Mary's sister that had succeeded William in 1702—the throne
passed to a fairly distant relative, one that barely spoke English,
George I (1660–1727). This marked the transition from the Stuarts to
the Hanoverian Dynasty. Those who supported the Catholic line of the
Stuart family, known as Jacobites, actively continued to organize
uprisings, but were decisively crushed by the middle of the eighteenth
century.

At the time of the Black Death, England was an economic backwater. It
was economically less prosperous than countries like Italy and Spain,
and probably lagged behind Flanders. Like the Low Countries, however,
England experienced a jump in per capita GDP in the decades after the
Black Death. According to Broadberry and colleagues,[^35] unlike in the
Low Countries, GDP per capita then remained roughly constant until the
English Civil Wars. Notably, however, this constant level remained while
the population increased quite substantially in the two hundred years
before the Civil Wars. By the middle of the seventeenth century,
England's population had finally recovered from the Black Death, almost
two hundred years later than in the Low Countries.

After the Civil Wars, Broadberry and colleagues conclude that England's
GDP per head as well as its population grew substantially until the eve
of the industrial revolution. From the 1650s to 1770s, they measure that
GDP per capita grew at 0.44% per year while population increased by
0.22% per year. These trends were the product of structural changes
within the economy and urbanization. Just like the Dutch Republic, but
unlike most other European countries, English prosperity was, thus,
becoming more resilient in dealing with population growth.[^36] In 1707,
Scotland and England formally fused through the Treaty of Union, which
created the Kingdom of Great Britain.[^37] After the Union, Scotland
also began to display significant economic development and it is in this
period that the **Scottish Enlightenment** flourishes as an intellectual
movement with groundbreaking thinkers such as Adam Smith (1723–1790)
and David Hume (1711–1776).

During the Industrial Revolution, beginning in roughly the 1760s,
Broadberry and his colleagues then conclude GDP per capita growth
accelerated in England to 0.64% annually and population growth to 1.15%
annually. The changes to the English landscape and society were rapid.
While the first cotton mill only appeared in Manchester in the 1780s,
for example, there were 104 of them by 1825.[^38] Similar
transformations transpired in Scotland when it began industrializing one
or two decades after England. The Dutch Republic had been Europe's
leading economy from the early sixteenth century, but England decisively
overtook it by the early nineteenth century.[^39] While economic growth
in England was impressive, the conditions for the working class were
initially often worse though than before the Industrial Revolution. Only
in the second half of the nineteenth century did workers begin to see
the benefits of industrialization through increased consumption, higher
wages, and more leisure time.[^40]

From the seventeenth century onwards, the American Colonies also
embarked on a journey of impressive economic growth by pre-modern
standards.[^41] While much of that economic growth was due to the
spectacular growth of the population, it seems that the American
colonies also experienced substantial GDP per capita growth, at a rate
that was even somewhat higher than that seen in the Dutch Republic and
England over the entire early modern period.[^42] Given its much lower
starting position, however, the thirteen colonies had an economic output
that was still much lower than Great Britain's on the eve of the
American Revolution. Even with its higher growth rate, the economic
output of the United States was only about one-third of Britain's by
1820.[^43]

How prosperous were Europe and America compared to the rest of the world
in the early modern period?

According to Broadberry, Guan, and Li,[^44] at the turn of the second
millennium, China, under control of the Song Dynasty (960–1279), was
probably the most prosperous area in the world. It had around twice the
population of Europe at around 60 million.[^45] By the end of the High
Middle Ages, however, northern and central Italy had certainly pulled
ahead of China in GDP per capita when adjusted for purchasing power.
England and Holland also pulled away substantially from China in the
Late Middle Ages.[^46] By 1500, Western Europe was already outperforming
China in GDP per capita adjusted for purchasing power,[^47] even though
China's larger population, estimated at slightly over 100 million,[^48]
made the overall size of its economy larger.[^49]

While Europe continued its per capita growth in the early modern
period—particularly in the Dutch Republic and England (and Great
Britain after 1707)—it seems that China's per capita GDP actually
declined during the Ching Dynasty (1644–1912) due to rapid population
expansion and increasingly smaller agricultural plots per head of the
population.[^50] By the eve of the English Industrial Revolution,
England and Western Europe, therefore, already had substantially higher
living standards than China. The same is probably also true for the
United States, where GDP per capita grew rapidly in the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries.[^51]

The comparison to China is hardly accidental. China is generally
considered the most prosperous non-European country until the Industrial
Revolution. While India had fairly high living standards during the
early years of the Moghul Empire (1526–1857)—being over 60 percent of
England's level in 1600 when adjusted for purchasing power—India never
quite seems to have achieved China's level of prosperity.[^52] Whereas
both China and India's per capita GDP declined in much of the early
modern period, Japan actually begins to display substantial growth in
the eighteenth century. This continued into the Meiji Restoration when
Japan, as one of the few exceptions outside of Europe and its offshoots,
began to industrialize. Japan overtook China in per capita GDP, when
adjusted for purchasing power, somewhere in the eighteenth century,
probably right around the time England began to industrialize.[^53]
Given China's status as the most prosperous country outside of Europe
and its offshoots before industrialization, it is, therefore, important
to note that European living standards on the eve of the English
Industrial Revolution were already substantially higher than China's.

During the Industrial Revolution, European per capita growth rates then
start increasing to modern levels, as the efficiency gains from
knowledge and technology improvements started to far outpace population
growth.[^54] While Great Britain already began industrializing in the
late eighteenth century, for the rest of Europe industrialization was
mainly a nineteenth century phenomenon. The same is true for the United
States. And during industrialization is when Europe and some of its
offshoots, notably the United States, really strongly pull away from the
rest of the world economically and technologically. This period is,
therefore, often called the **Great Divergence**. Japan was perhaps
somewhat of an exception to this Euro-centric trend, but it long lagged
far behind Europe and the United States in living standards, only
catching up in the decades after World War II.[^55]

While the gap between the European and non-European world grew
enormously during the Great Divergence, it is important to reiterate
that it was the continuation of a trend that had already begun, though
at a slower pace, many centuries before the Industrial Revolution. It is
indeed the case, however, that, due to China's greater population, the
absolute size of Europe's economy only surpassed China's in the
nineteenth century, being more than twice its size already in 1870.[^56]

## 3. The Wealth of Nations

What were the main drivers of the European economic expansion from the
High Middle Ages onwards? What made Europe—and some of its settler
societies like the United States, Canada, and Australia—diverge so
substantially from much of the rest of the world particularly during
industrialization, but also in the preceding centuries? And why have
only a few countries outside of Europe and its more successful settler
societies—such as Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan—really been able to
catch up with European prosperity?

Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations*, published a few months before the
Continental Congress adopted the American Declaration of Independence,
is one of the first systematic treatments on the nature and causes of
wealth.[^57] He contends that national prosperity is largely dependent
on the physical goods possessed within the nation, rather than only its
stock of gold and silver. He further views the main driver behind
national wealth as the specialization of labor, particularly in
manufacturing. It is, so Smith argues, in "the great multiplication of
the productions of all the different arts, in consequence of the
division of labour, which occasions, in a well-governed society, that
universal opulaence which extends itself to the lowest ranks of the
people".[^58]

These ideas in the *The Wealth of Nations* are specifically meant to
debunk the mercantilist ideas that prevailed at the time. While
**mercantilism** was not a unified body of thought, mercantilists
commonly thought national wealth and power was to a large extent
dependent on a nation's stock of gold and silver. They, therefore, often
promoted exports over imports as a way to increase the national stock of
precious metals. Many European states, including Great Britain,
implemented mercantilist policies in the early modern period, including
import restrictions and export subsidies.[^59]

Adam Smith's theory of economic growth more specifically benefits from a
self-reinforcing cycle. Within the economic sphere, humans, according to
Smith, are largely driven by self-interest.[^60] As he famously puts it,
"It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the
baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own
interest".[^61] Because self-interest dominates our economic
interactions, they are marked by barter and economic exchange, which
encourages specialization. This specialization results in productivity
gains, as people become better at specific tasks, the progression in
tasks becomes more efficient, and machines and tools are more easily
developed to support production. As capital accumulates from
productivity gains, so Smith argues, it allows for investing in a
further expansion of markets. His main concern here is the reduction of
geographic barriers through reducing transport and communication costs.
The expansion of markets, finally, further increases the division of
labor.

The cycle of increasing prosperity—involving specialization,
productivity gains, capital accumulation, and market
expansion—operates well as long as government does not get in the way
too much. Notably, Smith is strongly, though not quite categorically,
against import restrictions, export subsidies, guilds, and trade
monopolies. According to him, the core functions of government in a
developed society are national defence, preferably through professional
armies; the administration of justice, particularly with regards to
property rights; the construction and management of certain public works
such as roads, canals, and bridges as well as the maintenance of certain
public institutions like trade ambassadors; and public education for
youths and particularly religious education for the general public. To
support these functions, the government can levy certain taxes and
duties. Smith also recognized the legitimacy of trade restrictions in a
few cases, such as for national defence purposes, and supported usury
laws. But other than for such limited exceptions, Smith advocated for
governments to abstain from interference with economic activities; he
was a strong proponent of market competition and free trade.

Smith's preferred style of economic system is known as **laissez-faire
capitalism**, a system of private property rights and free economic
exchange with very limited government interference. It fits more broadly
into what is commonly known as **classical liberalism**, a tradition of
political and social thought—including thinkers such as John Locke
(1632–1704), David Hume, Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), and John Stuart
Mill (1806–1873)—which emphasizes personal freedom, private property
rights, the rule of law, and political and civil rights.[^62]

Smith's ideas were largely adopted by the mainstream economists that
came after him, at least until the era of the Great Depression,
including both classical economists like David Ricardo (1772–1823),
Thomas Malthus, and John Stuart Mill (1806–1873) as well as
neoclassical economists such as Stanley Jevons (1835–1882), Léon Walras
(1834–1910), and Carl Menger (1840–1921). The main distinction that
separates **classical** from **neoclassical economists** is the latter's
endorsement of **marginalism**, the theory that the price an economic
agent is willing to pay for a good is determined by the marginal utility
for her. This is a subjective theory of value. The neoclassical
economists also endorsed the **law of diminishing marginal utility**,
namely, that each additional unit of a good consumed has a lower
marginal utility.

Adam Smith and his classical successors had notably been unable to
resolve the **diamond-water paradox**: the puzzle as to why the price of
water, which is essential to life, is so cheap, while diamonds, a
trivial luxury, are so expensive. The primary obstacle for the classical
economists was that they—as well as Karl Marx (1818–1883)—had
sought to identify the labor and costs that went into production as an
explanation of its exchange value. Even Bentham and Mill, who developed
extensive utilitarian moral frameworks in the nineteenth century,
continued to hold onto this mistaken notion of how prices are actually
determined. In the 1870s, however, various economists began promoting
marginalism, notably Jevons, Walras, and Menger. This idea was so
profound that it has been dubbed the **marginal revolution**.

While the marginal revolution marked a profound theoretical shift in
economics, both classical and neoclassical economists emphasized the
positive effects on prosperity of competition, free trade, and limited
government intervention in the economy. Just like Smith, his successors
generally emphasized the importance of specialization and the
development of markets for economic growth, Ricardo most famously
through his theory of comparative advantage. This theory states that
countries benefit from producing what they are relatively most efficient
at, even if another country is better at producing it. While some
**heterodox schools of economic thought** also arose in the late
nineteenth and early twentieth century—including the **Austrian School
of Economics**,[^63] the **Marxian School of Economics**,[^64] and
institutional economics—Smith's ideas were largely accepted by
mainstream economists until the publication of John Maynard Keynes' *The
General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money* (1936).

## 4. The role of institutions

The modern scientific literature on economic growth and development is
still substantially influenced by the ideas of Smith on the
specialization of labor and the expansion of markets, certainly when it
comes to the pre-modern era. Kohn,[^65] for example, contends that the
prime mover of pre-industrial growth in Europe was indeed the creation
and expansion of markets. Many scholars also emphasize the importance of
intercontinental trade and empire to Europe's economic rise and the
Great Divergence, and openness to trade is also seen as a consistent
factor in explaining modern global inequality.[^66] Of course, many
other intermediating factors and ultimate causes of Europe's economic
rise and persistent global inequality have been proposed within the
scientific literature, including climate and geography;[^67] religious
and cultural attitudes;[^68] human capital formation;[^69] and
policymaking knowledge.[^70]

In the last few decades, the factor probably most frequently put forward
to explain Europe's economic rise and the origins of global inequality,
however, has been that of political and legal institutions.
Specifically, the economic and technological divergence of Europe and
some of its offshoots—such as the United States, Canada, and
Australia—from the rest of the world is explained by their distinct
political and legal institutions.[^71] "Institutions" here refers to
formal institutions such as constitutions, laws, central banks, and
parliaments, as well as more informal institutions such as rules of
conduct, norms, and traditions. Much of the effort at bringing
institutions back into the forefront of explanations on economic growth
and prosperity has been due to scholars from a school of economic
thought known as new institutional economics.

While the embrace of marginalism was the key theoretical difference with
classical economists, the majority of neoclassical economists also
generally began to move away from analyzing the political, social, and
legal context of consumer and producer choices. They also increasingly
incorporated formal mathematical modelling into their work. A key figure
in propagating these methodological shifts was Alfred Marshall
(1842–1942) who systematized and consolidated neoclassical principles
in his *Principles of Economics* (1890). The supply and demand diagram
with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis,
ubiquitous in modern economics, is his invention. Marshall's
contribution essentially provided the framework for modern
micro-economics. Whereas the discipline had standardly been called
**political economy** until the middle of the nineteenth
century—reflecting that it concerned the study of production,
distribution, and consumption in the context of social and political
arrangements—it was commonly called just economics by the turn of the
twentieth century.

The old **institutional economics** that arose in the early twentieth
century, primarily in the United States, was largely a critical response
to the theoretical and methodological developments in mainstream
neoclassical economics. It emphasized that economic actors were not just
utility maximizers, but embedded into path-dependent legal and social
institutions that shaped their beliefs, values, preferences, and
economic transactions. These institutions were largely the product of
cultural and technological developments. The institutional economists
also argued that economic theory should be closer to reality,
incorporating insights particularly from psychology, and more open to
empirical testing, including through both quantitative and qualitative
methods. They generally supported government interference in the economy
and were instrumental in Franklin Delano Roosevelt\'s New Deal. But
while it was popular in the interwar period, the institutional economics
research program largely faded into obscurity after World War II.[^72]

Since the 1970s, many of the topics pursued by the old institutional
economists have resurged within economics, most notably through the
**new institutional economics** subdiscipline. A main theme in this
literature is how formal political and legal institutions as well as
more informal institutions affect economic activities and social
outcomes, as well as the economic, social, and political factors that
influence institutional developments. With respect to the latter, the
literature emphasizes how rational cost-benefit calculations impact
institutional dynamics. While the early literature in new institutional
economics often emphasized the emergence of efficient institutions,
there has come an increasing acceptance that inefficient institutions
may arise and persist. By contrast to the old institutional economics,
the new institutional economics is largely an effort to improve and
extend mainstream neoclassical economics, rather than to replace
it.[^73] Whereas the old institutional economics was more descriptive
and historical, the new institutional economics utilizes the formal
mathematical techniques and assumptions from neoclassical economics to
produce more rigorous theoretical results.

The importance of institutions is now generally recognized by both
neoclassical and non-neoclassical economists, a scientific position that
would have been obvious to Adam Smith and the other classical political
economists.[^74]

So, while any account of the Great Divergence and modern global
inequality can hardly be a simple grand narrative, political and legal
institutions matter a lot. An illustrative account of the dynamics
between political and legal institutions on the one hand, and economic
growth and development on the other, is given by Daron Acemoglu and
James Robin—2024 winners of the Nobel prize in economics together with
Simon Johnson—in *Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity,
and Poverty*.

According to Acemoglu and Robinson,[^75] the key to prosperity is
attaining political institutions within a state where power is
distributed across a broad coalition with checks on power, yet with
sufficient centralization to maintain law and order. Such inclusive
political institutions tend to uphold the rule of law, promote legal
equality, protect property rights, allow businesses and workers to exit
and enter into markets, and provide public services such as education
and infrastructure. They also allow technological advancements to
flourish. Inclusive political institutions, therefore, are the real
engines of economic prosperity.

States where small groups wield political power, by contrast, tend to
create economic institutions which extract wealth from the rest of
society to the benefit of the ruling group. While such states can indeed
achieve economic growth for some time, they typically cannot produce
long-lasting prosperity. This is why the Soviet Union eventually
stagnated, after initially showing decades of growth by moving peasants
into more productive industrial activities. A similar fate is likely
also to befall China, as it has failed to use the economic momentum of
the previous decades to embrace the more inclusive political
institutions which can bring about enduring prosperity.

Inclusive political and economic institutions tend to persist. Political
and economic institutions that only benefit a narrow elite also tend to
be stuck in a vicious cycle. This, according to Acemoglu and Robinson,
largely explains why global inequality is so entrenched. The movement
towards more inclusive political and economic institutions frequently
depends on critical historical junctures and is often affected by what
at first blush might appear to be minor contingencies. To illustrate the
capriciousness of the historical trajectories of institutions, consider,
for example, the impact of the Black Death on serfdom.

Western Europe made a significant move towards more inclusive political
and economic institutions in the fourteenth century. The Black Death had
namely created an acute shortage of labor which empowered peasants in
Western and parts of Central Europe. Progress did not come easy, of
course, particularly due to government efforts to stop it. Notable are
the various peasant uprisings in the wake of the Black Death in which
the suppression of wages was a contributing factor.[^76] Despite the
challenges to progress, however, peasants in Western and parts of
Central Europe were able to begin removing the yoke of feudalism, obtain
higher wages, and demand better treatment.

Towards the East in places such as Hungary, Prussia, Bohemia, Poland,
Ukraine, and Russia, the Black Death, by contrast, actually led to a
more servile status for peasants in what is known as the **second
serfdom**. In the words of Acemoglu and Robinson,[^77]

> Though in 1346 there were few differences between Western and Eastern
> Europe in terms of political and economic institutions, by 1600 they
> were worlds apart. In the West, workers were free of feudal dues,
> fines, and regulations and were becoming a key part of a booming
> market economy. In the East, they were also involved in such an
> economy, but as coerced serfs growing the food and agricultural goods
> demanded in the West. This institutional divergence was the result of
> a situation where the differences between these areas initially seemed
> very small: in the East, lords were a little better organized; they
> had slightly more rights and more consolidated landholdings. Towns
> were weaker and smaller, peasants less organized. In the grand scheme
> of history, these were small differences. Yet these small differences
> between the East and the West became very consequential for the lives
> of their populations and for the path of institutional development
> when the feudal order was shaken up by the Black Death.

While European political and legal institutions were still more
rudimentary in the High and the Late Middle Ages, they likely already
had some role in causing the economic expansion of the period besides
serving as the ultimate foundation for economic progress in the early
modern and industrialization eras.

The feudal lord-vassal system decentralized political power between
monarchs, nobility, and clergy. Monarchs were generally quite dependent
on their direct vassals for raising armies. While the monarch could
generally tax subjects on royal domains, those who lived on fiefs owned
by noble and clerical vassals could only be taxed indirectly, namely
through their Lord at the request of the monarch.[^78] As already noted
by Montesquieu in the eighteenth century, this system prevented imperial
overreach and fostered long-term order even if it was not always stable.
In his words,[^79] the system

> diminished the whole of the weight of lordship by giving many people
> various kinds of lordship over the same thing or the same persons,
> which set various limits to empire that was too extensive, which
> produce rule with an inclination to anarchy and anarchy with a
> tendency to order and harmony.

In an illustrative study, Blaydes and Chaney show that by 1100 European
rulers already lasted longer than their Islamic counterparts. In the
Islamic world, the sultans generally relied on slaves from the
non-Islamic world to maintain their armies, an institution called
mamlukism. These mamluks generally lived an existence separately from
the rest of the population—in terms of physical location, language,
and customs—and were directly loyal to the sultan. The nobility in the
Islamic world, therefore, had much less influence than in Europe.
According to Blaydes and Chaney, the feudal system of military
recruitment drastically increased the costs of unsuccessful revolts for
the European nobility.[^80]

More stability in leadership and a reduction in infighting would have
contributed to Europe's economic development. According to Acemoglu and
Robinson, "extractive institutions create incentives for infighting in
order to control power and its benefits".[^81] It is precisely this
dynamic that explains the poverty of so many African states.

> In this light, it is no surprise that the extractive institutions that
> many African countries inherited from the colonial powers sowed the
> seeds of power struggles and civil wars\.... In Angola, Burundi, Chad,
> Côte d'Ivoire, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia, Liberia,
> Mozambique, Nigeria, Republic of Congo Brazzaville, Rwanda, Somalia,
> Sudan, and Uganda, and of course in Sierra Leone \... these conflicts
> would turn into bloody civil wars and would create economic ruin and
> unparalleled human suffering—as well as cause state failure.[^82]

Europe has a long tradition of utilizing sovereign-constraining and more
inclusive political institutions. In the sixth and fifth century BC,
Athenian political power was dispersed among male citizens through the
open general assembly, and through the various administrative and
judicial offices that were mostly allocated by lottery.[^83] For nearly
500 years, power was constrained in the Roman Republic by competition
between the Senate, Assemblies, and other administrative, judicial, and
military institutions.[^84] At the start of the High Middle Ages,
monarchs and regional rulers typically had informal councils and
assemblies with nobles, clergy, and sometimes peasants. Parliaments in
the "modern" sense, the key institution to check political power and
provide a voice to a broad coalition in society, then began to appear
during the High Middle Ages.[^85]

According to Van Zanden, Buringh, and Bosker,[^86] the key driving force
behind the formation of parliaments in the High and Late Middle Ages was
the growth of fairly independent towns and cities with corporate
identities and rights, typically called communes. Including these
communes into the informal feudal assemblies and councils transformed
them into parliaments. Understanding urban representation as a key
condition for our conceptualization of a parliament,[^87] then the first
parliament was arguably founded at Léon in 1188.[^88] From there
parliaments gradually spread throughout other parts of Spain and the
rest of Europe. They arose for monarchies as well as specific regions.
These early parliaments could only be convened by the monarch (or
another ruler at the regional level) and did not meet according to a
regular schedule. The key power attained mostly during the Late Middle
Ages was that monarchs required parliamentary approval to levy new
taxes, including notably for wars.

The main geographical exception to the growth of parliaments was
northern Italy. In the High and Late Middle Ages, semi-autonomous
communes arose all throughout Europe. Many German towns and cities, for
example, became semi-autonomous in this period. Sometimes these towns
and cities were formally, but not de facto, under the lordship of a
prince or ecclesiastical authority. The most famous examples, however,
were directly under the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor. These free
imperial cities were officially granted access into the Imperial Diet in
the fifteenth century and included places such as Cologne, Strasbourg,
Hamburg, Bremen, and Basel. Flemish cities such as Ghent, Bruges, and
Ypres who obtained their wealth from cloth production and merchant
activities also enjoyed a substantial degree of independence in the High
and Late Middle Ages. They often zealously protected it. An effort by
the French King Philip IV (1268–1314) to subdue the Flemish in the
early fourteenth century, led to a massacre of French soldiers by the
townspeople of Bruges in May of 1302; the subsequent army of knights
sent in July was also massacred by a militia of townspeople and peasants
in what is known as the Battle of the Golden Spurs.

Nowhere were the communes as independent from feudal rulers, however, as
in northern and central Italy. After the Treaty of Verdun, Carolingian
control over northern and central control gradually subsided. While Otto
I integrated much of northern and central Italy into the Holy Roman
Empire during the tenth century, the region's cities and towns became
increasingly independent during the High Middle Ages. This is also true
for Venice, which was still formally part of the Byzantine Empire at the
turn of the millennium. Many of the towns and cities in northern and
central Italy even became fully independent and very influential
city-states with the power to set their own foreign policy, raise
armies, tax their populations, and so on.[^89] City-states such as
Genoa, Venice, and Florence were actually some of the most powerful
European political entities in the High and Late Middle Ages. Venice and
Genoa were essentially maritime empires for much of the period.

The absence of monarchical influence in northern Italy during the
crucial years of the growth of parliaments probably explains why the
parliamentary institution did not really take root in that region.[^90]
The South was instead marked by monarchical rule throughout the crucial
years of parliamentary growth in the High and Late Middles Ages. And
parliaments indeed developed there.[^91] But while parliaments may not
have developed as strongly in the north of Italy, inclusive political
institutions did develop in most of the towns and cities. These
institutions generally gave a high degree of influence to elite
landowners and merchants. To varying degrees, they also sometimes gave
influence to lower classes, most notably in Florence, where a
guild-based political system also granted influence to more modest local
merchants, manufacturers, shopkeepers, artisans, and notaries from the
late thirteenth to the early fifteenth century. Following the Ancient
Athenian tradition, lottery procedures were common in the selection of
executives and administrators in the northern Italian cities and
towns.[^92]

Many of the northern Italian cities and towns were able to amass great
prosperity in the High and Late Middle Ages, and the region was the most
powerful economically in Europe. That prosperity fuelled the revival of
Greek and Roman learning and the general architectural, artistic,
scientific, and literary advancements of the **Italian Renaissance**
from the fourteenth to the sixteenth centuries. Above all this
prosperity extended to Venice and Florence. According to the famous
nineteenth century German historian Jacob Burckhardt, Venice and
Florence were "of deep significance for the history of the human
race\.... \[N\]either can be compared to anything else which the world
has hitherto produced".[^93]

In the early modern period, the differences between some parts of Europe
and the rest of the world became more pronounced, at least in
significant part due to the economic impact of inclusive and
sovereign-constraining political and legal institutions.

Admittedly, the monarchs in many European states increasingly obtained
unchecked and centralized control over their territories with
professional bureaucracies, standing armies, and unified laws in the
face of weak parliaments and a lack of other checks on power. Rulers
often claimed that their authority came directly from God, a tenet known
as the divine right of kings. These strong centralized monarchies with
unchecked power came to be known as absolute monarchies and the ideology
behind such rule as **absolutism**. While monarchs were generally forced
to rule through elites during the Middle Ages, those checks on power,
therefore, dwindled within many European monarchies in the early modern
period, though the degree of absolute rule was generally not as strong
as in North Africa or Asia.

Perhaps the epitome of the absolutist state in Western Europe was
France. In the wake of the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598)—a
series of civil wars between French Catholics and French Calvinist
Protestants known as Huguenots—the monarchy increasingly seized
control. The kings of France did not convene the French Parliament—the
Estates General—at all between 1614 and 1789. Louis XIV (1638–1715),
the Sun King, supposedly famously quipped "L'État, c'est moi" ("The
State, it's me").

In 1469, the marriage of Ferdinand II of Aragon (1452–1516) and
Isabella I of Castille (1451–1504) united much of the Iberian Peninsula
outside of Portugal. In 1492, the last Muslim Kingdom in the Iberian
Peninsula, the Emirate of Granada, was defeated. As already noted, the
daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella, Joanna, married Philip of Habsburg
and their son, Charles V, became the first Habsburg king of Spain
(1516). His son, Phillip II, then began unifying Spain administratively.
In this period, increasing absolutism was fuelled by the Crown's control
over colonies and international trade from which it profited
spectacularly. It is from this period until the early nineteenth century
that an absolutist monarchy ruled Spain. The Spanish style of absolutist
rule, as already discussed, was one of the main reasons behind the Dutch
Revolt.

The character of the Italian city-states and communes also had changed
by the modern period. In 1434, the Medici family gained *de facto*
control over Florence's republican institutions. This lasted until 1494
when they were expelled from the city. After several decades of struggle
between the Medici and republican forces, the Habsburg King Charles V
invaded Florence in 1530. He transformed the city into a Duchy with
Alessandro di Medici as the first duke. Eventually, the title changed to
the Grand Duke of Tuscany, as surrounding areas were annexed. The
territory then experienced fairly absolutist rule of which almost two
hundred years under the Medici. Many of the Italian communes and
city-states underwent such transformations earlier and Florence was one
of the few to survive into the fifteenth century. The Visconti family,
for example, had gradually established hereditary control over Milan in
the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries already, officially establishing
the Duchy of Milan in 1395 with Giangaleazzo Visconti as the first duke.
Venice was a little bit different. It was able to maintain republican
institutions until Napoleon invaded in 1797. However, Venice's
republican institutions had already become much more aristocratic in the
early fourteenth century,[^94] and by the time Napoleon invaded the city
had become controlled by a very small aristocracy.[^95]

Two states were prominent exceptions to the progress of absolutism in
early modern Europe: the Dutch Republic and England (or Great Britain
after 1707). As was seen in the previous section, it is precisely these
two states that were marked by significant economic growth in the early
modern period.

The first meeting of the Estates General in the Low Countries only took
place in 1406. In the 1570s, however, it became an independent body with
scheduled operation. Thereby, it was the first major parliament in
Europe to acquire such modern features. The same transformation occurred
for the parliaments within each of the rebelling, notably in Holland.
The federal structure of the Republic protected against the
concentration of power. In England, the foundation for a national
parliament was laid by the **Magna Carta** (1215) and by the end of the
thirteenth century it became the first such institution in Europe to
meet fairly frequently. Independence from the monarchy and regular
operation was attained, however, only after the Glorious Revolution
(1688). After the Glorious Revolution, England also adopted increasingly
strong property rights, ensured more equal application of the law, and
abolished arbitrary taxation and monopolies. By 1700, the English
Monarch was balanced by a strong parliament, an independent judiciary,
and the rule of law.

The increased parliamentary rights in the Dutch Republic and England
were not just historical accidents. The Dutch Revolt (1568–1648), the
English Civil Wars (1642–1651), and the Glorious Revolution (1688)
were, in fact, largely driven by merchants that sought to increase the
power of parliaments at the expense of the monarch.[^96] The subsequent
success of parliaments in these regions would have also been supported
by their geographies. According to Stasavanger,[^97] as distance and
communication constraints were lower than in bigger states such as
France and Spain, the Dutch Republic and England were able to acquire
substantial power over expenditures in the early modern period. This
also allowed those states to raise more revenues.

Given these political developments, the strong economic development and
per capita income growth in the Dutch Republic, particularly in Holland,
and in England (and Scotland after 1707) in the early modern period
relative to the rest of Europe is hardly surprising. These areas really
became the main engines of economic growth and the center of economic
power clearly shifted from northern Italy to northwestern Europe.[^98]

While Europe had generally begun to pull away from the rest of the world
already in the early modern period, it most decisively diverged in the
modern period. While Great Britain began to industrialize in the 1760s,
this was largely a nineteenth century phenomenon in the rest of Europe.
The key event to broader European industrialization was actually the
French Revolution (1789).[^99]

When Louis XVI (1754–1793) ascended to the throne in 1774, his
predecessors had already amassed massive debts. The situation worsened
under his reign, particularly due to France's financing of the American
Revolution (1775–1783) which was done almost entirely with borrowed
money. In 1787, he made moves to levy new taxes. The country was in a
poor economic state and had recently suffered some bad harvests. While
the clergy and nobility had traditionally many exemptions on taxes,
Louis XVI decided he now had to tax them. So, he convened the Assembly
of Notables—consisting of nobility and church officials—for the
first time in 150 years. But as they would only agree to new taxes with
more political power, Louis refused and next year just tried to impose
new taxes without their permission through a royal edict.

The regional high courts of justice—known as parlements—were
required to register such edicts for them to have the effect of law.
While normally these parlements would have just complied with royal
edicts, the parlement of Paris and various other regional parlements
this time refused to register the law on the basis that it lacked
legitimacy. Without any alternative, Louis called the Estates General to
Versailles on May 5 of 1789 for the first time since 1614.

The Estates General was composed of representatives within three
estates: the clergy (the first estate), the nobility (the second
estate), and the rest of society (the third estate). While traditionally
each estate had received one vote, the third estate demanded that voting
proceed by head. Given that they had more than 95% of the
representatives, this would have clearly favored the third estate. After
Louis refused to grant voting by head, the third estate proclaimed
itself the legitimate government of the French nation calling themselves
the National Assembly. A large number of clergy and some nobles joined
them. They convened separately and effectively sidelined the Estates
General.

The delegates of the third estate had been influenced by the ideas of
enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke (1632–1704), Montesquieu
(1689–1755), Voltaire (1694–1778), Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778),
and Adam Smith (1723–1790). They were particularly angered by the
privileges enjoyed by nobles and clergy, the excesses of the French
monarchy, and the dire living conditions for much of the general public.
In general, they believed absolutism was responsible for the plight of
France. The news of the events in Versailles began to circulate in
Paris. As many in the public shared the sentiments of the
representatives at Versailles, riots broke out in the Summer of 1789.
Fearing an invasion of Paris to restore order, rebellious Parisians
descended on the Bastille to seize weapons for self-defence on July 14,
1789, butchering many of the guards in the process.

While initially many revolutionaries had sought a constitutional
monarchy, republican radicals—who sought complete abolition of the
monarchy and a popular government— increasingly became influential as
the revolution progressed. Their rise was particularly encouraged by
fears that the nobility and clergy were conspiring with foreign powers
to overthrow the revolutionary government. The attempt by Marie
Antoinette and Louis XVI to flee France in June of 1791 and the outbreak
of war with Austria in April 1792 further fuelled the rise of republican
radicals.

The most notable of the republican radicals were a group that called
themselves the Jacobins. They increasingly began to influence public
sentiments against the monarchy, nobles, and clergy. On August 10 of
1792, thousands attacked Tuileries Palace where the royal family was
staying. While Louis XVI initially fled, he was soon arrested. In the
so-called September massacres of 1792, thousands of nobles and clergy
were executed by militias that broke into prisons in Paris. Similar
events followed in other parts of the country. While the Jacobins did
not directly organize such events, they certainly created an environment
for them.

In September 1791, a new constitution had been written and the National
Assembly disbanded. In October 1791, it was replaced by the legislative
assembly of the constitutional monarchy. During the events of August 10,
1792, a radical group, many of them Jacobins, formed a revolutionary
municipal government in Paris called the Paris Commune (1792–1795).
Recognizing the end of the monarchy, the Legislative Assembly dissolved
itself in September and called for elections to a new body, the National
Convention, which would govern a republic. The National Convention was
elected by universal suffrage for males over 25. It convened for the
first time on September 21 at which point the convention officially
abolished the monarchy and declared France a republic. The National
Convention included many of the foremost Jacobins, including Maximilian
Robespierre, Louis Antoine de Saint-Just, and Georges Danton.

The Jacobin-led rule in the First Republic became increasingly violent.
In January of 1793, Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette were beheaded by
guillotine. In April 1793, the Committee of Public Safety was formed,
the primary executive body in the new republic. From September 1793 to
July 1794, mainly under the leadership of Robespierre, carried out a
violent campaign against all suspected enemies of the Republic. Known as
the Reign of Terror, thousands of nobles, clergy, and other supposed
conspirators were killed. In July 1794, delegates of the convention
organized the arrest and execution of many high-profile Jacobins,
including Robespierre and Saint-Just. Many other Jacobins were also
arrested, expelled from government, or just lost their influence. While
the government became more moderate, it was not that popular with the
public.

Pretty soon after declaring war on Austria, other European powers—for
fear of French revolutionary ideas and other reasons—joined Austrian
forces, including Prussia, Great Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic.
Initially France was not that successful, but by July 1794 its
performance had started to improve. While the French army was less
professional, the successful introduction of mass conscription in 1793
had particularly begun to bear fruit. What motivated France in their war
with European powers was not only the desire to protect its own
revolutionary government, but also to export the ideals of the French
Revolution elsewhere. In the subsequent years, France managed to
establish many sister republics, often short-lived, including the
Batavian Republic (1795–1806) instead of the Dutch Republic, the
Helvetic Republic (1798–1803) to replace the old Swiss confederacy, and
the Ligurian Republic (1797–1805) to replace the Republic of Genoa.

While France was successful militarily, the government was unable to
overcome the country's economic problems, factional tensions, and
general unrest. Napolean Bonaparte (1769–1821) grew in popularity
within this environment of weak government. His military success,
particularly in the Italian campaigns, humble background, and
republicanism fuelled this popularity. In 1799, Napolean orchestrated a
coup and installed himself as head of state. By that time, French
control and influence had already expanded into the Low Countries,
Switzerland, much of Italy, and parts of the Holy Roman Empire. But
under Napoleon, France consolidated this influence and further expanded
it through conquest, annexation, and alliances. In 1804, Napoleon
declared himself emperor. By 1806, all the sister republics had
disappeared, either annexed or turned into monarchical client states by
Napoleon.

The French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802) and the Napoleonic Wars
(1803–1815) had a substantial impact on Europe. Particularly Napoleon
supported the establishment of strong bureaucracies, legal equality, the
protection of private property, the abolition of guilds, and the
elimination of feudal laws. The re-introduction of monarchies in many
regions was more practical, rather than a complete abandonment of the
ideals of the Revolution. By 1814, allied forces had conquered France
and Napoleon was exiled to Elba. After his escape in 1815, he was able
to raise an army, but suffered a crippling defeat at the Battle of
Waterloo in June. He was, then, exiled to St Helena and died there in
1821.

While the French Bourbon monarchy had been re-instated in 1814 with
Louis XVIII, there was no return to absolutist government. When his
successor, Charles X, attempted to concentrate monarchical power again,
protests broke out. After the July Revolution of 1830, Charles X fled to
England and the House of Orleans assumed the throne. The monarchy
promoted the growth of business and industry. By the 1840s, however, due
to an economic downturn and lack of ability to keep order further
protests broke out, which led to the final deposition of the monarchy
and the beginning of the Second Republic. Napoleon III was elected
President in December 1848, but seized power by force in December 1851
and declared himself emperor in 1852. He also promoted business and
economic development. After an economic downturn and a bad defeat
against Prussia in 1870, Napoleon III was captured and the Third
Republic was proclaimed. This time it stuck and the French were able to
build a political and social system that united its citizens better.

While throwing the yoke of absolutism was messy, the Revolution set
France on the road to more inclusive political and economic institutions
which allowed the country to industrialize and build long-lasting
prosperity. While monarchies persisted after Napoleon's fall, guilds,
feudal laws, aristocratic privileges, and absolutism were substantially
weakened in many parts of Europe. The French Revolution also inspired
independence movements, unification movements, and political revolutions
and reforms throughout the nineteenth century. In the early 1820s, there
were liberal uprisings in Spain and Italy. The same decade saw Greece
obtain its independence from the Ottoman Empire. Belgium had been
included in the Netherlands at the Congress of Vienna and successfully
gained independence in 1830. As in France, the year 1848 saw widespread
European protests for liberal constitutions and universal suffrage.
Great Britain greatly expanded the male suffrage in the nineteenth
century, extending it fully to all adult males and females in 1928.

The French Revolution had, thus, set in motion the road towards more
inclusive political institutions, industrialization, and long-run
prosperity throughout much of Western and Central Europe.
