# Chapter 5: The Modern Corporation

The modern corporation developed out the evolution of the practices and
laws surrounding joint stock companies. In this chapter, we trace the
origins of three broad attributes of modern corporate regimes.

- The shares of public corporations can now be traded on modern stock
  exchanges such as the NASDAQ or the New York Stock Exchange. Various
  contracts based on share prices can also be traded on exchanges,
  including futures, options, and more niche products such as
  exchange-traded notes and contracts for difference. Certain types of
  contracts based on share prices are partially or predominantly traded
  over the counter such as repurchasing agreements and swaps.

- Commercial corporations are now predominantly created and managed
  through state registration regimes. The creation of commercial
  companies through royal charters or legislative acts is now highly
  uncommon in England and elsewhere in the developed world. This
  transformation of incorporation from a political to an administrative
  act has made the corporate status widely available.

- Limited liability in the modern sense—where owners' private assets
  are shielded from claims by company creditors—is now a common
  feature of corporations and typically the default within corporate
  registration regimes.

We begin in *Section 1* by briefly describing the rise of exchange
trading. In *Section 2*, we show how exchange trading for company
shares, futures, and options developed in the Dutch Republic during the
seventeenth century, and from there spread out to England and elsewhere.
In *Section 3*, we describe the negative aura that developed around the
secondary markets for joint stock company shares. *Section 4* traces how
incorporation with (full) limited liability has become generally
available through a registration process. Our focus is on England and
the United States, where registration regimes first seem to have
developed before they spread out to other jurisdictions.

# 1. The development of exchange trading

As shown in *Chapter 4*, active secondary markets for securities already
existed in Europe by the end of the High Middle Ages. Genoa, Venice, and
other northern Italian cities had active markets in ownership shares for
ships called *luoghi* or *loca*.[^1] Such systems of ownership and
secondary markets for them also evolved in other parts of the economy,
notably within the Genoese associations that managed public debts known
as *compere*. Shares in these associations were probably the first
government bonds in Europe. Shares in the House of Saint George—which
consolidated many of the *compere* in the fifteenth century—became an
extremely popular investment product in Genoa and elsewhere.[^2] The
issuance of government bonds also spread to other Italian cities such as
Venice, Pisa, and Florence. In the Late Middle Ages, active secondary
markets arose for such bonds.[^3]

Systems of share ownership in ships also were popular in other parts of
Europe, particularly in the Low Countries. While the system already
existed in the northern trade by the turn of the twelfth century,[^4] it
became really popular during the Late Middle Ages, especially in
Holland. The so-called *partenrederijen* were a popular investment with
merchants, outfitters of ships, and their families and friends. The
share system of ownerships also was popular for managing other types of
commercial operations such as windmills, breweries, and tile works.[^5]
While bonds were not introduced into northern Europe until the early
modern period, municipal annuities had been popular investment products
in France, Germany, and the Low Countries since the thirteenth century.
As with the Italian bonds, active secondary markets developed almost
immediately, particularly in the Low Countries.[^6]

Importantly, these early secondary markets were, despite having
reasonable liquidity and sophistication, not yet marked by exchange
trading. Following Poitras,[^7] we can understand **exchange trading**
as the organized, regulated, and centralized sale and purchase of
standardized, transferrable financial contracts on an ongoing basis with
the support of a clearance and settlement system. Clearance and
settlement systems are discussed more elaborately at a later point in
the context of payments. For now, we can understand a clearance and
settlement system as one that ensures purchases and sales of financial
contracts are periodically netted between a group of traders, rather
than individually processed. This simplifies the number of payments and
deliveries that eventually have to be made.

A key aspect to exchange trading is that the financial contracts are not
just agreements between two parties that occur in the regular course of
business. As stated by Poitras,

> In particular, speculative exchange trading of a transferable security
> contract needs to be distinguished from a situation where a buyer and
> seller meet at, say, the Royal Exchange and agree to a forward sale of
> goods with a contract then drawn up by a 'scrivner'. In this
> situation, it is the goods that are being traded, not the forward
> contract. Speculative exchange trading requires transferability and an
> exchange-clearing mechanism to settle positions. In turn,
> transferability requires standardized contract terms and relatively
> homogeneous deliverable commodity.[^8]

Early modern exchange trading, according to the aforementioned
conceptualization, was probably first seen in Antwerp during the
sixteenth century.

In the medieval period, the core economic activity in the Low Countries
was in the South in cities such as Ghent, Bruges, and Ypres. During the
fifteenth century, Antwerp replaced Bruges as the main port in
northwestern Europe, and it was probably the busiest port in Europe at
the start of the early modern period. In 1531, Antwerp erected the first
permanent structure dedicated to housing the ongoing purchase and sale
of commodities and goods as well as the organization of accompanying
financial contracts.

The creation of the **Antwerp Exchange** was not a small feat.
Throughout the High and Late Middle Ages, European trade had been marked
by merchants and capital that concentrated periodically at large trade
and financial fairs, such as those at Champagne, Geneva, and
Besançon-Piacenza. As trade volume grew in the Late Middle Ages,
however, capital, merchants, financiers, brokers, and bankers
increasingly concentrated more permanently in certain areas of
commercial cities, typically on central squares and in accompanying
buildings such as inns. This type of trading is sometimes called
**bourse trading**. The name "bourse" comes from an inn called *Huis ter
Beurze*. Bruges was the first northern European city where bourse
trading developed, and this inn was located on a central square. It was
built in the thirteenth century by the Van Der Beurze family.[^9] The
**Antwerp Exchange** was, however, the first permanent structure, one of
great scale and grandeur, dedicated purely to housing the trade of goods
and financing. Antwerp was the city where ongoing commodity markets were
first really established.[^10]

In the second half of the sixteenth century, the Antwerp Exchange saw
exchange trading develop in promissory notes. A **promissory note** is a
contract that contains a promise by a debtor to pay or otherwise settle
a sum of money by a certain date or upon demand, with or without
interest. A **bearer promissory note** is one where the rights accrue to
whomever has physical possession of the note. In the second half of the
sixteenth century, exchange trading arose specifically for bearer
promissory notes. These notes were, in other words, bought and sold
before their due date at a discount, that is, for less than their face
value plus, if applicable, interest. This type of secondary market also
existed for the sale of interest deposits held by the House of Saint
George, as discussed in *Chapter 4*. But in Antwerp the market was
highly liquid, continuous, and supported with a clearance and settlement
system. The opportunity for the exchange of promissory notes helped
financiers manage risks and liquidity.

In the early seventeenth century, exchange trading also developed for
promissory notes that were tied to specific parties by name. These would
be reassigned via signatures. Exchange trading also developed for bills
of exchange, payment orders which enjoyed use in a variety of contexts
in the Late Middle Ages and early modern period.[^11]

The southern Low Countries had substantial economic growth in much of
the sixteenth century.[^12] Antwerp was the cultural, economic, and
financial capital of northwestern Europe. But its fortunes began to turn
at the end of the sixteenth century. In August 1585 the city fell to the
Spanish after a year-long siege. The devout Roman Catholic Phillip
offered Protestants the opportunity to convert within four years or
leave. In a meantime, Dutch forces had blocked Antwerp\'s access to the
North Sea. The city is located along the Scheldt River. And primary
access to the North Sea in the sixteenth century (and today) was through
the Western Scheldt Estuary. The Dutch controlled this estuary. At the
Peace of Münster (1648), this blockade was formalized under
international law as part of a peace deal between Spain and the Dutch
Republic. It was in place until the invasion of Napoleon in 1795.

The capture of Antwerp by the Spanish and the resulting blockade led to
a massive exodus of merchants, financiers, artisans, and others to the
north, particularly to Amsterdam. Among them was, for instance, Frans
Hals (1582–1666) who fled as a young boy with his parents to Haarlem
and became one of the foremost painters of the Dutch Golden Age.
Immigrants from elsewhere in the southern Low Countries also settled in
the north during this period. While northern areas of the Low
Countries—particularly Holland and Zeeland—were already prosperous
by the end of the Middle Ages, the capital, knowledge, and networks that
were brought by southern immigrants and the lasting blockade of the
Western Scheldt Estuary had the result that economic activity shifted
decisively to the north, particularly to Amsterdam. It is in the wake of
these events that significant financial innovation in exchange trading
took place in the Dutch Republic.

## 2. Exchange trading in stocks, futures, and options

Throughout the sixteenth century, the Portuguese were the main middlemen
for the Dutch in obtaining goods from Asia. The Portuguese trade system
was inefficient. In 1580, Phillip II also ascended to the Portuguese
throne—an event known as the Iberian Union—while the Dutch Republic
had an ongoing war of independence with Spain. Many Dutchmen also had
extensive information about Portuguese trading practices. All these
factors made the Dutch become particularly keen on sidelining Portuguese
middlemen in the Asia trade.[^13] Traditionally, Dutch maritime trade
had focused on the Baltic Sea and the Atlantic coast. But in the late
sixteenth century, Dutch maritime trade was expanding significantly to
Italy, the Levant, Russia, West Africa, and the Caribbean. And in the
last decade of the sixteenth century, various companies arose to trade
directly with Asia.[^14]

The earliest Dutch companies for conducting trade directly with Asia are
known as **precompanies** (*voorcompagnieën*) because they arose before
the foundation of the VOC. They were joint capital investments for
purchasing merchandise as well as outfitting and operating ships, the
union typically lasting an expedition. These precompanies typically sent
multiple ships on single expeditions, a practice that had become more
common with the growth of the West African trade.[^15] While the details
are scarce, the precompanies are known to have consisted of both passive
and active investors reminiscent of a *commenda* or a limited
partnership. But they seem to have combined this arrangement with
transferrable shares similar to those of the *partenrederijen*.[^16]
These shares, for both types of investors, were understood to have been
subject to limited liability.[^17] Supposedly, these precompanies
typically had a few dozen shareholders.[^18]

The VOC was a horizontal merger between six of the precompanies.[^19]
The precompanies stemmed from Amsterdam, Delft, Rotterdam, Enkhuizen,
Middelburg, and Hoorn. These cities were all located in Holland, except
for Middelburg which was located in Zeeland. Accordingly, the literal
translation of the "Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie" is actually "the
United East India Company". Politicians and the States General of the
Dutch Republic played a crucial role in the company's formation. They
believed it could spur much needed tax income for the war with the
Spanish via a boom in trade as well as more generally support
competition against Spain and Portugal.

Unlike the precompanies, the VOC was intended as a continuing operation.
It was granted a charter for twenty-one years by the States General,
which included a monopoly over the trade with Asia as well as rights to
build fortifications, conduct diplomacy, organize local governance
structures, and engage in warfare. Many ships of the VOC carried both
goods and guns. While the charter is not explicit, it describes a
corporate entity that can own assets, transact, and hold privileges.
Guarantees were given against the taxation and confiscation of its
assets.

The charter locked capital into the company until 1612. As taking out an
investment in 1612 was only granted upon finding a new investor to cover
the loss, capital was essentially locked-in for the entire period of the
charter. The investors were not entitled to dividends after each journey
and only received their first one in 1610. It was paid in pepper and
mace, which a number of stockholders apparently refused. A dividend in
1612 was primarily paid in pepper and nutmeg, only 7.5 percent awarded
in cash. These dividends totaled to 162.5 percent of the initial
investment, or 10.4 million guilders.[^20] It seems that many of the
investors into the VOC were not clearly aware that capital would be
locked in.[^21]

A VOC chamber was opened in each of the six cities from which the
precompanies had originated. The main investors marketed the shares to
the public. Foreigners could also purchase shares. The initial share
issuance had an estimated 1,815 subscribers for a capital raise of more
than six million guilders, more than half of it raised in Amsterdam.
Fewer than two hundred investors accounted for half of this sum.[^22]
Both the number of subscribers and the capital raised was far greater
than anything previously seen. Notably, the initial capital raise was
ten times that raised by the EIC. No share certificates were issued;
instead, the shares were managed through a registration system. The
investor base of the company seems to have been quite heterogeneous,
though many of the main investors in the precompanies also invested in
the VOC. The majority were Dutch, many of them merchants that had
migrated from Antwerp such as Hans Thijs (1556–1611) and Isaac Le Maire
(c. 1558–1624).[^23]

As in the precompanies, there were two classes of investors; they were
called *bewindhebbers* and *participanten*. The former had governance
rights and a managing role in their respective chambers. They controlled
all aspects of the business, including the number of ships that would
sail and from where, the merchandise they were to sell and buy, when
dividends were to be allocated, and so on. The *participanten*, in turn,
were investors without information or voting rights. Initially, there
were seventy-six *bewindhebbers* out of almost two thousand
investors.[^24] The *bewindhebbers* of each city's chamber had
substantial overlap with the active partners of the precompany that had
been located in that city. Each chamber selected a board of
governors—these ranged from seven to twenty individuals— from among
the *bewindhebbers*. General policy was determined by a central body
known as the *Heeren XVII* (the seventeen directors). It was filled by a
selecting members of the local governing boards.

A governor-general was appointed to oversee the VOC's operations in
Asia. While several locations were discussed in the early years, the
governor from 1619 to 1623, Jan Pieterszoon Coen (1587–1629),
established the Asian headquarters at Batavia in 1619. Coen was in favor
of an aggressive approach to protecting the VOC's trade interests. The
Dutch fort at Jayakarta had been under siege by local rulers (and
initially also the English) since 1618. Coen used it as an opportunity
to burn the city to the ground in May 1619 and establish Batavia.[^25]
Due to the distances involved and the slow communications, the
governor-generals of the VOC operated with substantial independence.
Increasingly, the VOC also conducted inter-Asian trade.

The continuity in the organization of the VOC and the extent of capital
raised had numerous positive consequences. Unlike its predecessors, the
VOC was able to engage in long-term planning. It had more means for
investment in ships, fortifications, warehouses, and long-term
relationships, even though it was commonly cash-strapped, particularly
in the early days. The VOC was in a better position train personnel,
impose standards on goods and supplies, and to install inspection
regimes. Credit was more easily available. Without the pressure to
quickly pay investors and creditors, the VOC could time the sales of
their merchandise better at auctions. The supply of imports became more
predictable as the VOC's resources allowed to fend off pirates and
hostile powers.

The sale of shares was enabled by the VOC's charter and secondary
markets soon developed around the six chambers.[^26] It was the first
real secondary market for the shares of a joint stock company. More
importantly, however, while secondary markets had certainly existed in
Europe before the VOC, the market for VOC shares quickly displayed
unmatched levels in volume and liquidity. They also displayed an
unprecedented public and impersonal character. From 1602 to 1612, often
more than 0.5% percent of the shares were traded each month in Amsterdam
alone. Until 1607, there were about 100 to 200 trades per year; this
more than doubled from the period of 1607 to 1612.[^27]

Various factors can explain the vibrancy of the secondary market for VOC
equity shares: a primary issuance that was far larger and more
widespread than with any preceding capital raise; the transparency of
the rules surrounding share transfers; the fact that shareholders were
to make payments for their shares in four installments over as many
years, which fostered speculation on the price; and the lure of the
Asian trade. The liquidity of VOC shares, daily price quotations, the
company's public standing, and the ease with which ownership of shares
could be established led VOC shares to being increasingly used as
collateral for loans. Before this time, municipal and state annuities,
which were highly illiquid, had commonly served as collateral. The drop
in short-term interest rates early in the seventeenth century is
probably partially attributable to the availability of the highly liquid
VOC shares as collateral.

The VOC did not engage in any further equity raises throughout its
lifetime. But it did actively lend and was the first joint-stock company
to issue corporate bonds to the public. Secondary markets in other
financial contracts also arose around VOC shares.

Forward contracts have a long history. Chinese rice producers and
merchants were apparently already using them over 4,000 years ago for
rice sales.[^28] At roughly the same time as the ancient Chinese rice
producers and merchants, several thousands of miles away, a business man
in Ancient Mesopotamia made a forward agreement with three farmers to
pay a certain amount of money for various crops upon harvest or at the
conclusion of their business.[^29] In the fourth century BC, Aristotle
recounts the story of Thales the Milesian, who successfully speculated
on the olive harvest by locking in low prices for the use of olive
presses.[^30]

But while forward contracts have a long history, the shift to trade in
such contracts for purely speculative purposes seems to have arisen only
in Antwerp and Amsterdam at the end of the sixteenth century. During the
sixteenth century, Dutch trade with the Baltic Sea area grew
substantially and Amsterdam became the main market for wheat, rye,
herring, and other bulk commodities. As the volume of trade increased,
commodities were also increasingly becoming standardized and prices were
listed on a daily basis. These conditions increasingly allowed merchants
in Antwerp and Amsterdam to make forward contracts that were cash
settled. They did not cash settle them in a straightforward manner.
Instead, forward contracts had options for cancellation against a
premium. Increasingly deliveries were cancelled and cancellation
premiums were paid instead. The nature of such exchanges was, thus,
purely speculative.[^31]

While the rules with regards to transfers in VOC shares were
transparent, these transfers did entail time and costs. Because of the
installment payments for shares, many investors had also bought more
shares than they intended to hold. Given the experiences with purely
speculative forward contracts on commodity markets, a market for purely
speculative forward contracts naturally developed for VOC shares. And
given the hassle in transfers, these contracts were naturally settled in
cash. As the market in these contracts quickly became extensive, groups
formed that established clearing processes among themselves before
settling of the outstanding balances.

Soon, the first known market for repurchase agreements also seems to
have arisen.[^32] With a **repurchase agreement** or **repo** one party
sells a security to another party with a promise to buy it back for a
slightly higher price at a later date. The difference in price defines
an inherent interest rate, which is commonly called the **repo rate**.
While similar to a collateralized loan, ownership of the underlying
asset actually exchanges hands in the transaction. The repurchasing
agreement is fundamental to the operations of the modern European
Central Bank and also used by central banks elsewhere.

The transactions in VOC shares, forwards, and other types of contracts
were originally handled in the open air at specific locations in the
city where merchants, brokers, financiers, and others met for engaging
in the commodity trade and its financing. In 1611, however, a dedicated
building was opened in the center of the city, a stone's throw from the
VOC's headquarters. The building was known as the **Beurs van Hendrick
de Keyser**, after its main architect. It was set up in 1611 for similar
purposes as the Antwerp Exchange eighty years earlier. By 1613, all
financial trading with regards to VOC shares had move to the exchange.
It is around this time that the city mandated the use of brokers in
transactions. Brokers had characterized the market for VOC shares almost
immediately. By 1612, there were already 300 such brokers. They not only
bought and sold shares and related contracts, but also handled share
transfers and facilitated private clearing and settlement arrangements.
In 1613, the city made them required intermediaries for transactions.
Over time, they began to offer clearing and settlement services in a
centralized fashion.

By the time trading moved to the exchange, the contracts exchanged also
included standardized forwards, or futures, that were easily sold on the
secondary market. While options were already used by Amsterdam grain
dealers in the 1550s and 1560s, they only seem to have become more
common for VOC shares later on in the seventeenth century.[^33] In any
case, by the middle of the seventeenth century, developed markets
existed for futures and option contracts based on the shares of the VOC,
and to a lesser extent for the WIC. Dutch provinces and municipalities
had traditionally relied mostly on annuities for public financing. Even
though bonds had been introduced in the late sixteenth century, active
secondary markets in government debt only developed during the
seventeenth century in the wake of the development for exchange trading
for VOC shares, bonds, and derivatives. Towards the end of the century,
trading in the shares of English joint stock companies, also became
important.

The **Amsterdam Stock Exchange**—now **Euronext Amsterdam**—traces
its history to the birth of the secondary markets for VOC shares in
1602, and is commonly considered the world's oldest stock exchange.
Amsterdam was also the first place where developed derivative markets
made their appearance. Originally, the futures and options markets were
based on VOC shares, but eventually those contracts were also utilized
in commodity markets. All this was actually more than a century before
the opening of the **Dojima Exchange** in Osaka (1730), a venue commonly
cited as the world's first modern futures exchange.[^34] Shortly after
the foundation of the VOC, in 1609, arguably the world's first central
bank was created in Amsterdam. It was known as the **Amsterdamsche
Wisselbank** and the precursor institution of the modern-day Dutch
Central Bank. These developments occurred in an era where Amsterdam also
became the central city internationally for financing European and
global trade as well as lending to sovereigns. These developments
arguably made Amsterdam the first true international financial center.
It remained in that position until it was surpassed by London, certainly
by the early nineteenth century.[^35]

In London, a developed market for trading in the shares and debt of
joint stock companies only arose at the very end of the seventeenth
century, about a hundred years after it developed in Amsterdam. At that
time, there was an explosion of new share and debt issues from joint
stock companies, including from the Bank of England. While there were an
estimated 15 joint stock companies in the year of the Glorious
Revolution, there were an estimated 140 by 1695.[^36] It is also in this
period that England gradually shifts from short term, personal loans to
the sovereign to long-term, structured public debt. This transformation
is commonly known as the **Financial Revolution**.

Dutch merchants and bankers that moved to London after the Glorious
Revolution apparently had a substantial influence on these developments.
They brought with them the unincorporated joint stock company.[^37] They
also introduced the English to modern derivatives markets.[^38] Dutch
financial expertise about tontines, annuities, and bonds helped William
III mobilize capital for his expensive wars against the French and bring
about the Financial Revolution.[^39] The stock registration and transfer
system for the Bank of England was practically identical to that used by
the Dutch East India Company.[^40] The English also pushed EIC and Bank
of England shares as liquid collateral for lending. This led to a
lowering of the interest rates in the eighteenth century, a result that
was undoubtedly expected on the basis of the Dutch experience a hundred
years earlier.

Before 1696, exchange trading in joint stock shares took place at the
**Royal Exchange**—London's counterpart to the Antwerp Exchange and
the Amsterdam Exchange, established in 1571—and **Exchange Alley**
(now known as **Change Alley**), primarily in its various coffee houses,
including **Jonathan's Coffee House** and **Garraway's Coffee House**.
The stock traders, however, left the Royal Exchange in that year under
somewhat mysterious circumstances, and further conducted their business
in Exchange Alley until 1773. Throughout the eighteenth and the early
nineteenth century, exchange trading in England was dominated by the
securities of a few companies: the Bank of England, the East India
Company, the South Sea Company, and the two insurance companies created
by the *Bubble Act 1719*. It was only starting with the substantial
issues of railway shares in the 1830s that exchange trading moved beyond
a few companies.[^41]

While exchange trading for stocks and derivatives largely developed in
Amsterdam, it is in England that we first see deep long-term capital
markets develop during the eighteenth century. Due to the Dutch
Republic's fragmented political system, national debt was not issued
until the Napoleonic era in the early nineteenth century. So the
issuance of sovereign debt was limited to states and municipalities.
England also saw more activity in the founding of joint stock companies,
even if also limited until the nineteenth century. Even more
importantly, it seems that many of these English companies such as the
EIC and Bank of England were not shy to hold subsequent equity capital
raises. By far the foremost joint stock company in the Dutch Republic,
the VOC, never utilized subsequent equity capital raises for
financing.[^42] By the early nineteenth century, London had become the
undisputed global financial center, a position it held until after World
War II when surpassed by New York.[^43]

While securities trading seems to have occurred on a low scale in the
United States during much of the eighteenth century, the sharp rise in
securities trading came after the Revolution and the subsequent growth
in joint stock companies. In 1790, the origins of the Philadelphia Stock
Exchange were formed. The center of securities trading was the **City
Tavern**, later renamed the **Merchants Coffee House**. Trading shifted
to a dedicated building, the **Merchants' Exchange Building**, in 1834.
In 1792, a group of traders in New York City signed the famous
**Buttonwood Agreement**, which laid the foundation for the New York
Stock Exchange. Trading in New York centered around the famous **Tontine
Coffee House** that opened in 1793, but from 1817 onwards operated in
dedicated buildings. The Boston Stock Exchange was formed in 1834.[^44]

## 3. Growing pains

The ubiquity of the modern corporation can make it easy to forget that
its forerunner, the joint stock company, had an arduous and protracted
struggle in becoming politically, legally, and socially accepted. Bishop
Carleton Hunt, an early twentieth century legal and business historian,
described that struggle as follows in England:

> In fact, the history of the business corporation or joint stock
> company in England during the one hundred and fifty years following
> the \[*Bubble Act 1719*\] is the story of an economic necessity
> forcing its way slowly and painfully to legal recognition in the face
> of strong commercial prejudice in favor of "individual" enterprise and
> in spite of determined attempts of both the legislature and the courts
> to deny it. Several eruptions of company promotion and speculation in
> defiance of the law, and the opening-up of new industries with which
> it was impossible for the individual capitalist to cope, were forces
> which operated finally to break down opposition and to cause
> Parliament, retracing its steps, gradually to erect the legal
> framework of the new form of business organization.[^45]

While the complaints about joint stock companies were diverse, the often
frantic trade in their stocks and derivatives certainly had a
substantial role to play in the negative sentiments towards them. We
should not underestimate the sophistication and capabilities of early
investors in the shares of joint stock companies and related securities.
Nevertheless, from its inception, trading in joint stock company
securities was susceptible to naïve and malevolent schemes, dubious
practices, rumors and innuendo, disinformation campaigns, and irrational
exuberance.

In Amsterdam, it took only a few years for the world\'s first bear raid
to hammer the VOC\'s share price. A **bear raid** is a coordinated
attempt by a group of traders to drive down a stock\'s price in order to
profit from a short position. The modern version works like this: the
group first borrows shares, typically for a fee, and sells them
immediately. This is also known as **short selling**. They then try to
depress the price—often by spreading false or damaging rumors—before
buying the shares back at a lower price to return to the lender,
pocketing the difference.

The Amsterdam bear raid was organized by Isaac Le Maire, the wealthy
merchant that had emigrated from Antwerp and had been the largest
investor in the VOC. He actually had served on the first Heren XVII,
until he was removed due to suspicion of fraud. His group\'s approach
was somewhat different: they entered into forward contracts agreeing to
sell VOC shares at a fixed price for future delivery, settled in cash
rather than through actual share transfers. They then worked to push the
price down—partly by selling real VOC stock they owned, and partly by
spreading rumors—so that by the delivery date they could settle the
contracts at a profit. Crucially, they held far less stock than they had
contracted to deliver, making this a short position in economic terms,
even though no shares were actually borrowed. In the end, Le Maire\'s
raid appears to have had only a limited and temporary effect on the
share price.

The exchange business was often not looked upon kindly due to some of
these types of excesses. Illustratively, a **stock jobber** was the
pejorative term used loosely in England for anyone engaging in trading
activities seen to be excessively speculative or manipulative, often
being reserved, it seems, for people trading on their own account.[^46]

A **bubble** is the common term for a market phenomenon where the price
of a stock or other asset rises far beyond what the asset's fundamental
value warrants, typically due to irrational exuberance, herd mentality,
and (often) manipulation. While the Dutch Republic, particularly
Amsterdam, was at the forefront of modern stock and derivatives trading,
in the history of bubbles, it is better known for the spectacular
**Tulip Bubble** of the 1630s. With regards to securities trading, the
most well-known and spectacular early bubbles involved the already
mentioned English South Sea Company (1711) and the French **Mississippi
Company** (1717). Both bubbles transpired in 1720, the Mississippi
Bubble peaking slightly before the South Sea Bubble.

It is the latter that actually gave rise to the modern understanding of
the term "bubble". In early eighteenth century England, the term
"bubble" actually had a broader meaning as "an insubstantial, delusive,
or fraudulent project or enterprise, especially of a commercial or
financial nature".[^47] After the South Sea Bubble deflated in 1720, the
term "bubble" increasingly became used in its more narrow modern sense
both in England and elsewhere. Shortly after the South Sea bubble, for
example, the Dutch play writer Gijsbert Tijssens wrote a comical play
entitled *De Windhandel, of Bubbles Compagnien*. The Dutch term
*windhandel* (literally "wind trade") still refers to purely speculative
trading, typically at very inflated prices (particularly in the context
of futures, options, and other derivatives). So Tijssen's play describes
the *windhandel*, or how bubbles in stocks and derivatives for companies
arise.

The South Sea Company was created in 1711. It obtained the monopoly
rights to trade with South America and its surrounding waters in
England. The surrounding waters were often called the "South Seas" at
the time, hence the name. In 1713, it also acquired certain rights with
regards to the slave trade from Spain. The main trigger for the bubble
was a scheme between the company and the government—the relevant
legislation passed on April 7, 1720—to swap the majority of the public
debt, which had been increasing rapidly primarily due to wars, for
equity in the company.[^48]

Specifically, the scheme was to convert £31 million pounds of public
debt into company stock, offer a lower rate of interest for the assumed
debt to the government, and to pay the government a one-time fee of £7
million pounds. The government would benefit from a lower interest rate
and lower administration costs. And the company would stand to receive
debt payments, which would allow it with support of the Sword Blade
Bank, to issue loans. The Bank of England had similarly executed such a
scheme, with success, in 1697. It was during the execution of the scheme
by the South Sea Company, however, that a bubble arose and burst.

At the beginning of the year 1720, South Sea stock traded at slightly
over 100 pounds. It traded at roughly the same price at the end of the
year. But in the middle of the year, it had managed to climb to over
1,000 pounds. At least a hundred imitators tried to coast along in the
share price boom, including a joint stock company "for carrying on an
undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is".[^49]

The episode, at its core, would have probably been remarkably familiar
to any modern long-term Bitcoin observer that has been in the unenviable
position to witness all the fraudulent schemes, hare-brained ideas,
unscrupulous characters, and unfortunate victims that have tainted the
sector for years. As noted by Galbraith,[^50]

> All the predictable features of the financial aberration were here on
> view. There was large leverage turning on the small interest payments
> by the Treasury on the public debt taken over. Individuals were
> dangerously captured by belief in their own financial acumen and
> intelligence and conveyed this error to others. There was an
> investment opportunity rich in imagined prospects but negligible in
> any calm view of the reality. Something seemingly exciting and
> innovative captured the public imagination, in this case the joint
> stock company\.... And as the operative force, dutifully neglected,
> there was the mass escape from sanity by people in pursuit of profit.

The standard view among economic historians follows the conclusions of a
Parliamentary enquiry that "Company officials bought, or already held,
large quantities of South Sea stock, worked behind the scenes to push
the market price higher, and then sold out a large proportion of their
holdings at the top of the market."[^51] Various strategies have been
proffered as supporting the artificial price inflation. In modern
parlance, the company officials were thought to have executed a **pump
and dump**.

According to Kleer,[^52] however, the standard interpretation is
probably wrong. The existence of some of the strategies mentioned in the
literature to pump up the price enjoys little support from the available
evidence. Other strategies—including lending company money to
investors for stock purchases as well as the company purchases of stock
and stock options at inflated prices—are better explained by the need
to keep the price of company stock high during the execution of the debt
for equity swap.

Specifically, to manage the swap, the Company set out to arrange various
stock issuances in 1720. In some of these issuances, stock would be
swapped for government debt. In other issuances, subscribers could
purchase their shares with money on the basis of a down payment. The
latter issuances were intended to cover the £7 million pound payment to
the government. As the debt was swapped in various issuances, the
company would have been motivated to prevent price declines during the
period of the conversions, otherwise debt holders might not have
converted in later rounds. As money subscribers could make a purchase
with only a down payment, the company would also have needed to keep the
price high to ensure they could raise the 7 million pound payment to the
government.

The Mississippi Bubble displays many similarities to the South Sea
Bubble. The French Mississippi Company had been granted trading rights
to French Louisiana in 1717. The stocks and other securities of the
French Mississippi Company were traded initially on Rue Quincampoix, but
later also in other locations in Paris. Even more so than the South Sea
Company, the Mississippi Bubble seems to have been the result of an
ill-conceived scheme of financial engineering, one, like the South Sea
bubble, involving the exchange of equity for public debt, as well as
rampant paper money printing. At the heart of the scheme was a colorful
Scotsman known as **John Law** (1671–1729).[^53]

The schemes, rumors, irrational exuberance, and tainted practices that
surrounded the trading of joint stock companies had long-lasting
deleterious consequences on the general impression of them. More than
two hundred years after their first appearance, the father of *laissez
faire* economics, Adam Smith, believed that the main reason any joint
stock company ever had success was because of their monopoly rights, and
suggests that otherwise the joint stock company is fundamentally flawed.

> The trade of a joint stock company is always managed by a court of
> directors. This court, indeed, is frequently subject, in many
> respects, to the controul of a general court of proprietors. But the
> greater part of those proprietors seldom pretend to understand any
> thing of the business of the company; and when the spirit of faction
> happens not to prevail among them, give themselves no trouble about
> it, but receive contentedly such half yearly or yearly dividend, as
> the directors think proper to make them. This total exemption from
> trouble and from risk, beyond a limited sum, encourages many people to
> become adventurers in joint stock companies, who would, upon no
> account, hazard their fortunes in any private copartnery. Such
> companies, therefore, commonly draw to themselves much greater stocks
> than any private copartnery can boast of\.... The directors of such
> companies, however, being the managers rather of other people's money
> than of their own, it cannot well be expected, that they should watch
> over it with the same anxious vigilance with which the partners in a
> private copartnery frequently watch over their own. Like stewards of a
> rich man, they are apt to consider attention to small matters as not
> for their master's honour, and very easily give themselves a
> dispensation from having it. Negligence and profusion, therefore, must
> always prevail, more or less, in the management of the affairs of such
> a company.[^54]

## 4. General incorporation with limited liability

In modern legal regimes, commercial corporations are typically created
and managed through a registration regime. Limited liability is
typically a default attribute of the modern corporation. England was the
forerunner with introducing such a system during the nineteenth century.

The general registration regime was introduced into English law by the
*Joint Stock Companies Act* *1844*.[^55] While widely applicable, it did
exclude a few types of industries such as banking, insurance, railways,
and public utilities.[^56] The act defined a "joint stock company" as
any association with more than twenty-five members, or as one with
freely transferrable shares. Any such company was subject to the
exclusive right and obligation to register and, in virtue of that
registration, would obtain corporate status. Special arrangements were
made for existing joint stock companies that met the requirements,
whether established by charter, act of parliament, letters patent (which
had been available since 1834),[^57] or some unincorporated legal
approach.

The *Joint stock Companies Act 1844* established an expansive regulatory
regime, including many regulations applicable to corporations today,
such as the mandated provision of a **prospectus** to share with stock
subscribers, and the required keeping of books of account and balance
sheets by the directors. As summarized by Stern,[^58] the act replaced
"a political process of the granting of select rights and privileges
with a bureaucratic and administrative one". The act was immediately
followed by the *Joint Stock Companies Winding-Up Act 1844*,[^59] which
for the first time enabled creditors to dissolve insolvent joint stock
companies. Before that time, incorporated joint stock companies at least
could only be dissolved by the Monarchy or Parliament. And companies
that were insolvent would often continue to operate. Typically in modern
legal regimes, companies may file for bankruptcy voluntarily, but
creditors may also initiate involuntary bankruptcy proceedings against
them.

Why was the registration regime introduced for commercial corporations?

As already mentioned, in 1720 there was a dramatic rise and crash in the
prices of the shares, futures, and options of the South Sea Company, an
event known as the South Sea Bubble. At roughly the same time,
unincorporated joint stock companies were officially banned in England
by the *Bubble Act 1719*. The act actually received royal assent and
became law shortly before the bursting of the financial bubble in 1720.

The *Bubble Act 1719* was marked, however, by legal ambiguity and weak
enforcement. The law only led to a single prosecution in the eighteenth
century.[^60] Supposedly unincorporated joint stock companies largely
continued to operate, even if enthusiasm in them had been dampened by
the bursting of the South Sea Bubble.[^61] The primary reason was, of
course, the costs, duration, and political lobbying involved in
obtaining corporate status through a parliamentary act, as well as the
limitations of partnerships for setting up businesses with large capital
requirements. The uncontrollable and incessant popularity of
unincorporated joint stock companies, particularly after a boom of new
stock offerings in 1824–5,[^62] seems to have been the predominant
motivation for the repeal of the *Bubble Act 1719* in 1825.[^63]

The repeal meant that unincorporated joint stock companies were now
regulated by the Common Law. Here they seem initially to have had an
unclear status. But after several favorable rulings in the Common Law
courts towards unincorporated joint stock companies,[^64] the *Joint
stock Companies Act 1844* was passed. At this point, however, joint
stock companies that obtained corporate status through registration
still had unlimited liability. By contrast, joint stock companies that
had been incorporated by royal charter or private act of Parliament
indeed sometimes enjoyed (partial) limited liability. The Bank of
England, for example, was understood to have this attribute by
1825.[^65]

At what point the notion and manifestation of limited liability for all
shareholders—whether active in management or not—came into practice
and law is a contested issue. According to Gelderblom and Jonker,[^66]
the Dutch *partenrederijen* that were popular in the fifteenth,
sixteenth, and seventeenth centuries already had limited liability
through maritime and customary law.[^67] Gelderblom, De Jong, and Jonker
contend that limited liability for directors was also introduced to the
VOC in 1623, largely as a product of the financial constraints within
which the company operated.[^68] The VOC is sometimes dubbed the world's
first modern corporation by scholars, precisely because it is believed
to have first acquired all the relevant main features, including
corporate status; deep secondary markets for its shares, futures, and
options; permanently locked-in capital; a separation between ownership
and management with many passive owners; strong entity shielding; and
both shareholder and directorial limited liability. Other joint stock
companies, such as the EIC, then are thought to have adopted all those
features. In this vein, for example, Hansmann, Kraakman, and Squire
claim that "\[a\]n important implication of the English and Dutch
chartered joint stock companies is that commercial firms had been
established by the early seventeenth century with all the elements of
the modern business corporation: strong entity shielding, limited
liability, and tradable shares".[^69]

By contrast, Harris,[^70] quite convincingly, contends that the notion
and relevance of limited liability for shareholders only began to arise
around 1800. Limited liability implies that creditors cannot make claims
on the private assets of any of the shareholders in case of insolvency;
they can only make claims on the pool of corporate assets. Harris
contends that limited liability in this modern sense can only exist
where business corporations have both equity investors and long-term
creditors, are subject to the possibility of insolvency, operate under
procedures for liquidating insolvent corporations, and afford creditors
legal priority over shareholders in claims to corporate assets. Before
the nineteenth century, so Harris claims, these conditions were
generally not present, so the whole idea of shielding owners from
creditors was generally not yet relevant.

Of the VOC, for instance, Harris contends that there was no evidence
that a limited liability regime was anywhere defined, nor that limited
liability was a possible attribute separate from the legal personality
attribute. He also notes that "\[w\]hen the VOC became insolvent in
1799, the Dutch government revoked its charter and nationalized the
company's territorial possessions in Asia, its assets and debts".[^71]
According to Harris, this shows that any notion of limited liability was
just not relevant throughout the VOC's history. The EIC only began
borrowing significantly right before the Glorious Revolution. Nowhere in
its charter is the idea of limited liability laid out. After the Indian
Rebellion of 1857, the Crown assumed control over the company's
possessions and armed forces in India.

According to Harris, only at the end of the eighteenth century did a
notion of limited liability originate in England with practical
relevance. One of the first traces is within unincorporated insurance
companies that tried to limit the liability of shareholders who could
not shield behind the legal personhood of the company. As incorporation
by private acts of Parliament had become more common—which allowed for
more negotiation in charters—clauses about the limited liability of
shareholders also began to appear in corporate charters. Limited
liability became an important motivation for incorporation. As
registration regimes for incorporation developed during the nineteenth
century, they also allowed for the legal formalization of liability
regimes. In the 1850s, the United Kingdom adopted a regime where
corporations could choose from a continuum between unlimited liability
and full limited liability, and the vast majority chose some
intermediate position. Only in the twentieth century, did full limited
liability in the modern sense become the universal standard.

While the history of the introduction of the notion of limited liability
and its early manifestations is somewhat unsettled, the introduction of
general access to limited liability clearly transpired during the
nineteenth century in England. As stated, the *Joint Stock Companies Act
1844* only granted unlimited liability to companies that met the
registration requirements. But that changed with the *Limited Liability
Act* *1855*.[^72] It offered to all companies that met the registration
and other requirements—including the requirement to have more than
twenty-five owners or freely transferrable shares—various possible
regimes with regards to limited liability. The act was quickly repealed,
but re-enacted in the *Joint Stock Companies Act* *1856*.[^73]

The *Joint Stock Companies Act* *1856* also simplified the process of
registration and extended that right to companies of seven owners or
more. It really set into motion the increasing availability of the
corporate legal form to associations that were not economically
organized as joint stock companies—with freely transferrable shares
and many owners—but rather as private companies. Small partnerships
and even proprietorships increasingly utilized the legal form of a
corporation with limited liability by skirting the requirement of seven
members.[^74] Towards the end of the nineteenth century, the notion of a
privately held corporation—characterized by few members, no public
solicitation for capital, and transfer restrictions on
shares—increasingly became recognized.[^75] By 1925, Ireland claims,
86,065 companies out of a total of 95,055 companies in the United
Kingdom were private.[^76]

For the banking sector in England, access to incorporation by
registration and limited liability followed a separate legal trajectory
though on a similar timeline.

In 1697, the Bank of England was given the exclusive monopoly right to
act as an incorporated bank.[^77] That, however, still granted a lot of
room for banks to operate as partnerships and unincorporated joint stock
banks. So, in 1709, all banks were prohibited from issuing bank notes in
England and Wales, if they had more than six partners.[^78] The purpose
of the **six-partner rule** was to stymie the competition to the Bank of
England from unincorporated competitor banks. The exclusivity to
incorporation and the six-partner rule granted the Bank of England a
virtual monopoly on the issuance of bank notes, the primary activity of
commercial banks at the time.

These privileges were granted to the Bank of England in exchange for
government access to credit from it.[^79] While that may sound repugnant
to more enlightened modern minds, such *quids pro quo* were hardly
uncommon in pre-modern society. Merchant bankers like the Medici, for
example, who dominated the European commercial and financial landscape
from the late middle ages to the early modern era, were the principal
lenders to sovereigns. As Kohn notes,

> The profitability of sovereign lending did not, however, derive solely
> or even principally from the economies of intermediation. The main
> reward, especially for large loans, generally came in the form of
> favors the government granted the merchant bank in return for its
> lending–commercial concessions, monopolies, and tax farms.[^80]

The restriction on incorporated banks and the six-partner rule were both
lifted in 1826 for banks operating outside of a 65 mile radius around
London through the *Country Bankers Act 1826*.[^81] This paved the way
for both incorporated and unincorporated joint stock banks, though
growth came exclusively from the latter. The number of joint stock
country banks was estimated at thirty-three by 1833.[^82] In 1833, the
Bank of England's charter was renewed with the *Bank of England Act
1833*.[^83] It also allowed joint stock banks of any type within the
London area, as long as they did not issue notes. In fact, the act also
made Bank of England notes legal tender. The number of joint stock banks
grew from 48 in 1834 to 111 in 1836, though most of that growth was
outside of London.[^84]

By 1844, English joint stock banks other than the Bank of England
remained beholden to joint and several liability. All but the Bank of
England were unincorporated.[^85] In that year, Parliament, then, made
banking extremely restrictive by requiring any new bank with more than
six partners to incorporate via a private act of parliament or letters
patent with the *Joint Stock Banks Act 1844*.[^86] There were also
various other regulations specified for banks, including capital
requirements. In combination with the restrictions on note issuance
within the London area, very few banks formed in this era. According to
Grossman,[^87] only seven new joint stock banks formed between 1844 and
1857, compared to well over one hundred in the previous thirteen year
period. In 1857, the Joint Stock Banks Act 1844 was repealed. Banks were
finally granted access to the same registration regime with
incorporation and limited liability as English companies by 1858.[^88]

Few banks seems to have taken advantage of the limited liability regime.
According to Acheson,[^89] setting aside the Bank of England, in 1869,
England and Wales had 73 unlimited liability joint stock banks, while
only 41 with (partial) limited liability. Apparently, there was a
conviction among bank shareholders and depositors that the attribute of
unlimited liability formed a constraint on excessive risk taking. It was
only after the spectacular collapse of the **City of Glasgow Bank** in
1878 and the collapse of several other banks in its wake, that those
sentiments abruptly changed. English Parliament also fostered conversion
to limited liability regimes through the *Companies Act 1879*.[^90] The
act allowed for a 'reserve liability', a sum of capital that could only
be called upon in a case of liquidation due to insolvency. By 1889, only
two banks in England and Wales had unlimited liability. Over one hundred
enjoyed (partial) limited liability.[^91]

These developments towards making incorporation with limited liability
accessible through registration was largely also a nineteenth century
phenomenon in the rest of Europe as well as in the United States. Given
that incorporation was, and still is, largely the prerogative of the
state governments, the United States' progression towards general
incorporation and limited liability regimes was much more piecemeal than
in England.

The joint stock company was slow to develop in the United States, either
in incorporated or unincorporated form.[^92] While corporate charters
were granted to public and semi-public institutions such as churches,
colleges, and libraries, before the Revolution they were not granted to
commercial businesses. At least one reason was that chartering was
generally considered the prerogative of the Crown, and obtaining a
charter would have been difficult and costly. There are some examples of
unincorporated joint stock companies in Colonial America, but they
appear to be relatively few.

After its battle for independence, however, United States state
legislatures were, in contrast to the English Parliament, extremely
liberal with granting corporate charters. By 1800, over three hundred
charters had been granted.[^93] Many of these charters also pertained to
joint stock banks, so, in this regard, the United States banking system
developed earlier than in England, where prohibitions on joint stock
banking were only lifted by the *Country Bankers Act 1826*. While the
federal government generally did not grant incorporation through
legislation, two notable exceptions by 1800 are in the realm of banking.
The **Bank of North America** was chartered in 1781 at the start of the
Confederation period (1781–1789) by the Continental Congress (and later
also by the state of Pennsylvania). The **First Bank of the United
States** (1791–1811) was also chartered in 1791 by the US Congress.
Unincorporated banks also arose during this period, but such initiatives
increasingly became prohibited by legal statutes from the early
nineteenth century onwards.

While the American states were much more generous than the English
Parliament in granting incorporation, the practice led to some of the
same problems: slow and costly procedures, and serious problems with
corruption. In large part due to such concerns, the United States
gradually also moved to general incorporation with limited liability
throughout the nineteenth century.[^94] A New York law from 1811, which
allowed for the general incorporation of manufacturing companies, is
generally regarded as the first step in that transition in the United
States and globally. By 1860, most states had in place general
incorporation laws for manufacturing companies with (partial) limited
liability.[^95]

For banks, statutes for incorporation by registration first appeared in
the 1830s. By 1860, over half the states had passed some type of
registration law.[^96] Incorporation at the national level, then, became
available through the *National Banking Act of 1864*.[^97] The law
implemented what is known as a **double liability regime**, where
shareholders risk their initial investment and an additional amount up
to that initial investment in the case of bankruptcy.[^98] This type of
liability regime was the most common between 1863 and 1913, and all but
a handful of states implemented it at least some of the period. Some
states also had triple liability regimes and a few had unlimited
liability. By 1930, still only four states maintained full limited
liability regimes. The shift towards that regime mostly occurred in the
1930s in the wake of the Great Depression.[^99]
