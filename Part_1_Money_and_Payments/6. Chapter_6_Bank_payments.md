# Chapter 5: Bank payments

Suppose that you are on holiday in Rome. After a long walk through the Roman Forum and Palentine Hill, you really feel like having a nice cold coke. You walk into a corner grocery store and grab a can from the fridge. It costs €1. You, then, walk up to the cash register and hand the cashier a €1 coin. You walk out the store. At this point, the corner store owns what was previously your €1 coin and you own the coke. 

Setting aside fundamental questions about the nature of money and economic exchanges, a **cash payment**—that is, a payment where coins and notes are transferred from the payer to the payee—is a fairly simplistic act. Almost everyone clearly understands what happens when someone buys a coke with a €1 coin, even though they probably rarely give it a second thought. 

Suppose now instead that you had bought the coke with a credit or debit card. Do you understand exactly how your payment would have been made to the corner store? Few people do. Yet, to really make sense of Bitcoin and why it matters, you need a solid understanding of the players, processes, and systems that facilitate such conventional payment methods.    

To build that understanding, we explore payments from a United States perspective over the next two chapters. Occasionally, we will reflect on arrangements elsewhere. In this first chapter on payments, we explore how payments are made between accounts at banks and credit unions.<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> The next chapter focuses on more complex payment methods, primarily those involving physical payment cards. 

We begin in *Section 1* with a conceptual discussion on payments and payment methods. In *Section 2*, we lay out a key architectural concept required to understand bank payments, namely the correspondent banking system. We, then, in *Section 3*, introduce the bank payment, whereby we mean payments between accounts at banks and credit unions (as described in Chapter 1). We distinguish between two types of bank payments: credit and debit transfers. In *Section 4*, we discuss banking and payments in the early Republic. In *Sections 5*, *6*, *7*, and *8*, we analyze the main players, processes, and systems involved with domestic bank payments in the United States. In *Section 9*, we cover cross-currency bank payments. 


## 1. Payments and payment methods

A **payment** is the transfer of an amount of money from one party to another. Typically, payments are made in exchange for goods or services: a customer that pays for their groceries at a checkout; a student that pays via Amazon’s online portal for some textbooks; an employer that pays out a salary to an employee; and so on. But payments can also constitute donations, gifts, fines, tax payments, and so on.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup>

We commonly refer to the party from whom the money is transferred as the **payer**, and to the party that receives the money as the **payee**. Within economic exchanges for goods and services, or in the context of some other type of debt settlement, we can also refer to these parties as the **debtor** and **creditor**, respectively. A party to a payment—whether the payer or the payee—can be a person or group of people, a business, an organization, or a government agency. 

We can distinguish between various general schemes for making payments. These are commonly called **payment methods**. As already mentioned, a cash payment is a scheme where coins and notes are transferred from the payer to the payee. Typically, the transfer is made by physically handing the cash over. But in some cases, the transfer can also be made more indirectly such as through a cash drop box or the mail. 

There are a number of other types of payment methods than the cash payment, including *schemes that use* personal or other types of checks; money orders; (physical) debit and credit cards; Bitcoin and other cryptocurrency systems; online banking applications; digital wallets such as Apple Wallet and Google Wallet; and so on. 

The term payment instrument is sometimes used synonymously with payment method. But that is a somewhat awkward use of language. Instead, it is better to see a payment method as some general scheme involving objects and actions, while a **payment instrument** is just a specific type of physical or digital object involved in such a scheme: a credit or debit card; a personal or other type of paper check; a paper money order; a digital wallet or banking app; and so on. Hence, why we utilized the expression “schemes that use” in the previous paragraph to describe payment methods. 

Let’s now first turn to a core architectural concept relevant to bank payments (as well as other types of payment methods): the correspondent banking system.  


## 2. The correspondent banking system

A **correspondent banking arrangement** is typically understood as an arrangement in which one bank offers an account and accompanying services to another bank. The bank which offers the service is known as the **correspondent bank**, while the bank that uses the service is known as the **respondent bank**. 

From the perspective of the correspondent bank, the account offered to the client bank is known as a **vostro account** (from the Latin meaning “your”). From the perspective of the respondent bank, the account is called a **nostro account** (from the Latin meaning “our”).

Finally, correspondent banking arrangements are also important to facilitating access to intra-currency payment systems.

These banks will have various bilateral correspondent banking arrangements with other large, global banks, and offer correspondent services to smaller banks. 

As we will learn later, many banks have accounts with a central bank to facilitate payments. However, we would typically not call the central bank a correspondent bank in these arrangements. The concept of correspondent banking is typically reserved for such arrangements between commercial banks. 


## 3. Bank payment

By a **bank payment**, we simply mean a payment that is completed by a direct transfer of money from one account at a bank or credit union to another bank or credit union. For convenience, we will just speak about banks and bank payments from hereon out. But it should be understood that all of the discussion mostly also applies to credit unions. 

Our illustrations around bank payments will generally leverage consumers and businesses as payees. But it should be understood that a bank payment can also have government agencies and banks in the role of payers and payees. Banks continually make payments to one another as a part of lending arrangements, purchases of securities, and so on. 

We need to distinguish carefully between bank payments and money transfers between banks. Banks play a crucial role in most payment methods and are, therefore, at the core of the American payments infrastructure. Hence, a money transfer between two banks is often a leg of some larger chain of events in a payment, rather than a payment *pur sang*. That is, while all bank payments involve money transfers between banks, not every money transfer between banks is a payment. 

To illustrate, consider the example in *Figure X* below. Bank A seeks to make a payment to bank C. But they have no direct correspondent relationship with bank C that would facilitate such a payment. So, bank A transfers some money to bank B, who, in turn, transfers some money to bank C. The payment is made from bank A to bank C. But that payment is facilitated by money transfers from Bank A to bank B, and bank B to bank C. 

In practice, of course, people will not carefully distinguish in this manner between money payments and money transfers. So a bank payment, in our strict sense of the expression, is commonly just called a bank transfer or a payment by bank transfer. The distinction is made here merely for extra clarity in the discussions to follow. 

For the purposes of our survey, it is useful to distinguish between two general forms of bank payments: credit transfers and debit transfers. 

A **credit transfer** is a payment from the payer’s bank account to that of the payee, which is initiated by the payer’s bank. Credit transfers are typically made via a web-based or mobile banking application. But traditional methods are still utilized as well, such as placing an order at a local bank branch or arranging the transfer over the telephone. When a credit transfer is electronically automated, it is often called a **direct credit**.

Credit transfers are sometimes part of a standing arrangement set up by the payer. Any such standing order is convenient in situations where payments of a specific amount have to be made regularly. Good examples are rent payments and automatic deposits into savings accounts from a salary. 

A **debit transfer** is a payment from the payer’s bank account to that of the payee, which is initiated by the payee’s bank. Typically debit transfers occur as part of a standing arrangement that was initiated by the payer signing a certain type of contract with a service provider such as a phone or electric company. The contract allows the service provider to deduct variable amounts periodically from the payer’s account. When a debit transfer is electronically automated, it is often called a **direct debit**. 

One major distinction in bank payments is that between the intrabank and the interbank payments. The **intrabank payment** (also known by various other names such as a **book transfer** or an **on-us transfer**) is simply a payment where both the payer and payee utilize the same bank. The payment can constitute either a credit or a debit transfer, the two general forms of the bank transfer we identified above.     

Let’s illustrate the mechanics of an intrabank payment with an example. Suppose that Bob, residing in Houston, and Alice, residing in Boston, are two friends. On his last visit to Boston, Bob lost his wallet and Alice was kind enough to lend him $200 in cash. He would now like to pay her back. It just so happens that Bob and Alice both have an account at Citibank. 

So, Bob opens up his mobile banking application. From within his checking account, he now creates a transaction where he specifies the amount ($200), Alice’s name and bank account details, and a thank you message. Suppose that Bob initially has $453 in his bank account, while Alice has $419. After Bob presses “send”, the following essentially transpires:

* Citibank’s IT infrastructure for payment processing will receive the transaction from Bob’s mobile banking application and perform various checks on it: Can we really be assured that it’s Bob making this payment? Is there anything suspicious about this payment? Does Bob have sufficient funds or at least a sufficiently large credit limit to complete it? And so on.
* If the transaction passes all those controls, Citibank will deduct $200 from Bob’s checking account and add $200 to Alice’s checking account. So Bob’s resulting balance will be $253 and Alice’s $619. While fees may sometimes be charged for intrabank transfers, that is not very common in consumer banking, particularly in developed economies. Typically, the costs of intrabank payment services (as well as several other types of bank payment services) are indirectly covered by monthly fees. 
* In the background, Citibank will update various other electronic records. The details of the transaction, for example, will be recorded somewhere in a database.

Importantly, in the discussion of our example above, we implicitly assumed “Citibank” specifically meant Citibank, N.A., the national bank in the United States, headquartered in New York City. As explained in *Chapter 1*, most of the well-known banking brands are actually part of complex legal organizational structures. In this case, the national bank is actually owned by a holding company called Citigroup, which (ultimately) also owns many other companies within the financial services industry. 

Why does all this matter for our discussion on intrabank payments? 

Because it is important to understand that intrabank payments only typically take place at the level of a singular legal entity. A transfer between a bank account at Citibank, N.A., and a bank account at Citibank UK, for instance, would *not* actually be an intrabank payment. While both entities are owned by the same holding company, Citigroup, these subsidiaries have separate operations, accounting systems, and so on. So any payment between them would technically be an *interbank payment*, rather than an *intrabank payment*. 

An **interbank payment**, in other words, is just a bank payment where the payer and payee have accounts at different banks (even though those banks may have similar brands). 

To see how an interbank payment could be made, let’s consider the example of Bob and Alice again. But this time assume that, contrary to above, Alice has her bank account with Winchester Savings Bank. In order to send Alice $200, Bob’s payment now has to travel between Citibank and Winchester Savings Bank. How are these types of interbank transfers realized in the United States?

One way to facilitate them would be through a well-developed correspondent banking system. If sufficiently interconnected, any US bank would always be able to discover a route for making a payment to another bank.  

Suppose, for instance, that Winchester Savings Bank had an account with Citibank. The latter, in other words, served as a correspondent bank for the former.

In this case, all that would be required, ignoring any possible fees, is for Citibank to deduct $200 from Bob’s account and add $200 to Winchester Savings Bank’s account, and then for Winchester Savings Bank to add $200 to Alice’s account. ## 4. Banking and payments in the early Republic

As mentioned in *Chapter 1*, institutional banks—those that really embraced all the core activities and services of banking—really did not develop in the United States until the end of the eighteenth century. In the Confederate Period and the early days of the Republic, three of these institutions were national banks. 

The **Bank of North America** was chartered in 1781 at the start of the Confederation period (1781–1789) by the Continental Congress (and later also by the state of Pennsylvania). The bank was majority-owned by the government. It accepted deposits; enabled payments, in particular for the government; issued bills of credit, the first main bills in America that were officially, and not just *de facto*, convertible into specie; and was important to financing of the Revolutionary War.<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup> The bank continued as a state bank in Pennsylvania once the Confederation period ended. 

The **First** and **Second Banks of the United States** were chartered in the early days of the Federal Republic. They operated from 1791 to 1811 and from 1816 to 1837, respectively. 

The First Bank was largely the brainchild of Alexander Hamilton who, in turn, was mainly inspired by the Bank of England that had been established in 1694. Originally, it was owned for 20% by the government. Down payments were made on share subscriptions, and these shares would be fully paid for in specie (25%) and U.S. treasury bonds (75%) over time. Operating out of Philadelphia, other branches were soon opened after its founding in 1791. And by the early nineteenth century, it was the main bank in the United States. 

The bank focused on supporting the government through lending, tax collection, the management of treasury money, facilitating credit payments, and so on. The bank also issued banknotes and credit money on a fractional reserve basis through loans. The notes could be used to pay federal taxes. While focused on servicing the government, the First Bank also supported the commercial sector. It also conducted a crude national monetary policy through the purchase, redemption, and holding of banknotes from state banks. Congress refused to renew the charter for various reasons, including the extent of foreign ownership as well as due to pressure from state banks.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup>

Despite opposition from state banks and speculators on banknotes, the Second Bank was chartered in 1816. The main arguments for it were a stabilization of the currency, managing war debt, and pulling the country out of the economic slump caused by the War of 1812. The bank was again located in Philadelphia. The government again took a twenty percent ownership share and shares had to be paid for in specie (25%) and U.S. treasury bonds (75%).

The Second Bank had many resemblances to the First Bank. It serviced commercial activities, but was also the main bank of the government. It offered the treasury deposit and payment services, supported the raising of public funds, and extended loans to the treasury. The Second Bank also issued banknotes that were useable for paying federal taxes, and it engaged in the same type of crude monetary policy through its own lending policies and the purchasing, redeeming, and holding the notes of state banks. The Second Bank did, however, have a much greater reach than the First Bank. Andrew Jackson had a general distrust of paper money and credit, and also believed (correctly) that the second bank was using it’s influence for political favoritism. After his re-election in 1832, he was the key figure to ensuring that the bank came to an end in 1837.<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup>

All other banks from the late 18th century onwards were state chartered until the second half of the 19th century when the National Banking Acts of 1863 and 1864 were passed.<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> Institutional banks spread rapidly in this period. According to Markham, the number of banks operating in the United States was eighteen in 1794, twenty-nine in 1800, and more than seven hundred in 1837.<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> 

Banks at this time came to be predominantly formed as **joint stock companies** with a focus on financial services. This was a marked difference from the merchants in the Colonial era that were often organized as proprietorships and partnerships, and which typically only engaged in banking as one of their commercial activities. Joint stock companies were legal structures that are generally understood as the precursor to modern limited liability corporations with autonomous, exchange traded shares. This evolution was, according to Poitras,<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup> only completed by the end of nineteenth century. 

Joint stock companies have a history in the English-speaking world that far precedes the late eighteenth century. The settlement of many of the colonies was, in fact, arranged through joint stock companies, such as the Viriginia, Plymouth, and Massachusetts Bay Companies. The use of such companies in long-distance trade in England seems to go back at least to the middle of the sixteenth century.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup> But until the end of the eighteenth century, joint stock companies were primarily used for municipal, charitable, and educational organizations in the colonies, rather than commercial businesses. At least in part, they would have been discouraged because granting corporate charters was considered the prerogative of the Crown.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup> After the Revolution, the number of for-profit corporations grew strongly, to more than 350 by 1800.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup>

You will sometimes hear people draw the distinction between a chartered and an unchartered bank. A **chartered bank**, in this parlance, is just a “bank” that has been granted a charter by the government or a legislature. An **unchartered bank** is just a “bank” without such a charter. With our modern characterization of banks and credit unions in *Chapter 1*, we clearly do not allow for making such a distinction (that is, some institution without a charter is just not a bank). But in the late eighteenth century such a distinction would have still been sensible, as not all joint stock company banks were officially approved. But it is precisely in this period that governments and legislatures started to take control over the banking industry by requiring them to be chartered and incorporated. Massachusetts and New Hampshire prohibited unchartered and unincorporated banks in 1799, and New York passed a similar measure in 1804.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup> Virginia followed suit in 1816.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup>

The bills of credit that were issued and circulated by the provincial governments in the colonial era often experienced inflation and led to a complex system of multiple currencies. As a consequence, the *U.S. Constitution* prohibits states from issuing specie and paper currencies. It also prohibits them from requiring anything other than specie to be considered legal tender. The relevant restrictions are included within *Section 10* of *Article 1* of the constitution, which generally seeks to restrict state rights on various matters.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup>

> No State shall enter into any Treaty, Alliance, or Confederation; grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal; coin Money; emit Bills of Credit; make any Thing but gold and silver Coin a Tender in Payment of Debts; pass any Bill of Attainder, ex post facto Law, or Law impairing the Obligation of Contracts, or grant any Title of Nobility.

The *U.S. Constitution* instead shifted these powers to the federal government. Specifically, it states within *Section 8* of *Article I* that “The Congress Shall have Power ... To coin Money, regulate the Value thereof, and of foreign Coin”.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup> These powers have since been broadly interpreted within the courts. 

What the *U.S. Constitution* did not do was prohibit the issuance of banknotes by banks. And the issuance of banknotes accompanied the rapid growth in state banks in the early days of the Republic. While 28 banks were privately issuing banknotes in 1800, by 1815 that number exceeded 200.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup> These notes were exchangeable for specie, but that promise could often not be honored. Many banks failed in this period as a consequence. Forgeries of these state bank notes was also a common concern. The whole currency system had become incredibly complex.<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup> The frenzy at this time is not dissimilar to the absolute explosion of altcoins and other types of tokens that we have seen since the early days of Bitcoin. The worst offenders in the period after the Second Bank of the United States were typically called **wildcat banks**. 

Notable about the US bank system is that it already offered safekeeping and savings services to the general public by the eighteenth century. In Europe, banking was much more the prerogative of the privileged classes until later in the nineteenth century. In addition, notwithstanding the three national banks in this era, the banking system was highly fragmented. Geography played an important role as did the legal restrictions on banking that were coming into existence, particularly restrictions on interstate branching and even intrastate branching.<sup>[18](#footnote18)</sup>

Both these factors contributed to the rise of checks as a payment instrument in lieu of cash by the early 1800s. In a standard bill of exchange, the drawee first has to sign and accept the bill before it is considered valid. A **check** is, therefore, really just a special type of bill of exchange, namely one where the drawee, the drawer’s bank, automatically accepts the bill as long as certain conditions are met (primarily a sufficient account balance). And checks in the early 1800s were settled purely through correspondent bank balances both locally and interregionally. Opening and maintaining these correspondent accounts required extensive transport of gold and currency.<sup>[19](#footnote19)</sup>

Banks in New York, for example, before 1850 would settle checks on a daily basis. This was a cumbersome process and became more complex over time. The system required porters to be sent around town with a ledger book, checks drawn on other banks, and bags of gold. As described by Gorton,<sup>[20](#footnote20)</sup> “Porters crossed an recrossed each other’s tracks lugging bags of gold, hoping to reach each of the other banks by the end of the day.... But as the number of banks grew, these paths became a tangled web”. In response to this complexity, the banks decided to exchange checks daily, but only to settle on Friday mornings. But that was to little avail. In the words of an observer from the time:<sup>[21](#footnote21)</sup>

> A Porters’ Exchange was held on the steps of one of the Wall Street banks, at which [the porters] accounted to each other for what had been done during the day. Thomas had left a bag of specie at John’s bank to settle a balance, which was due from William’s bank to Robert’s; but Robert’s bank owed twice as much to John’s. What had become of that! Then Alexander owed Robert also, and William was indebted to Alexander. Peter then said that he had paid Robert by a draft from James, which he, James, had received from Alfred on Alexander’s account. That, however, had settled only half the debt. A quarter of the remainder was cancelled by a bag of coin, which Samuel had handed over to Joseph, and he had transferred to David. It is entirely safe to say that the Presidents and the Cashiers of the banks could not have untangled this medley. 

The complexities and inefficiencies of relying purely on correspondent banking relationships assured that such a system would not last. These have result in various core payment systems, which we will turn to next. 


## 5. Payment systems

While nothing prevents American banks from processing domestic interbank transfers purely on the basis of correspondent banking relationships—and on occasion they might still do so—this is not how domestic interbank transfers (and other non-cash payment methods like checks) are standardly processed. Instead, we can identify three distinct **payment systems** through which practically all modern-day interbank transfers are processed in the United States. These are as follows:

1. The **Fedwire Funds Service** (**FedWire**)
2. The **Clearing House Interbank Payments System** (**CHIPS**) 
3. The **Automated Clearing House Network** (**ACH Network**) 

What exactly is a payment system?

That a payment is **settled** means that it cannot be reversed or undone, and that the obligations of the payer to the payee (as defined by the size of the payment) have been unconditionally fulfilled. Settlement in all three systems above occurs through the Federal Reserve System, the central bank of the United States.  

The **clearing** of a payment is, in short, the period in which all the steps necessary to realize for settlement are completed. In the second and third systems above, much of the heavy lifting with regards to clearing the payment is performed by CHIPS and ACH, respectively. These are, correspondingly, called **clearing houses**. Unsurprisingly, payment systems are sometimes also, somewhat more generally, called **clearing and settlement systems**.  

Essentially, the three core payment systems have centralized domestic interbank transfers in the United States. While correspondent banking relationships are still important for many banks to access these systems—particularly FedWire and CHIPS—processing interbank transfers entirely through correspondent banking relationships is no longer standard practice. 

The key role of the Federal Reserve System in all three payment systems is hardly an accident. One of the key reasons for its establishment was precisely to support and monitor payments. This was hardly an anomaly in the history of central banking. Many early public banks were already founded with important roles for centralizing payments, such as Barcelona’s *Taula di Canvi* (1401), Genoa’s *Banco di San Giorgio* (1408), Venice’s *Banco di Rialto* (1587), the Bank of Amsterdam (1609), the Bank of Hamburg (1619), and Nuremberg’s *Banco Publico* (1621). 

Other central banks that were established for different reasons, notably the Bank of England (1694), quickly adopted a role in payments.<sup>[22](#footnote22)</sup> And the Bank of England would hardly have stumbled on that role by accident. Already in 1636, the famous seventeenth century financier, Philip Burlamachi (1575–1644), discussed it with the then Secretary of State Francis Windham. 

> That which he thinks of proposing to the King is the erection of a bank for the payment of all large sums which shall be negotiated. He knows that the same proposal has been formerly made, and that the proposal has been lately renewed. Those who have previously managed the business have had a regard solely to their own profit. Burlamachi’s ends are the King’s service, the public good, and the convenience of merchants.<sup>[23](#footnote23)</sup> 

The best way to understand the three core payment systems in the United States is through examples. We deal, in turn, with FedWire, CHIPS, and ACH. We offer reflections on the global payments landscape throughout. While the global payment landscape does show substantial variety, most intra-currency payments in other countries and regions are indeed also processed through similar systems as in the United States.  


## 6. FedWire

Let’s forget about Alice and Bob for the moment. Instead, let us suppose that Chevron wishes to make a bank transfer of $5M to Halliburton as prepayment for services on one of their oil fields in the Gulf of Mexico. Let’s assume that Chevron has an account with Wells Fargo with a balance of $135M and that Halliburton has an account with Bank of America with a balance of $243M. 

For these types of large payments, the direct use of FedWire would be common. To understand how FedWire works, we first need some background on the Federal Reserve System and master accounts. 

The **Federal Reserve System** is the central banking system of the United States. We also commonly refer to it as the **Federal Reserve** or simply the **Fed**. The system is composed of twelve regional **Federal Reserve Banks** as well as the **Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System** in Washington, D.C. Many countries just have a single central bank, so the Federal Reserve System is fairly decentralized as far as central banking systems go. 

Various types of institutions can hold deposit accounts with the Federal Reserve. These accounts are known as **master accounts**. The funds held within these master accounts are standardly called **reserves** (or sometimes **federal reserve deposits**). 

Obtaining a master account at the Federal Reserve is subject to strict eligibility criteria. Most master account owners are commercial banks and other types of banks and credit unions based in the United States with US ownership. As mentioned in *Chapter 1*, all nationally chartered commercial banks and savings associations are required to be members of the federal reserve. State banks and savings associations have the option. Membership entails that the institution must purchase stock in the Federal Reserve Bank of their region. Besides the right to own master accounts, membership also offers additional rights such as the ability to vote for the directors of their regional Federal Reserve Bank.  

Master accounts are also owned by the US Treasury and US banks (ultimately) owned by foreign holding companies (e.g., HSBC Bank USA, N.A., which is ultimately owned by the HSBC holding company based in the United Kingdom). Other types of entities with master accounts include financial market utilities (e.g., CHIPS and ACH), government-sponsored enterprises, foreign governments, foreign central banks, and international organizations. <sup>[24](#footnote24)</sup>

Let’s now turn back to our example. Not every US bank has a master account with the Federal Reserve. But both Wells Fargo and Bank of America do. For US banks, master accounts are held with the Federal Reserve Bank for the institution’s particular region. So, Wells Fargo, headquartered in Sioux Falls (SD), has a master account with the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis (MN). The Bank of America, headquartered in Charlotte (NC), has a master account with the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond (VA). 

While any institution that has a master account typically also has access to Fedwire, this is not necessarily the case. But in our example, this is indeed the case for both Wells Fargo and Bank of America. Given these circumstances, the FedWire payment from Chevron to Halliburton can now processed as depicted in *Figure X*. 

[Figure X as described above.]

To start, Chevron initiates an interbank transfer through an online banking environment or some other method. Wells Fargo first deducts $5M from Chevron’s deposit account, so that $130M remains. Next, $5M is deducted from Wells Fargo’s master account at the Federal Reserve, while $5M is added to the Bank of America’s master account. The former’s balance changes from $1,200M to $1,195M, while the latter’s changes from $2,632M to $2,637M. Finally, the Bank of America credits Halliburton’s account by $5M, so their final balance is $248M. 

In reality, Chevron would have to a pay a substantial fee for this service. The fee would be allocated downstream indirectly and directly to the participating intermediaries: Wells Fargo, the Federal Reserve, and the Bank of America. For convenience, however, we have left out any consideration of fees, as it does not change the basic players and processes within the example.   

While the twelve Federal Reserve Banks have some operational independence, FedWire operates in a centralized fashion between them. So the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis and the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond do not require a further bilateral relationship with accounts such as would be needed between two commercial banks to make a payment directly between them.   

As soon as the money has been debited from Wells Fargo’s master account and credited to the Bank of America’s master account, the payment is *considered settled*. At this point, the payment can no longer be revoked, and the obligations of Chevron to Halliburton, as defined by the size of the payment, have been unconditionally fulfilled. Were the Bank of America suddenly to become insolvent and Halliburton never to receive their payment, for instance, Chevron could not be held liable. Until the moment that the Bank of America’s master account is credited, the payment is being cleared. With a direct use of the FedWire service that is typically not very long. 

Note that colloquially, people will often state that a transaction is settled once the payee receives their funds. So in this case, that would be when Halliburton actually has their account credited. That, however, is technically incorrect. Settlement occurs at the Federal Reserve and this is so-determined by laws and regulations.  

The Federal Reserve in our example is called the **settlement agent**. It ensures that payments cannot be revoked and that financial obligations are unconditionally fulfilled. As we will see shortly, the Federal Reserve also acts as the settlement agent for transfers that utilize CHIPS and ACH. Hence, while it is possible that sometimes private institutions act as a settlement agent for interbank transfers, the predominant settlement agent is the Federal Reserve.

This is not unique to the United States. Across the globe, Central Banks typically play the role of the settlement agent in payments. And this is hardly an accident.  

As we have already noted, many central banks were partially at least established to centralize payments for the purpose of reliability and efficiency in payments. This role for the central banks has been enshrined in modern practices. 

The main international forum which discusses, monitors, and issues recommendations on the smooth functioning of payment systems is the **Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures** at the **Bank of International Settlements**. It has 28 central banks as its members. One of their core recommendations for systematically important payment systems is that “[a]ssets used for settlement should preferably be a claim on the central bank”.<sup>[25](#footnote25)</sup> Compared to settlement assets provided by private institutions, settlement assets issued by a central bank are argued to be devoid of credit and liquidity risks on the issuing institution: that is, the central bank cannot become insolvent and central bank reserves can always be converted into other liquid assets.<sup>[26](#footnote26)</sup>

Not all banks in the United States have a master account with the Federal Reserve. These banks can only access FedWire indirectly. They would do so through a correspondent bank, which forwards any FedWire transfers to the Federal Reserve and settles the transaction on their behalf.

Suppose, for example, that Halliburton had a bank account with Texas Regional Bank. Furthermore, suppose that the latter utilized the Bank of America for access to FedWire.<sup>[27](#footnote27)</sup> In this situation, Chevron’s FedWire transaction would be processed as in *Figure X* below.

The Federal Reserve offers a public database with all those institutions that have direct and indirect access to its financial services.<sup>[28](#footnote28)</sup> On the basis of the information given, we can assume there are somewhere around 9,000 institutions that utilize the master accounts and financial services offered by the Federal Reserve, either directly or indirectly through correspondent banks. Presumably many of these institutions would utilize FedWire. The best source specifically mentioning the number of institutions utilizing FedWire is from 2014, so somewhat outdated. It claims that around 6,500 institutions utilized FedWire in 2013.<sup>[29](#footnote29)</sup>

Payment systems such as FedWire are known as **real-time gross settlement systems** (**RTGS systems**). These systems settle transactions very quickly. That is, in large part, because they process transactions individually (i.e., “gross”) without **netting**, though some also do have basic netting facilities (in which case they are sometimes called hybrid systems). A sophisticated netting system tallies transactions in the system to make a more simplified set of transactions for settlement. We will discuss the details of this practice more elaborately while exploring CHIPS and ACH in subsequent sections. 

RTGS systems are a common feature of the modern global payments landscape. Within the World Bank’s Global Payment Systems Survey (GPSS) from 2021, 97% of the 94 economies within the survey indicated that they are using at least one RTGS system.<sup>[30](#footnote30)</sup> This was not always the case. FedWire has always been an RTGS system since its inception in 1918.<sup>[31](#footnote31)</sup> The “wire” in the name actually stems from the fact that originally telegraphic communications were used for the system. But the real worldwide growth in these systems has really only taken place over the last few decades.<sup>[32](#footnote32)</sup>

The Federal Reserve owns and operates FedWire. This arrangement is very common globally. By the same light, for instance, the **Clearing House Automated Payment System** (**CHAPS**) is owned and operated by the Bank of England and **TARGET2** is owned and operated by the ECB. The World Bank’s GPSS from 2021 indicates that for the RTGS systems reported in the survey, the central bank was the owner in 93% of the cases and the operator in 88% of the cases.<sup>[33](#footnote33)</sup>


## 7. CHIPS

Real-time gross settlement systems (RTGS systems) are often called **high-value payment systems**. Most high-value payment systems are RTGS systems, but there are some other types of high-value payment systems as well. Within the World Bank’s Global Payment Systems Survey (GPSS) from 2021, 15 out of 94 economies indicated that they also process high-value payments through other systems.<sup>[34](#footnote34)</sup> Most of those alternative systems were reported to work in conjunction with an RTGS system for settlement. One economy reported not to work with an RTGS system, but instead settled with the central bank via some other avenue. <sup>[35](#footnote35)</sup>

The United States has one systematically important payment system for high value payments that is not a real-time gross settlement system: the Clearing House Interbank Payments System (CHIPS). It is offered by a private company, the **Clearing House Payments Company**.<sup>[36](#footnote36)</sup> In 2023, CHIPS had 45 participating banks,<sup>[37](#footnote37)</sup> substantially less than the thousands of banks and other types of institutions that participate in FedWire. But as with Fedwire, financial institutions can indirectly access CHIPS through correspondent banking arrangements with one of its participating banks. A subset of the banks participating in CHIPS actually own the Clearing House Payments Company. 

CHIPS offers real-time settlement to its participants, but, in contrast to Fedwire, does so with the support of sophisticated netting arrangements. Hence, it is not an RTGS system, because payments are not processed individually. The precise operation of CHIPS is captured within detailed guidelines. But roughly, the system works as follows.<sup>[38](#footnote38)</sup>

CHIPS operates from 09.00 to 18.00 each working day. It has a master account with the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The total balance in this account belongs to the participating banks; the individual shares of this balance are specified within the CHIPS ledger. Specifically, all participating banks have two balances registered on the CHIPS ledger to account for the funds in the CHIPS master account: a primary position and a supplemental position.

* At the start of the day, participating banks pre-fund the CHIPS master account using FedWire. These pre-funding amounts for each institution are revised weekly by CHIPS. The pre-funding amount for each institution is recorded as its primary position on the CHIPS ledger. The primary position can only be funded at the beginning of the day, and no withdrawals can be made at any point during the day. So, the primary position only changes as a result of payments being processed. The balance of the primary position cannot be less than zero. 
* Throughout the day, participating banks are allowed to deposit additional funds to the CHIPS master account. These funds are registered in the CHIPS ledger under the institution’s supplemental position. As long as the balance remains non-negative, banks can withdraw from the supplemental position throughout the day. These deposits and withdrawals are made using FedWire. Next to deposits and withdrawals, payment processing through CHIPS can also impact a bank’s supplemental position. As with the primary position, the balance of the supplemental position cannot be less than zero. 

Throughout the day, participating banks send payments to the CHIPS system, either individually or in batches. Each payment eventually affects either the primary positions or the supplemental positions of the banks involved. For each type of payment, CHIPS continually nets payments between the different banks in the system, and updates their primary positions and supplemental positions on the CHIPS ledger accordingly. 

We will not delve into the exact algorithms used by CHIPS for netting. But they rely on both **bilateral** and **multilateral netting**. Examples are shown, respectively, in *Figures X* and *Y* below.

[Figure Y]

In Figure X, Bank of America needs to make three payments to Citibank of $3M, $2M, and $8M, while Citibank needs to make one payment to Bank of America (of $9M). Rather than process four individual payments, as would be the case with Fedwire, a bilateral netting procedure can reduce the burden to just one transfer of $4M from Bank of America to Citibank.

In Figure Y, the required payments are as in Figure X, except that Wells Fargo now also needs to process a payment of $1M to Bank of America and Citibank needs to send payments of $2M and $1M to Wells Fargo. Instead of processing seven payments individually, the participants can use multilateral netting to reduce the burden to only two payments. For example, Bank of America could transfer $2M to Wells Fargo and $1M to Citibank. Another option is for Bank of America to send $3M to Wells Fargo, and for the latter to send $1M to Citibank. 

Both bilateral and multilateral netting procedures as illustrative above are utilized by CHIPS throughout the day. At the end of the day, the primary and supplemental position of each participating bank is combined to help settle any remaining payments in the system. CHIPS, then, in a final procedure allows any participants to send additional funds through Fedwire for any payments that still cannot be released. Any payment not covered in this way at the end of the day is cancelled. The combined position of each participating bank at the end of the day is, then, paid out from the CHIPS master account using FedWire. So all balances reset at the end of each day.    

There are many intricate rules attached to this system and we will not delve into all those details. However, one key principle to emphasize is that no participant can have a negative balance in their primary position, supplemental position, or combined position at the end of the day. CHIPS, in other words, *does not extend any credit*. This means that payouts of the balances on the CHIPS ledger at the end of the day are guaranteed. This, in turn, ensures that any payments made throughout the day which are already reflected in the CHIPS ledger *can be considered settled*. 

So settlement here works differently than for FedWire. The Federal Reserve is the settlement agent for both systems. But for CHIPS, the point of settlement occurs when the CHIPS ledger is updated to reflect a payment. The payouts at the end of the day to the participating institutions only resets their balances on the CHIPS ledger to zero. This is in contrast to Fedwire where an actual transfer of central bank reserves has to occur in order for a payment to settle. 

While CHIPS is a real-time system in virtue of its continuous netting engine, it offers a lower guarantee of immediate settlement than FedWire. But CHIPS also has various advantages. The costs per transaction are lower. In addition, CHIPS makes much lower demands on liquidity in central bank reserves than FedWire. Perhaps somewhat surprisingly, due to the alternative means of settlement within CHIPS, payments actually tend to settle more quickly on this system than through Fedwire. According to McAndrews and Vartin, “[O]n average CHIPS settles payments hours earlier than Fedwire, and CHIPS is more than four times more economical with liquidity than is Fedwire”.<sup>[39](#footnote39)</sup>

Both systems are important to payments in the US. In June of 2020, CHIPS processed an average of 447,650 payments per day with a mean daily payment value of $1.62T.<sup>[40](#footnote40)</sup> Fedwire, in turn, processed 708,778 transactions per day on average in June 2020. The total daily payment value was somewhat over $3T.<sup>[41](#footnote41)</sup>

CHIPS did not always work in the way that has been described. In 1970, CHIPS became an electronic system. Until 2000, payments sent through CHIPS were netted throughout the day. Then, at the end of the day, those participants with a net debit position would have had to pay into a master account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York through Fedwire. Those with a net credit position would, then, receive payouts from this account through Fedwire. Only once a payout from the CHIPS master account had occurred which pertained to a payment would it have been considered settled. 

This type of system with netting and delayed settlement is known as a **deferred net settlement system**. These systems are the norm for retail payments. But they are also common for high-value payment systems that are not real-time gross settlement systems. Within the World Bank’s Global Payment Systems Survey (GPSS) from 2021, most of the economies that had non-RTGS high-value payment systems reported to be a deferred net settlement system (63 percent).<sup>[42](#footnote42)</sup>

CHIPS has a long history before 1970, one that is actually much longer than for Fedwire. It was originally founded in New York City in 1853 as the **New York Clearing House Association** (**NYCH**) by 52 private banks (all but a few of the smallest New York banks). There were various factors that influenced the formation of CHIPS within the environment we described in *Section 4*. A main driver was the complexity and inefficiency of the correspondent banking system, particularly for settling checks. Maintaining liquidity with many other banks would have also been challenging. Unsurprisingly, similar associations were created shortly after the founding of NYCH in other major American cities such as Boston (1856), Chicago (1863), and San Francisco (1876).<sup>[43](#footnote43)</sup>

While these associations were originally intended to improve efficiency in payments, their responsibilities quickly started evolving. By the early 20th century, they were operating as proto central banks. Contrary to how it is often presented, the Federal Reserve was, thus, really an evolution from arrangements that originated in the private sector.<sup>[44](#footnote44)</sup>

Many of the 19th century clearing houses have ceased to exist or taken on a different function. But CHIPS has withstood the test of time and still operates as a key payment system within the United States today.  


## 8. ACH

We can divide payment systems into two types: high-value payment systems and **retail payment systems**. We have already discussed the main high-value payment systems in the United States. We will now discuss the main retail payment system: the **Automated Clearing House Network** (**ACH Network**). 

The ACH network is a deferred net settlement system. It supports both ACH credit transactions—initiated by the payer’s bank—and ACH debit transactions—initiated by the payee’s bank. Any payments settle within a few hours to two days. The direct participants in the ACH Network are financial institutions such as commercial banks and payment processors (e.g., Adyen, Stripe).

ACH payments are much less costly than Fedwire or CHIPS payments and what most consumers, businesses, and organizations utilize for their standard interbank transfers. As one would expect, the number of transactions processed by the ACH Network is much greater than in Fedwire and CHIPS. In 2020, the ACH network saw over 25 Billion transactions. By comparison, Fedwire only saw slightly over 180 million transactions in that year. That said, the transaction value processed within Fedwire is much higher. The value of the transactions processed by the ACH network in 2020 amounted to slightly more than $60T in 2020, while the Fedwire system processed more than $840T in the same year.<sup>[45](#footnote45)</sup>

The ACH Network is governed by **Nacha** (formerly known as the **National Automated Clearing House Association** or **NACHA**). Created in 1974, Nacha is a nonprofit corporation registered in Delaware. This means that it does not have shares or pay dividends to owners. Essentially, there are no owners. Instead, it is an organization that represents the interests of commercial banks and their customers, primarily through governance of the ACH Network. 

The **direct members** of NACHA receive various benefits, primarily with regards to governance. They help shape the rules, nominate and elect the board of directors, and so on. Larger commercial banks and other types of depository institutions are typically direct members. Among the direct members are also ten regional **payment associations**. Their members include banks and other types of depository institutions, corporations, e-commerce companies, and so on. Any members of these payment associations that are not allowed to be direct members of Nacha or choose not to participate directly can also influence governance indirectly through these regional associations. 

Nacha only governs the ACH Network and is not responsible for its operation. Key to its operation are two automated clearing houses: **FedACH** and the **Electronic Payments Network** (**EPN**). An **automated clearing house** is a clearing house that performs netting operations electronically and for a retail payment system. FedACH is owned and operated by the Federal Reserve, while the EPN is owned and operated by the Clearing House Payments Company (which also owns CHIPS). 

Participants in the ACH Network can choose to work with either or both operators for their transactions. The financial institutions in the ACH Network will collect transactions from consumers, businesses, and organizations into batches. These batches are periodically sent to one of the operators. For any payments which FedACH receives where both the **originating depository financial institution** and the **receiving depository financial institution**, as the two participants to a ACH payment are formally called, are the same, it will just multilaterally net all these all these transactions into a number of simplified transactions for settlement. 

Settlement cannot, however, be directly arranged just on the basis of the obligations that arise from the multilateral netting process. That is because settlement requires transfers between master accounts at the Federal Reserve. And many financial institutions participating in the ACH Network will not have a master account. 

The solution here is bilateral correspondent banking arrangements. In this way, some financial institutions with master accounts can service financial institutions without one. Essentially, a select group of financial institutions settles all the obligations arising from the multilateral netting process at FedACH. But anyone that serves as a settling bank keeps tabs on what part of their net burden at the master account level is due to their own activities and which part of their net burden belongs to one of their correspondent banks. The banks involved in these correspondent arrangements will have to resolve their mutual obligations after settlement at the Federal Reserve.

Payments that are processed solely by the EPN essentially work the same, but for one key difference. The EPN only offers a multilateral netting service, it cannot settle transactions. That can only happen at the Federal Reserve. So, for settlement, the EPN sends net obligations to what is known as the **National Settlement Service**. 

This is a settlement service offered by the Federal Reserve specifically for private clearing houses like the EPN. Any files with information about multilateral net positions will include information about a settling correspondent financial institution for those financial institutions without a master account. The financial institutions in these bilateral arrangements will have to resolve their obligations after settlement between master accounts at the Federal Reserve. 

Note that Fedwire and the National Settlement Service are really two distinct services. The former is really a distinct individual clearing and settlement system, while the latter is a service utilized as part of a clearing and settlement system. 

In a last type of scenario, the originating depository financial institution and the receiving depository financial institutions might utilize different automated clearing houses. In that case, the multilateral netting process has to proceed through communication between the two clearing houses.  

Most economies have one main ACH system like in the United States. According to the World Bank’s Global Payment Systems Survey (GPSS) from 2021, this was the case for 84% of the economies surveyed.<sup>[46](#footnote46)</sup> In most cases ACHs are at a country-level, such as the **Bankers’ Automated Clearing Services** (**BACS**) in the United Kingdom. One notable exception is **STEP2**, a pan-European ACH system that is owned and operated by the Euro Banking Association. By contrast to real-time gross settlement systems, ACH systems are often owned and operated by the private sector.<sup>[47](#footnote47)</sup>
