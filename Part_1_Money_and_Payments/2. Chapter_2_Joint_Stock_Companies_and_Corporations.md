# Chapter 2: Joint stock companies and modern corporations

The modern history of money, payments, and banking is closely
intertwined with the evolution of the joint stock company into the
modern corporation. To deepen our understanding, we examine this
historical development from its institutional emergence in sixteenth-
and seventeenth-century England and the Dutch Republic through to its
maturation by the early twentieth century. We briefly reflect on joint
stock companies and corporations in the United States.

The historical literature contains varied opinions on the essential
nature of the joint stock company, its historical origins, and the
development of various features such as limited liability. Our purpose
is not to conduct an extensive enquiry into all these details and
controversies here, but merely to offer a broad outline of the main
concepts and developments.

We start by briefly introducing some financial jargon in *Section 1*.
Our discussion is organized around three main financial concepts: equity
capital, securities, and derivatives. In *Section 2*, we describe the
nature of the joint stock company and its appearance in England and the
Dutch Republic in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. We draw a
distinction between incorporated joint stock companies and
unincorporated joint stock companies.

Modern legal regimes for corporations developed out of the evolution of
the practical-contractual practices and laws surrounding joint stock
companies. At least one key aspect to modern legal regimes for
corporations is that incorporation with (full) limited liability has
become generally available through a registration process. In *Section
3*, we show how that evolution occurred within England, and briefly also
reflect on it for the United States.

Another key aspect to modern corporations is that, for public companies
at least, their shares as well as other securities can be traded on
public exchanges. In *Section 4*, we trace how exchange trading
developed in the Dutch Republic during the seventeenth century, and from
there spread out to England and elsewhere. As the development of
exchange trading is particularly illuminating at placing the Bitcoin
ecosystem into a proper perspective later, we will delve into some
detail.

## 1. Equity Capital, Securities, and Derivatives

We begin this chapter by briefly introducing some financial jargon. We
structure the discussion around three main concepts: equity capital,
securities, and derivatives. They are treated in order.

At the core of describing the performance of modern commercial ventures
are so-called **financial statements**. These are standardized financial
overviews that give government agencies, investors, and others certain
insights into the performance of a company. While legal reporting
requirements and customs vary across jurisdictions, companies in most of
the world—from proprietorships to publicly traded corporations—are
typically required to produce, at a minimum, periodic balance sheets and
income statements. These are the core financial statements in modern
accounting.

The **income statement** indicates the net income. This is the
difference between all the income generated and the costs incurred over
a certain accounting period, typically a quarter or a year. The net
income is also called net earnings, net profit, or bottom line (as it's
typically the last line on an income statement). It is sometimes also
just simply called profit, a term that usually denotes net profit and
not what is known as gross profit.[^1]

The income statement often only gives an indirect impression of the
company's money flows in the form of credit transfers and cash payments.
First, the costs in a certain period are not necessarily equivalent to
all expenses, as many expenses are treated as investments. Those
investment expenses are associated with costs over multiple quarters and
years through depreciation and amortization. Expenses incurred in a
particular period are also not necessarily paid in the period of their
occurrence. Second, income generated is not necessarily equivalent to
money received. Invoices generated through sales, for example, may not
have been paid yet. A different financial statement, namely the **cash
flow statement**, is used especially by larger companies to capture the
money flows over a quarter or year.

The **balance sheet** is the statement of all the assets of a company at
a particular point in time, typically at the end of a quarter or year,
with an indication of how those assets were acquired, either through
debt or capital investments. Balance sheets are governed by what is
known as the **accounting equation**, which states the following:[^2]

Assets = Liabilities + Equity Capital

Essentially, the accounting equation means that all company assets were
acquired either by "borrowing" in the loose sense (e.g., formal loans,
bank overdrafts, unpaid invoices, unpaid taxes) or by capital
investments. The latter is broadly determined by the investment of
external funds from the owners into the business as well as any profits
that have not been distributed via dividends. The term **equity
capital** denotes the sum of these capital investments.

Next, we turn to the concept of securities. We might broadly define a
**financial asset** as any type of asset that obtains its value from a
monetary contractual agreement. Financial assets can be distinguished
from **non-financial assets** such as land, buildings, equipment,
goodwill, patents, and trademarks. A **security** commonly refers to any
tradeable financial asset which represents an ownership position or a
creditor relationship, or derivatives based on such financial assets.
These are typically distinguished as **equity securities**, **debt
securities**, **hybrid securities**, and **derivative securities**.

The equity security category includes common and preferred stock, but
can also include more exotic financial assets such as interests in real
estate investment trusts. The category of debt securities includes
financial assets such as government and corporate bonds, debentures,
commercial paper, promissory notes, and convertible notes. Shares in
companies or trusts that pool various securities can also be called
securities. In some cases one might classify them as debt or equity
securities. But often they will really be hybrid products that combine
both debt and equity securities.

For example, one of the most common investment funds in the United
States is the **mutual fund**. It is the primary type of open-end
fund,[^3] one of the three main types of investment funds identified by
the SEC (closed-end funds and unit investment trusts are the other two
types). Mutual funds have a long-term time horizon and tend to invest
mostly in public stocks and bonds, though they could also invest in
commodities, futures, options, and so on. That mutual funds are
open-ended means that shares in the fund are not fixed and can be
created and redeemed throughout the fund's life. Shares in mutual funds
are not bought and sold on an exchange, but instead can only be bought
and sold once per day. Depending on the underlying investments, mutual
fund shares can be classified as either equity, debt, or hybrid
securities.

Notably, mortgage- and other asset-backed securities as well as
collateralized debt obligations pool debts and debt securities, and are,
therefore, typically classified as debt securities.

Loans such as mortgages are not easily transferrable. The concept of
**securitization** refers to the process of turning the income from
loans into tradeable financial assets. So-called **mortgage-backed
securities** are shares in companies or interests in trusts that own a
pool of mortgages. The owners of these securities are entitled to the
mortgage payments and any proceeds from foreclosures. The initial sale
of these securities benefits the issuer of the security. That is
typically not the bank or financial institution from which the mortgage
originated, but usually another financial institution that purchased it.
The issuers of mortgage-backed securities in the United States are
typically investment banks or certain government-sponsored enterprises
as **Fannie Mae** (Federal National Mortgage Association), **Freddie
Mac** (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation), and **Ginnie Mae**
(Government National Mortgage Association).

Many securities are created by a similar principle as mortgage-backed
securities, but on the basis of car and student loans, equipment leases,
credit card receivables, and so on. The general term for all such
financial instruments is **asset-backed securities**. Securities that
are based on a variety of debt assets—such as various loans,
receivables, bonds, or even other asset-backed securities—are
standardly called **collateralized debt obligations**. As all these
products are based on underlying debts and debt securities, we can also
categorize them as debt securities.

Finally, we turn to the subject of derivatives. We call a financial
asset a **derivative** when its value *derives* from the exchange price
of an underlying financial asset or commodity, or from some other
financial or non-financial indexes or indicators. Common derivatives
including forwards, futures, and options.

A contract between two parties which obliges one party to purchase a
specific commodity or financial asset at a predetermined price from the
other party at some point in the future is either a **forward contract**
or a **futures contract**.[^4] The former in case the contract is
customizable and privately negotiated; the latter in case the contract
is standardized and tradeable on an organized exchange.

Futures and forward contracts are usually settled in cash, rather than
in the underlying commodity or financial asset. For instance, suppose
that party A agrees to buy from party B one barrel of oil for \$100 on
December 31, but the contract stipulates that it should be settled in
cash. Suppose that a barrel on December 31 is \$120. Rather than sell a
barrel of oil for \$100 to party A, party B just pays party A \$20. The
monetary benefit of the contract is essentially the same for party A, as
if she would have been given a barrel of oil for \$100 and sold it for
\$120 (at least if we assume good liquidity of the barrel of oil).

Futures are not exactly traded like stocks. In the example above, party
A creates an offer to buy a barrel of oil for \$100 on December 31,
which party B accepts. That matching process creates the futures
contract at that point. Suppose that party A at some later point thinks
the price of a barrel of oil is, contrary to her initial expectations,
likely to be lower than \$100 on December 31 and that party C thinks the
opposite. Suppose that party C offers to purchase party A's position for
\$98 and the latter accepts. In that case, party A's position in the
contract is transferred to party C.

Futures and forwards create obligations for both parties. An **option**,
on the other hand, grants one party the right, but not the obligation,
to buy or sell some financial asset or commodity at a particular price
(called the **strike price**) within a certain period of time or on some
particular date. The other party has an obligation to follow the
decision of the option holder. They accept that obligation on the basis
of sum of money, or **premium**, which is paid by the buyer when the
contract is created. With futures, by contrast, no money exchanges hands
between the two parties. As with futures, both parties to an option can
sell their position to another party on an exchange.

According to our concepts of a security and a derivative, it follows
that some futures and options are indeed also securities. Given that
forwards are usually difficult, and sometimes impossible to transfer
depending on the terms, they are not really securities. In addition,
many options and futures are not based on the prices of debt or equity
securities. These include derivatives based on commodity prices, other
financial indicators (interest rates, sovereign debt default events),
and non-financial events and indicators like the weather, energy
production, and sports outcomes. In this light, it is fairly common for
practitioners to also speak of **derivative securities**, or those
derivatives based on debt and equity securities.

It should be noted that particularly the language on derivatives and
securities is not universal in the financial sector. Those terms are
used in a varied manner by practitioners and also vary across, and
sometimes within, legal regimes. Our characterization of a security
above, for example, is surely somewhat narrower than that maintained by
SEC.[^5]

## 2. The joint stock company

For much of human history, joint commercial ventures were typically
organized via some form of partnership. Many of these ventures centered
around long-distance trade, and partnerships often only lasted for the
duration of a single expedition. Such partnerships were commonly
organized around kinship, military, political, and religious
affiliations.

Ancient Rome developed a sophisticated legal system. It also covered
commercial partnerships which were organized in the legal form of the
*societas*. Contributions from partners came in the form of money,
skills and labor, or in-kind capital such as goods, rights, or claims.
Commercial partnerships typically only had a few members, and any
partner could generally dissolve the partnership at will, incurring
damages if stipulations on the duration had been included within the
partnership's contract. While Rome had well-developed laws on the
*societas*, it is important to realize that these laws codified
practices that often went back many centuries. The origins of the laws
and customs on partnerships really predate their recording.[^6]

Roman commercial law was very influential on the subsequent development
of European commercial law in the Middle Ages and the early modern
period. While the nomenclature varied across jurisdictions and time
periods, historians commonly use the Italian term *commenda* to refer to
the most common forms of partnerships in this era.[^7] These medieval
partnerships did not have really strong protections against liquidation
by any one of the partners or their creditors, and ownership shares were
illiquid.[^8]

The joint stock company, the pre-cursor to the modern corporation, makes
its first real appearance in England and the Dutch Republic within the
sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

We characterize the incorporated joint stock company in a manner that
largely follows the canonical discussion by William Scott in *The
Constitution and Finance of English, Scottish and Irish Joint stock
Companies to 1720*.[^9] Specifically, we can understand the
**incorporated joint stock company** as any *commercial association*
that has the following basic characteristics:[^10]

- Ownership over the association's equity capital (or the value of all
  assets minus liabilities) is distributed among owners through shares.
  Initial contributions to the equity capital are made via the issuance
  of new shares. Additions to the equity capital can come through the
  issuance of further shares, capital calls on existing shareholders, or
  through retained profits. Existing shares can be bought and sold, at
  least to some degree, in a secondary market. The equity capital is
  locked-in, meaning that there are substantial protections in place to
  prevent a shareholder or their creditors from liquidating the company
  and withdrawing their funds.

- The association has obtained the status of a "corporation" through a
  royal charter or a legislative act. We mean corporate status here in a
  very thin sense, namely that the association has a legally separate
  identity from its owners, employees, and other associates. It means
  that the association is marked by an independent existence that
  continues regardless of the participation of any particular owners,
  employees, and other associates.

- The association has formal rules and procedures for making commercial
  decisions and its internal governance. It also draws a basic
  distinction between the association's management and its owners. There
  are typically many owners and most of them are passive participants in
  the management of the company.

On the basis of this understanding, Scott contends that the earliest
incorporated joint stock companies were first seen in sixteenth-century
England. He suggests that,[^11] in the absence of better evidence, the
earliest such companies were the Africa and Muscovy Companies—both
formed in 1553 and chartered somewhat later—even though they did not
fully have all the features as mentioned above.[^12] It should be noted,
in particular, that, while these companies indeed show the union of the
corporate form with commercial capital, locked-in equity capital only
first seems to have been introduced as an attribute with the **Dutch
East India Company** or **VOC** (*Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie*) in
the early seventeenth century.[^13]

Many of the early incorporated joint stock companies in England revolved
around long-distance trade. A key company was the **East India Company**
or **EIC** (1600), which was legally entitled to conduct all English
trade with Asia. It eventually had thousands of shareholders. Following
the VOC, it organized locked-in equity capital in 1657.[^14] Other
notable early joint stock companies were those utilized to settle and
commercially develop the North American territories, including the
Virginia Company (1606), Plymouth Company (1606), Massachusetts Bay
Company (1628), and Hudson's Bay Company (1670). Other early joint stock
companies seem to have been involved primarily in mining, including the
Mines Royal (1568) and the Mineral and Battery Company (1568).[^15]

All the English incorporated joint stock companies mentioned so far were
incorporated through royal charters.[^16] In the previous chapter, we
stated that US banks are chartered by the authority of a state or the
federal government. This chartering occurs via a bureaucratic process
administered by a government agency. In the English context, it should
be noted that "chartering" typically refers only to the extension of a
**royal charter**, a formal legal document issued by a monarch which
instantiates a corporation and grants it certain rights and privileges.
All the English incorporated joint stock companies mentioned so far
received royal charters that were valid only for some period—typically
anywhere from a few years to several decades—though they could usually
be renewed.

Corporations of various kinds—of course in the thin sense of the
word—were common by the late sixteenth century, including "cities and
boroughs, guilds, universities and colleges, hospitals and other
charitables, bishops, deans and chapters, abbots and convents, and other
ecclesiastical bodies".[^17] Historically, there had been various paths
to incorporation other than the royal charter. Many associations, for
example, lacked formal approval of any sort and claimed corporate status
merely on the basis of custom or prescription.[^18] By the late
sixteenth century, however, the Crown had monopolized the authority over
incorporation. According to Harris, "an explicit, ex ante and direct
authorization by the King became the only mode of incorporation. This
authorization was normally given in the form of charter (or letters
patent), and, occasionally, by way of Act of Parliament bearing the
King's explicit consent, or a combination of an act and a charter".[^19]

As the general powers of the English Parliament increased relative to
those of the Crown—particularly after the **Glorious Revolution**
(1688) and the passing of the **English Bill of Rights** (1689)—it
also increasingly gained independent authority for incorporation by
private legislative acts. This practice, naturally, also became common
with regards to joint stock companies specifically. Notably, the **Bank
of England** (1694) was incorporated by a private act of Parliament, and
the initial issuance of shares saw 1,520 subscribers with stakes ranging
from £25 to £10,000.[^20] By the second half of the eighteenth century,
it seems that the incorporation of joint stock companies proceeded
predominantly via private legislative acts. That era was marked
particularly by the incorporation of canal companies—over 100 by
1800—and the vast majority indeed were incorporated by
Parliament.[^21]

Incorporation granted certain specific rights and privileges to joint
stock companies, such as the monopoly rights over the development of
trade between England and a certain geographic area. But the corporate
status of these companies also offered general legal privileges.
Notably, by the sixteenth century, the notion and custom of a legally
separate personhood for incorporated associations seems to have been
well-established.[^22] So incorporated joint stock companies had the
right to sue and be sued as an entity. It also meant that they had the
right to own assets which were seen as distinct from the personal assets
of their shareholders, a concept known as **asset partitioning**.

Scott contends that the joint stock company concept was probably not
imported from the Continent. Instead, it appears to have been a fusion
of two organizational traditions in England: that of the temporary,
capital intensive partnerships which marked medieval commerce and that
of the tradition of incorporation for institutions with a public or
semi-public function. The most direct pre-cursor to the incorporated
joint stock company was surely the **regulated company**, an
incorporated association which organized standards, rules, and
commercial opportunities for merchants, without pooling the capital of
their members. Some regulated companies eventually began pooling
capital, and so transformed into joint stock companies.

While the English may not have imported the concept of the incorporated
joint stock company, there were certainly commercial associations on the
continent at earlier times that had a similar nature. These include the
*maone* and *compera* capital associations that had already been used in
Italy far before the Muscovy and Africa Companies. Of the *compera* type
of capital association, the most prominent instance is that of Genoa's
famous House of St. George (*Casa di San Giorgio*) established in 1408,
about a 150 years before the rise of English joint stock companies.[^23]

By the second half of the twelfth century, the commune of Genoa had
begun a practice of issuing standardized public debt securities.[^24]
These securities were organized through managed funds called *campere*,
and the shares were called *luoghi* (or *loca* in Latin). The shares
paid out interest and repaid the principal on the basis of specific tax
payments. Each *campera* was typically named after a specific purpose
for the collected funds. Examples include the *Compere Capituli* (funds
for the Cathedral), the *Compera Venetorum* (a fund for the war against
Venice), and the *Compera Corsicae* (a fund for the war against Pisa for
Corsica). The shares of the *compere* were also traded in secondary
markets. They were quite possibly the first debt securities in
Europe,[^25] though, in contrast to modern practices, the loans involved
in these Geneose securities were often forced upon citizens.

Due to the high number of campere, the Genoese commune occassionally
made efforts to consolidate these debts. The most important effort in
this regard was precisely the establishment of the House of St. George
in the early fifteenth century. It was an office of the commune though
one with a corporate and highly autonomous status. Over a few decades,
it managed to consolidate most of the outstanding *campere* into a
single *campera*. Initially, San Giorgio's *campera* offered a fixed
interest rate of 7%, which was a few points lower than the average
interest rate over all the *campere* it had consolidated. Thereby, the
shift to centralized management reduced the debt burden for the commune.

Eventually, the shares obtained variable interest rates and were paid
out irregularly, so that they more began to resemble equity securities
with dividends. The shares in San Giorgio's *campera* were popular with
local and foreign investors and actively traded in secondary markets.
They also acquired other financial functions such as collateral for
loans. A secondary market where the rights to "interest" on shares at a
discount even arose, due to the uncertainty surrounding payouts. These
shares could not be reclaimed by San Giorgio—though they were
purchased on the secondary market—which supported their
popularity.[^26]

While the shares in the House of St. George clearly began their life as
debt securities, they seem to have morphed into equity securities in the
fifteenth century. Clearly, the institution, therefore, resembled the
incorporated joint stock companies that arose in England the Dutch
Republic in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Regardless the
exact details of the incarnation of the first joint stock companies,
however, it seems that "\[t\]he thrust of the general movement towards
commercial capital associations with corporate character and tradeable
shares appears in England following the Glorious Revolution of
1688".[^27]

Importantly, by the end of the seventeenth century, the English
landscape was not limited to incorporated joint stock companies.
Instead, in the second half of the seventeenth century, England also
increasingly saw the appearance of **unincorporated joint stock
companies**.[^28] According to Butler,[^29] their popularity grew
particularly due to the influence of the Dutch traders who migrated to
England after the Glorious Revolution (1688).

Obtaining royal charters or a private act from Parliament was subject to
extensive, costly procedures and political lobbying. So, many
entrepreneurs attempted to create companies that emulated the main
features of incorporated joint stock companies without formally
obtaining corporate status. At least in the eighteenth century, a key
feature of these arrangements was typically to entrust the company's
assets to the executive directors via a deed of settlement. Some
unincorporated joint stock companies also sought to obtain the right to
sue and be sued through private acts from Parliament in the early
nineteenth century.[^30]

The joint stock company of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was,
thus, not so much a specific type of legal entity, but rather an
economic arrangement. As suggested by Ireland,[^31]

> A joint stock company was an enterprise based on an accumulated
> capital fund; a firm involving many people, not usually closely
> related, whose membership took the highly impersonal form of a freely
> transferrable share and whose participation in management was minimal.
> The terms 'joint stock company' and 'company' conveyed nothing about a
> firm's legal status\.... Critically, the legal status of an enterprise
> made no difference to contemporary perceptions of its economic form: a
> joint stock company was a joint stock company whether it was
> incorporated or not.

From here on, we can follow that understanding of the **joint stock
company**, namely, as any commercial association that is characterized
by joint ownership over locked-in equity capital via transferable
shares; formal rules and procedures for making commercial decisions and
internal governance; and a basic distinction between management and
owners, there typically being many owners, most of whom do not
participate in management.

Unincorporated joint stock companies were statutorily banned in England
by the *Bubble Act 1719*.[^32] It went into effect in 1720 and
specifically mandated that joint stock companies could only be created
by royal charters or parliamentary acts. Various explanations for the
passing of the *Bubble Act 1719* have been put forward, but it seems
that that primary driver was probably the notorious **South Sea
Company** (1711), which attempted to shield itself from
competition.[^33] In fact, the act mostly pertains to the incorporation
of two marine insurance companies, and certain protections for the South
Sea Company, the East India Company, and the two new insurance
companies. Protection against bubbles formed only a small portion of the
law, and the name "Bubble Act" only became common in the nineteenth
century.

## 3. General incorporation with limited liability

Modern regimes for corporations are largely the product of the evolution
in the practical-contractual practices and laws surrounding joint stock
companies. In this respect, we should note at least three attributes of
modern corporate regimes in England and elsewhere in the modern world.

- Commercial corporations are now predominantly created and managed
  through state registration regimes. The creation of commercial
  companies through royal charter or legislative acts in England or
  elsewhere in the developed world is highly uncommon. This
  transformation of incorporation from a political to an administrative
  act has made the corporate status widely available.

- Limited liability in the modern sense—where owners' private assets
  are shielded from claims by creditors—is now a common feature of
  corporations and typically the default within corporate registration
  regimes.

- The shares of corporations can now be publicly traded on modern
  exchanges.

In England, the general incorporation by registration as well as the
general availability of limited liability was introduced into the legal
system during the nineteenth century.

The general registration regime was introduced by the *Joint Stock
Companies Act* *1844*.[^34] While widely applicable, it did exclude a
few types of industries such as banking, insurance, railways, and public
utilities.[^35] The act defined a joint stock company as any association
with more than twenty-five members, or as one with freely transferrable
shares. Any such company was subject to the exclusive right and
obligation to register and, in virtue of that registration, would obtain
corporate status. Special arrangements were made for existing joint
stock companies that met the requirements, whether established by
charter, act of parliament, letters patent (which had been available
since 1834),[^36] or some unincorporated legal approach.

The *Joint stock Companies Act 1844* established an expansive regulatory
regime, including many regulations applicable to corporations today,
such as the mandated provision of a prospectus to share with stock
subscribers, and the required keeping of books of account and balance
sheets by the directors. As summarized by Stern,[^37] the act replaced
"a political process of the granting of select rights and privileges
with a bureaucratic and administrative one". The act was immediately
followed by the *Joint Stock Companies Winding-Up Act 1844*,[^38] which
for the first time enabled creditors to dissolve insolvent joint stock
companies. Before that time, incorporated joint stock companies at
least, could only be dissolved by the Monarchy or Parliament. And
companies that were insolvent would often continue to operate.

As already mentioned, unincorporated joint stock companies were
officially banned in England by the *Bubble Act 1719*. In the same year,
there was a dramatic rise and crash in the prices of the shares,
futures, and options of the South Sea Company, an event known as the
**South Sea Bubble**. The act received royal assent and became law
shortly before the bursting of this financial bubble in 1720.

The *Bubble Act 1719* was marked, however, by legal ambiguity and weak
enforcement. The law only led to a single prosecution in the eighteenth
century.[^39] Supposedly unincorporated joint stock companies largely
continued to operate, even if enthusiasm in them had been dampened by
the bursting of the South Sea Bubble.[^40] The primary reason was, of
course, the costs, duration, and political lobbying involved in
obtaining corporate status through a parliamentary act, as well as the
limitations of partnerships for setting up businesses with large capital
requirements. The incessant popularity of unincorporated joint stock
companies, particularly after a boom of new stock offerings in
1824–5,[^41] seems to have been the predominant motivation for the
repeal of the *Bubble Act 1719* in 1825.[^42]

The repeal meant that unincorporated joint stock companies were now
regulated by the common law. Here they seem initially to have had an
unclear status. After several favorable rulings in the common law courts
towards unincorporated joint stock companies,[^43] the *Joint stock
Companies Act 1844* was passed. At this point, however, joint stock
companies that obtained corporate status through registration still had
unlimited liability. By contrast, joint stock companies that had been
incorporated by royal charter or private act of Parliament indeed
sometimes enjoyed (partial) limited liability. The Bank of England, for
example, was understood to have this attribute by 1825.[^44]

At what point the notion and manifestation of limited liability for all
shareholders—whether active in management or not—came into practice
and law is a contested issue. Gelderblom, De Jong, and Jonker contend
that limited liability for shareholders was already introduced to the
VOC in 1623, largely as a product of the financial constraints within
which the company operated.[^45] In similar vein, Hansmann, Kraakman,
and Squire, in turn, claim that "\[a\]n important implication of the
English and Dutch chartered joint stock companies is that commercial
firms had been established by the early seventeenth century with all the
elements of the modern business corporation: strong entity shielding,
limited liability, and tradable shares".[^46]

By contrast, Harris, quite convincingly, contends that a notion of
limited liability for shareholders only began to arise around 1800 and
manifested primarily via corporations with partially limited
liability.[^47] Before the nineteenth century, so Harris claims, the
whole idea of shielding owners from creditors was generally not yet
relevant. That is, "without debt finance, without a procedure for
dissolving insolvent corporations, without the legal ability to
determine whether shareholders would bear liability in insolvency, owner
shielding was a non-issue and limited liability in the modern sense
could not yet exist".[^48] He contends that only in the nineteenth
century did a notion of limited liability become an important motivation
for incorporation.[^49] U.S. and U.K. corporations could choose from a
continuum between unlimited liability and full limited liability, and
the vast majority chose some intermediate position. Only in the
twentieth century, did full limited liability in the modern sense become
the universal standard.

While the history of the introduction of the notion of limited liability
and its early manifestations is somewhat unsettled, the introduction of
general access to limited liability by statute in England clearly
transpired in the nineteenth century. As stated, the *Joint Stock
Companies Act 1844* only granted unlimited liability to companies that
met the registration requirements. But that changed with the *Limited
Liability Act* *1855*.[^50] It offered to all companies that met the
registration and other requirements—including the requirement to have
more than twenty-five owners or freely transferrable shares—various
possible regimes with regards to limited liability. The act was quickly
repealed, but re-enacted in the *Joint Stock Companies Act* *1856*.[^51]

The *Joint stock Companies Act* *1856* also simplified the process of
registration and extended that right to companies of seven owners or
more. It really set into motion the increasing availability of the
corporate legal form to associations that were not economically
organized as joint stock companies—with freely transferrable shares
and many owners—but rather as private companies. Small partnerships
and even proprietorships increasingly utilized the legal form of a
corporation with limited liability by skirting the requirement of seven
members.[^52] Towards the end of the nineteenth century, the notion of a
privately held company—characterized by few members, no public
solicitation for capital, and transfer restrictions on
shares—increasingly became recognized.[^53] By 1925, Ireland claims,
86,065 companies out of a total of 95,055 companies in the United
Kingdom were private.[^54]

For the banking sector in England, access to incorporation by
registration and limited liability followed a separate legal trajectory
though on a similar timeline.

In 1697, the Bank of England was given the exclusive monopoly right to
act as an incorporated bank.[^55] That, however, still granted a lot of
room for banks to operate as partnerships and unincorporated joint stock
banks. So, in 1709, all banks were prohibited from issuing bank notes in
England and Wales, if they had more than six partners.[^56] The purpose
of the **six-partner rule** was to stymie the competition to the Bank of
England from unincorporated competitor banks. The exclusivity to
incorporation and the six-partner rule granted the Bank of England a
virtual monopoly on the issuance of bank notes, the primary activity of
commercial banks at the time.

These privileges were granted to the Bank of England in exchange for
government access to credit from it.[^57] While that may sound repugnant
to more enlightened modern minds, such *quids pro quo* were hardly
uncommon in pre-modern society. Merchant bankers like the Medici, for
example, who dominated the European commercial and financial landscape
from the late middle ages to the early modern era, were the principal
lenders to sovereigns. As Kohn notes,

> The profitability of sovereign lending did not, however, derive solely
> or even principally from the economies of intermediation. The main
> reward, especially for large loans, generally came in the form of
> favors the government granted the merchant bank in return for its
> lending–commercial concessions, monopolies, and tax farms.[^58]

The restriction on incorporated banks and the six-partner rule were both
lifted in 1826 for banks operating outside of a 65 mile radius around
London through the *Country Bankers Act 1826*.[^59] This paved the way
for both incorporated and unincorporated joint stock banks, though
growth came exclusively from the latter. The number of joint stock
country banks was estimated at thirty-three by 1833.[^60] In 1833, the
Bank of England's charter was renewed with the *Bank of England Act
1833*.[^61] It also allowed joint stock banks within the London area, as
long as they did not issue notes. In fact, the act also made Bank of
England notes legal tender. The number of joint stock banks grew from 48
in 1834 to 111 in 1836, though most of that growth was outside of
London.[^62]

By 1844, English joint stock banks other than the Bank of England
remained beholden to joint and several liability. All but the Bank of
England were unincorporated.[^63] In that year, Parliament, then, made
banking extremely restrictive by requiring any bank with more than six
partners to incorporate via a private act of parliament or letters
patent with the *Joint Stock Banks Act 1844*.[^64] There were also
various other regulations specified for banks, including capital
requirements. Very few banks formed under this legislation. According to
Grossman,[^65] only seven new joint stock banks formed between 1844 and
1857, compared to well over one hundred in the previous thirteen year
period. Banks were finally granted access to a registration regime with
incorporation and limited liability by 1858.[^66]

Few banks seems to have taken advantage of the limited liability regime.
According to Acheson,[^67] setting aside the Bank of England, in 1869,
England and Wales had 73 unlimited liability joint stock banks, while
only 41 with (partial) limited liability. Apparently, there was a
conviction among bank shareholders and depositors that the attribute of
unlimited liability formed a constraint on excessive risk taking. It was
only after the spectacular collapse of the **City of Glasgow Bank** in
1878 and the collapse of several other banks in its wake, that those
sentiments abruptly changed. English Parliament also fostered conversion
to a limited liability regime through the *Companies Act 1879*.[^68] The
act allowed for a 'reserve liability', a sum of capital that could only
be called upon in a case of liquidation due to insolvency. By 1889, only
2 banks in England and Wales had unlimited liability. Over one hundred
enjoyed (partial) limited liability.[^69]

These developments towards making incorporation with limited liability
accessible through registration was largely also a nineteenth century
phenomenon in the rest of Europe as well as in the United States. Given
that incorporation was, and still is, largely the prerogative of the
state governments, the United States' progression towards general
incorporation and limited liability regimes was much more piecemeal than
in England.

The joint stock company was slow to develop in the United States, either
in incorporated or unincorporated form.[^70] While corporate charters
were granted to public and semi-public institutions such as churches,
colleges, and libraries, before the Revolution they were not granted to
commercial businesses. At least one reason was that chartering was
generally considered the prerogative of the Crown, and obtaining a
charter would have been difficult and costly. There are some examples of
unincorporated joint stock companies in Colonial America, but they
appear to be relatively few.

After its battle for independence, however, United States state
legislatures were, in contrast to the English Parliament, extremely
liberal with granting corporate charters. By 1800, over three hundred
charters had been granted.[^71] Many of these charters also pertained to
joint stock banks, so, in this regard, the United States banking system
developed earlier than in England, where prohibitions on joint stock
banking were only lifted by the *Country Bankers Act 1826*. While the
federal government generally did not grant incorporation through
legislation, two notable exceptions by 1800 are in the realm of banking.
The **Bank of North America** was chartered in 1781 at the start of the
Confederation period (1781–1789) by the Continental Congress (and later
also by the state of Pennsylvania). The **First Bank of the United
States** (1791–1811) was also chartered in 1791 by the US Congress.
Unincorporated banks also arose during this period, but such initiatives
increasingly became prohibited by legal statutes from the early
nineteenth century onwards.

While the American states were much more generous than the English
Parliament in granting incorporation, the practice led to some of the
same problems: slow and costly procedures, and serious problems with
corruption. In large part due to such concerns, the United States
gradually also moved to general incorporation with limited liability
throughout the nineteenth century.[^72] A New York law from 1811, which
allowed for the general incorporation of manufacturing companies, is
generally regarded as the first step in that transition in the United
States and globally. By 1860, most states had in place general
incorporation laws for manufacturing companies with (partial) limited
liability.[^73]

For banks, statutes for incorporation by registration first appeared in
the 1830s. By 1860, over half the states had passed some type of
registration law.[^74] Incorporation at the national level, then, became
available through the *National Banking Act of 1864*.[^75] The law
implemented what is known as a **double liability regime**, where
shareholders risk their initial investment and an additional amount up
to that initial investment in the case of bankruptcy.[^76] This type of
liability regime was the most common between 1863 and 1913, and all but
a handful of states implemented it at least some of the period. Some
states also had triple liability regimes and a few had unlimited
liability. By 1930, still only four states maintained full limited
liability regimes. The shift towards that regime mostly occurred in the
1930s in the wake of the Great Depression.[^77]

## 4. Public markets for stocks

The ubiquity of the modern corporation can make it easy to forget that
its forerunner, the joint stock company, had an arduous and protracted
struggle in becoming politically, legally, and socially accepted. In the
words of Hunt,

> In fact, the history of the business corporation or joint stock
> company in England during the one hundred and fifty years following
> the \[*Bubble Act 1719*\] is the story of an economic necessity
> forcing its way slowly and painfully to legal recognition in the face
> of strong commercial prejudice in favor of "individual" enterprise and
> in spite of determined attempts of both the legislature and the courts
> to deny it. Several eruptions of company promotion and speculation in
> defiance of the law, and the opening-up of new industries with which
> it was impossible for the individual capitalist to cope, were forces
> which operated finally to break down opposition and to cause
> Parliament, retracing its steps, gradually to erect the legal
> framework of the new form of business organization.[^78]

While the complaints about joint stock companies were diverse, the trade
in their stocks and derivatives that developed over time certainly had a
substantial role to play in the negative sentiments towards them.

Following Poitras,[^79] we can understand exchange trading as the
organized, regulated, and centralized sale and purchase of fairly
standardized, transferrable financial contracts with a clearance and
settlement system. Clearance and settlement systems are discussed more
elaborately in *Chapter 3* within the context of payments. For now, we
can understand it as a system that ensures purchases and sales of
financial contracts are jointly processed to simplify the number of
payments and deliveries.

A key aspect to exchange trading is that the financial contracts are not
just agreements between two parties that occur in the regular course of
business. As stated by Poitras,

> In particular, speculative exchange trading of a transferable security
> contract needs to be distinguished from a situation where a buyer and
> seller meet at, say, the Royal Exchange and agree to a forward sale of
> goods with a contract then drawn up by a 'scrivner'. In this
> situation, it is the goods that are being traded, not the forward
> contract. Speculative exchange trading requires transferability and an
> exchange-clearing mechanism to settle positions. In turn,
> transferability requires standardized contract terms and relatively
> homogeneous deliverable commodity.[^80]

The practice of exchange trading developed slowly over time. But the
fully developed form of exchange trading was probably first seen in
Antwerp during the sixteenth century.

In 1531, Antwerp erected the first permanent structure dedicated to
housing the purchase and sale of commodities and goods as well as the
organization of accompanying financial contracts. Originally, European
trade was marked by merchants and capital that concentrated periodically
at trade fairs, famously those at Champagne in the twelfth and
thirteenth centuries. As trade grew in the late Middle Ages, capital,
ordinary merchants, and merchant bankers also increasingly concentrated
in certain areas of commercial cities. The **Antwerp Exchange**,
however, was the first permanent structure dedicated to housing
merchants, financiers, brokers, and others involved in the trade of
goods and financing.

In the second half of the sixteenth century, the Antwerp Exchange saw
vibrant exchange trading develop in two key financial instruments:
promissory notes and futures contracts. Early in the seventeenth
century, it also emerged for, notably, bills of exchange.

A **promissory** note is a contract that contains a promise by a debtor
to pay or otherwise settle a sum of money by a certain date or upon
demand, with or without interest. In the second half of the sixteenth
century, exchange trading arose specifically for **bearer** promissory
notes, meaning their rights accrued to whomever had physical possession
of them. Exchange trading here specifically means that they were bought
and sold before their due date at a **discount**, that is, for less than
their face value plus, if applicable, interest. Such exchanges helped
merchants manage risks and liquidity. In the early seventeenth century,
exchange trading also developed for **assignable** promissory
notes—those which can be transferred to others via signatures—and
**bills of exchange**. The latter are essentially payment orders which
enjoyed use in a variety of contexts in the Late Middle Ages and early
modern period.[^81]

Out of forward contracts, early futures contracts and a market for them
also developed at the Antwerp Exchange—next to the market in bearer
promissory notes—already during the second half of the sixteenth
century.[^82] It should be noted that futures trading developed in
Antwerp more than a century before futures were traded on the Dojima
Exchange in Osaka, a venue commonly cited as the first modern futures
exchange.[^83]

Exchange trading practices spread out from Antwerp to other cities in
Europe. Most notably, we see important developments in exchange trading
in the Dutch Republic during the early seventeenth century, particularly
in Amsterdam. The latter really became the most important commercial and
financial center in northern Europe after the fall of Antwerp to
Habsburg Spanish forces in 1585.

The main joint stock company in the Dutch Republic was the already
mentioned VOC established in 1602. Its main purpose was to develop trade
between the Dutch Republic and the Eastern hemisphere. The VOC also had
a less prominent counterpart for the Western hemisphere known as the
**Dutch West India Company** or the **WIC** (*West-Indische Compagnie*)
established in 1621. Both companies were chartered by the States
General, the general assembly of the seven united provinces and the
highest state authority.

By the time of the incorporation of the VOC, trading in the shares of
joint stock companies had not really occurred yet in a developed market.
From the moment of its inception, however, the trading of VOC shares was
fairly well-organized, first in the open air, and from 1613 onwards in a
dedicated building in the center of the city, a stone's throw from the
VOC's headquarters. The building was known as the **Beurs van Hendrick
de Keyser**, after its main architect, and had been set up in 1611 for
similar purposes as the Antwerp Exchange almost 100 years earlier. The
**Amsterdam Stock Exchange**—now **Euronext Amsterdam**—traces its
history to the stock trading organized by the VOC in 1602, and is
commonly considered the world's oldest stock exchange.

By the middle of the seventeenth century, futures and option contracts
based on the shares of the VOC, and to a lesser extent for the WIC, were
also traded in Amsterdam. This is seemingly the first (recorded)
historical instance of the trading of derivatives that are based purely
on financial assets.[^84] As the seventeenth century progressed, trading
in government debt and, towards the end of the century, in the shares of
English joint stock companies, also became important.[^85] Amsterdam was
arguably the first true international financial center and remained in
that position until it was surpassed by London, certainly by the early
nineteenth century.[^86]

In London, a developed market for trading in the shares of joint stock
companies only arose at the very end of the seventeenth century. At that
time, there was an explosion of new share and debt issues from joint
stock companies, including from the Bank of England.[^87] While there
were an estimated 15 joint stock companies in the year of the Glorious
Revolution, there were an estimated 140 by 1695. Before 1696, exchange
trading had taken place at the **Royal Exchange**—London's counterpart
to the Antwerp Exchange and the exchange in Amsterdam, established in
1571—and **Exchange Alley** (now known as **Change Alley**), primarily
in its various coffee houses, including **Jonathan's Coffee House** and
**Garraway's Coffee House**. The stock traders left the Royal Exchange
in that year under somewhat mysterious circumstances, and further
conducted their business in Exchange Alley until 1773.

Throughout the eighteenth and the early nineteenth century, exchange
trading in England was dominated by the securities of a few companies:
the Bank of England, the East India Company, the South Sea Company, and
the two insurance companies created by the *Bubble Act 1719*. It was
only starting with the substantial issues of railway shares in the 1830s
that exchange trading moved beyond a few companies.[^88] By the early
nineteenth century, London had become the undisputed global financial
center, a position it held until after World War II when surpassed by
New York.[^89]

While securities trading seems to have occurred on a low scale in the
United States during much of the eighteenth century, the sharp rise in
securities trading came after the Revolution and the subsequent growth
in joint stock companies. In 1790, the origins of the Philadelphia Stock
Exchange were formed. The center of securities trading was the **City
Tavern**, later renamed the **Merchants Coffee House**. Trading shifted
to a dedicated building, the **Merchants' Exchange Building**, in 1834.
In 1792, a group of traders in New York City signed the famous
**Buttonwood Agreement**, which laid the foundation for the New York
Stock Exchange. Trading in New York centered around the famous **Tontine
Coffee House** that opened in 1793, but from 1817 onwards operated in
dedicated buildings. The Boston Stock Exchange was formed in 1834.[^90]

We should not underestimate the sophistication and capabilities of early
investors in the shares of joint stock companies and related securities.
Nevertheless, from its inception, trading in joint stock company
securities was susceptible to naïve and malevolent schemes, dubious
practices, rumors and innuendo, disinformation campaigns, and irrational
exuberance. The exchange business was not always looked up kindly due to
some of these excesses. Illustratively, a **stock jobber** was the
pejorative term used loosely in England for anyone engaging in trading
activities seen to be excessively speculative or manipulative, often
being reserved, it seems, for people trading on their own account.[^91]

A **bubble** is the common term for a market phenomenon where the price
of a stock or other asset rises far beyond what the asset's fundamental
value warrants, typically due to irrational exuberance, herd mentality,
and (often) manipulation. While the Dutch Republic, particularly
Amsterdam, was at the forefront of stock and derivatives trading, in the
history of bubbles, it is better known for the spectacular **Tulip
Bubble** of the 1630s. With regards to securities trading, the most
well-known and spectacular early bubbles involved the already mentioned
English South Sea Company (1711) and the French **Mississippi Company**
(1717). Both bubbles transpired in 1720, the Mississippi Bubble peaking
slightly before the South Sea Bubble.

It is the latter that actually gave rise to the modern understanding of
the term "bubble". In early eighteenth century England, the term
"bubble" actually had a broader meaning as "an insubstantial, delusive,
or fraudulent project or enterprise, especially of a commercial or
financial nature".[^92] After the South Sea Bubble deflated in 1720, the
term "bubble" increasingly became used in its more narrow modern sense
both in England and elsewhere. Shortly after the South Sea bubble, for
example, the Dutch play writer Gijsbert Tijssens wrote a comical play
entitled *De Windhandel, of Bubbles Compagnien*. The Dutch term
*windhandel* (literally "wind trade") still refers to purely speculative
trading, typically at very inflated prices (particularly in the context
of futures, options, and other derivatives). So Tijssen's play describes
the *windhandel*, or how bubbles in financial assets for companies
arise.

The South Sea Company was created in 1711 with monopoly rights to trade
with South America and its surrounding waters (which had the name "South
Seas" at the time). In 1713, it also acquired certain rights with
regards to the slave trade from Spain. The main trigger for the bubble
was a scheme between the company and the government—the relevant
legislation passed on April 7, 1720—to swap the majority of the public
debt, which had been increasing rapidly primarily due to wars, for
equity in the company.[^93]

Specifically, the scheme was to convert £31 million pounds of public
debt into company stock, offer a lower rate of interest for the assumed
debt to the government, and to pay the government a one-time fee of £7
million pounds. The government would benefit from a lower interest rate
and lower administration costs. And the company would stand to receive
debt payments, which would allow it with support of the Sword Blade
Bank, to issue loans. The Bank of England had similarly executed such a
scheme, with success, in 1697. It was during the execution of the scheme
by the South Sea Company, however, that a bubble arose and burst.

At the beginning of the year, South Sea stock traded at slightly over
100 pounds. It traded at roughly the same price at the end of the year.
But in the middle of the year, it had managed to climb to over 1,000
pounds. At least a hundred imitators tried to coast along in the boom,
including a joint stock company "for carrying on an undertaking of great
advantage, but nobody to know what it is".[^94]

The episode, at its core, would have probably been remarkably familiar
to any long-term Bitcoin observer that has been in the unenviable
position to witness all the fraudulent schemes, hare-brained ideas,
unscrupulous characters, and unfortunate victims that have tainted the
sector for years. As noted by Galbraith,[^95]

> All the predictable features of the financial aberration were here on
> view. There was large leverage turning on the small interest payments
> by the Treasury on the public debt taken over. Individuals were
> dangerously captured by belief in their own financial acumen and
> intelligence and conveyed this error to others. There was an
> investment opportunity rich in imagined prospects but negligible in
> any calm view of the reality. Something seemingly exciting and
> innovative captured the public imagination, in this case the joint
> stock company\.... And as the operative force, dutifully neglected,
> there was the mass escape from sanity by people in pursuit of profit.

The standard view among economic historians follows the conclusions of a
Parliamentary enquiry that "Company officials bought, or already held,
large quantities of South Sea stock, worked behind the scenes to push
the market price higher, and then sold out a large proportion of their
holdings at the top of the market."[^96] Various strategies have been
proffered as supporting the artificial price inflation. According to
Kleer,[^97] however, the standard interpretation is probably wrong. The
existence of some of the strategies mentioned in the literature enjoys
little support from the available evidence. Other strategies—including
lending company money to investors for stock purchases as well as the
company purchases of stock and stock options at inflated prices—are
better explained by the need to keep the price of company stock high
during the execution of the scheme.

Specifically, to manage the scheme, the Company set out to offer various
tranches of stock issuance in 1720. In some of these issuances, stock
would be swapped for government debt. In other issuances, subscribers
could purchase their shares with money on the basis of a down payment.
The latter issuances were intended to cover the £7 million pound
payment. As the debt was swapped in various tranches, the company would
have been motivated to prevent price declines during the period of the
conversions, otherwise debt holders might not have converted in later
rounds. As money subscribers could make a purchase with only a down
payment, the company would also have needed to keep the price high to
ensure they could raise the 7 million pound payment.

The Mississippi Bubble displays many similarities to the South Sea
Bubble. The French Mississippi Company had been granted trading rights
to French Louisiana in 1717. The stocks and other securities of the
French Mississippi Company were traded initially on Rue Quincampoix, but
later also in other locations in Paris. Even more so than the South Sea
Company, the Mississippi Bubble seems to have been the result of an
ill-conceived scheme of financial engineering, one, like the South Sea
bubble, involving the exchange of equity for public debt, as well as
rampant paper money printing. At the heart of the scheme was a colorful
Scotsman known as **John Law** (1671–1729).[^98]

The schemes, rumors, irrational exuberance, and tainted practices that
surrounded the trading of joint stock companies had long-lasting
deleterious consequences on the general impression of them. More than
two hundred years after their first appearance, the father of *laissez
faire* economics, Adam Smith, believed that the main reason any joint
stock company ever had success was because of their monopoly rights, and
suggests that otherwise the joint stock company is fundamentally flawed.

> The trade of a joint stock company is always managed by a court of
> directors. This court, indeed, is frequently subject, in many
> respects, to the controul of a general court of proprietors. But the
> greater part of those proprietors seldom pretend to understand any
> thing of the business of the company; and when the spirit of faction
> happens not to prevail among them, give themselves no trouble about
> it, but receive contentedly such half yearly or yearly dividend, as
> the directors think proper to make them. This total exemption from
> trouble and from risk, beyond a limited sum, encourages many people to
> become adventurers in joint stock companies, who would, upon no
> account, hazard their fortunes in any private copartnery. Such
> companies, therefore, commonly draw to themselves much greater stocks
> than any private copartnery can boast of\.... The directors of such
> companies, however, being the managers rather of other people's money
> than of their own, it cannot well be expected, that they should watch
> over it with the same anxious vigilance with which the partners in a
> private copartnery frequently watch over their own. Like stewards of a
> rich man, they are apt to consider attention to small matters as not
> for their master's honour, and very easily give themselves a
> dispensation from having it. Negligence and profusion, therefore, must
> always prevail, more or less, in the management of the affairs of such
> a company.[^99]
