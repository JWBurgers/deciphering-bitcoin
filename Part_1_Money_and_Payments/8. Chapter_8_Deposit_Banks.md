# Chapter 8: Deposit Banks

While we refer to the merchant bankers as "bankers", they are perhaps
more accurately described as financiers. Their main business was not
taking deposits from consumers and businesses and facilitating payments
with credit money.[^1] While they indeed commonly accepted time
deposits, those could not be withdrawn quickly and did not enable credit
payments. Instead, those real deposit banking activities were generally
provided by smaller, local companies in the late Middle Ages and early
modern period.[^2] Unlike the merchant bankers, these local companies
typically specialized in finance. That distinction with regards to
specialization interestingly also seems to have applied to the elite
financiers and deposit bankers in Ancient Rome.[^3]

Of course, this is not to say that merchant bankers did not provide
deposit banking services at all. Many of them would also run such
operations locally. Both the Medicis and the Datini, for instance,
maintained local deposit banks in Florence.[^4] Or, for another example,
it is clear that the Italian merchant bankers offered deposit services
to the upper echelons of English society.[^5] The point is merely that
accepting deposits and facilitating credit payments was generally *not*
a core business activity of the merchant banking houses. Tellingly, many
medieval towns and cities judicially distinguished between the core
activities of the merchant banking houses and the local deposit banks.
While the local deposit banks were often strongly regulated, the
international merchant bankers seem to have operated in a less stringent
environment.

In this Chapter, we begin by layout out the story of the Goldsmith
bankers.

## 1. The Goldsmith Bankers

How is it that banks can affect the money supply? While we turn
elaborately to that question elsewhere, we start by developing our
understanding through the history of the goldsmith bankers.

Earlier we defined deposit banks as institutions that accept deposits
from the public into accounts; facilitate payments with those funds,
including purely on the basis of credit money transfers; and usually
make loans to businesses and consumers. Deposit banking, understood in
this way, certainly existed in England before the 17^th^ century, even
if in a somewhat rudimentary form.

The English historian Edmund Fryde shows that a key advisor to King
Edward II (1284 – 1327), Hugh Despenser the Younger, maintained
substantial deposits in the early 14^th^ century with the Bardi and the
Peruzzi, two Florentine merchant banking houses that were operating in
England at the time. Despenser collected debts and made payments through
his accounts with them. The Bardi and Peruzzi also made loans with his
deposits on Despenser's behalf. Sometimes he would give specific
instructions, but at other times he would grant them substantial leeway
with the details. Probably most of Despenser's funds were available on
demand and it appears that he generally did not receive interest on
them.[^6]

We also have other evidence which clearly suggests that Italian merchant
bankers, already in the late Middle Ages, occasionally provided deposit
banking services in England.[^7] Deposit banking, thus, certainly
existed within England after the spread of Italian merchants throughout
northwestern Europe in the 13^th^ century. The Jewish bankers that lived
in England after the invasion of William the Conqueror and the Normans
probably also engaged in deposit banking to some degree.[^8] The Knights
Templar also practiced deposit banking by the thirteenth century,
including in England.[^9]

But while deposit banking certainly existed in England before the 17^th^
century, it appears to have been a relatively scarce practice.

The Jews that operated as financial intermediaries in England until
their expulsion (1290) were predominantly lenders. While we often talk
about the Jewish "bankers" of the Middle Ages and early modern period,
that is somewhat of a misnomer. Their activities were largely confined
to lending.[^10]

As Jews were generally exempted from **usury laws**­—laws that impose
restrictions on the charging of interest rates for loans[^11]—and
lending by Jews to non-Jews had solid biblical grounding,[^12] they
naturally turned to finance. Many Jewish lenders operated as local
pawnbrokers who, contrary to modern times, served all levels of society.
They were seen throughout much of Europe and were a prominent
intermediary for extending consumer credit.[^13] Some Jews had much more
substantial lending operations. The famous Aaron of Lincoln of 12^th^
century England, for example, had a network of operators throughout the
country; financed abbeys and monasteries; and was crucial to sovereign
liquidity. But he too was a financier.[^14]

In this vein, it is unsurprising that historical accounts of the Jewish
community in England until their expulsion by Edward III typically make
no mention of deposit banking practices, even though they emphasize the
crucial role of Jews as lenders.[^15]

As we have already mentioned, the Italian merchant bankers that settled
in England generally did not seem to offer deposit banking services.
This would be consistent with trends elsewhere in Europe, where deposit
banking and merchant banking were generally conducted as two fairly
distinct activities, typically subject to different legal
regulations.[^16] Naturally, Fryde concludes in his study on Despenser
the younger that in early 14^th^ century England deposit banking was
probably a fairly limited practice among the Italian merchant bankers,
certainly confined to the very upper echelons of society: "I have an
impression, though it can be no more, that in the middle and later years
of the reign of Edward II only a minority of the leading men of the
kingdom habitually kept deposits with Italian financiers."[^17]

The general impression that deposit banking did not gain in popularity
until the 17^th^ century in England is also echoed by other historians.
In his classic *The Early History of Banking in England*, Richards also
concludes as much. According to him, "There does not appear to be any
evidence to show that the goldsmith, the broker, or the merchant made a
practice of receiving deposits in Tudor and Jacobean times".[^18]

But by the end of the 17^th^ century, deposit banks had become fairly
prominent in London, typically in the form of small partnerships. Many
of these small deposit banks had been started by goldsmiths; hence, we
fittingly call these deposit banks from the period **goldsmith
banks**.[^19] While they certainly served nobles, they seemingly also
catered to the growing middle class in England, including merchants,
retailers, and manufacturers.[^20] On the basis of a study of the
account ledgers of Hoare's between 1695 and 1724, Temin and Voth
concludes as follows:[^21]

> The English financial system, as reflected in the loan books Richard
> Hoare and his descendants, was surprisingly open. There may have been
> few borrowers, and entire classes of citizens clearly had no access to
> credit. Yet the accidents of birth, of noble titles and royal
> connection, were small factors in lending decisions. In the ledgers
> and even on the list of top borrowers, the likes of Marcus Moses
> mingled freely with dukes and earls. In the few cases for which we
> know the uses of the loans—such as for Marcus Moses's diamond
> business or where Tuscan ham served as collateral—there also was no
> apparent reluctance to lend for commercial purposes.

Via what historical developments did the goldsmith banks become common
in London at the end of the 17^th^ century?

After the passing of the childless Elizabeth I in 1603, the Scottish
King James VI also ascended to the English throne where he was crowned
James I.[^22] It marked the transition from the Tudor to the Stuart
dynasty. While England, Ireland, and Scotland remained separate states,
James VI now ruled over all three states in what is known as the **union
of the crowns**. In 1625, James I passed and was succeeded by his son
Charles I.

Charles' reign saw the British Isles enter into a very turbulent and
violent period. There were various interrelated conflicts that
transpired across the three kingdoms. But at the center stage were the
**English Civil Wars**, which consisted of three main conflicts: the
First English Civil War (1642 – 1646), the Second English Civil War
(1648 – 1649), and the Third English Civil War (1649 – 1651). The main
parties were the royalists, known as the **cavaliers**, and the
Parliamentarians, known as the **roundheads**. The intense conflicts in
this period arose for various reasons including tensions over the
division of powers between the King and parliament, Charles's style of
rule, government finances, religious practices and institutions, and the
unpopular wars Charles had entered.

After the Second English Civil War, England was briefly governed as a
republic. It first came under the reigns of the **Rump Parliament**
(1649 – 1653), the Parliament that remained after it had been cleansed
of royal sympathizers in 1648. Given its unwillingness to hold new
elections, Oliver Cromwell—a main political and military figure of the
period—dismissed it in 1653. He, then, occupied the role of **Lord
Protector** from 1653 to 1658. After his death, the same title was
briefly held by his son Richard Cromwell until 1959 when he resigned. In
1660, the sentiments to the republican experiment had shifted, and both
the Parliament and the monarchy—now with Charles II as King, the son
of Charles I who had been executed in 1649—were restored with the
support of the army of George Monck.

The first notable emergence of the goldsmiths as deposit bankers is
standardly dated to be around the Cromwellian period. A famous pamphlet
about the supposed crimes of the goldsmith bankers that was published in
1676, "Mystery of the new fashioned goldsmiths or bankers", dates the
start of the rise of deposit banking to about thirty years before
publication.[^23] Richards also suggests that the goldsmiths had become
true deposit bankers during the Cromwellian period.[^24] Judges
similarly dates the beginning of the fully developed deposit banks to
the Cromwellian period.[^25] Supposedly the English merchant,
politician, and economist, Sir Dudley North (1641 – 1691) remarked in
1680 after many years abroad

> that the new practice of depositing cash with the goldsmiths and
> drawing checks on them and using their notes, had grown up in his
> absence. When one of his friends asked him where he kept his cash:
> 'Where should I keep it,' he asked, 'but in my own house?'[^26]

According to Richards, changes in the public perception on charging
interest—notably, a full ban on interest was dropped in favor of a
maximum rate of 10 percent in 1571[^27]—as well as the growth in trade
at the end of the 16^th^ century, promoted the emergence of native
financial middlemen. The most identifiable were the English merchants,
brokers, scriveners, and goldsmiths.[^28]

- While in the beginning of the sixteenth century, commercial credit and
  sovereign lending was still largely the prerogative of foreign
  merchants, including Italians, native English merchants had become key
  players in this regard at the end of the century.

- By brokers, Richards means both brokers for loans from merchant
  bankers as well as ordinary pawnbrokers. The latter specialized in
  consumer credit and were, for various reasons, commonly frowned upon
  in English society at the time. This is consistent with the negative
  view of pawnbrokers throughout the late Middle Ages and early modern
  period in Europe.[^29]

- By trade, scriveners read, wrote, and made copies of letters and
  official documents. As their work would have often involved financial
  contracts, they increasingly extended loans themselves.

- By trade, the business of the goldsmiths consisted of making jewelry,
  plate, and other valuables out of gold and silver. They would have
  also acted in some other capacities, such as gold and silver dealers,
  and safekeepers of valuables. In this period, we increasingly see
  goldsmiths also offering loans, though "\[t\]heir financial activities
  were overshadowed by those of the merchants, the brokers and the
  scriveners."[^30]

According to Richards, two main circumstances, then, influenced
particularly goldsmiths to turn to deposit banking. First, as Charles I
was unable to raise taxes via Parliament for the **Bishops' Wars**
between England and Scotland, he decided to forcefully borrow money from
the Mint at the Tower of London. This event was known as the **Great
Seizure**. Most of the gold and silver there seems to have belonged to
merchants, at least some of it probably to be minted and some just for
safekeeping.[^31] This event really damaged the trust of merchants in
storing their valuables with the mint and offered an opportunity to the
goldsmith bankers. Second, the period of the English Civil Wars was an
incredibly turbulent and violent one, and many seem to have turned to
the goldsmith bankers for this reason. The goldsmiths would typically
have had substantial security measures in place.

The goldsmith bankers are probably best known for their mainstream
introduction to English society of a system of fractional reserve
banking on the basis of goldsmith notes.

So-called **goldsmith notes** were commonly issued by the goldsmith
banks. Often they promised a payment on demand when presented to the
issuing bank. Many goldsmith notes also made such promises as long as a
certain amount of time had transpired. While early on many of the notes
paid interest, that practice seems to have largely been abandoned by the
end of the 17^th^ century.[^32] The promises of payment were generally
expressed in "pounds".

Essentially, goldsmith notes were just **promissory notes**, notes that
contain a promise by a debtor to pay a sum of money by a certain date or
upon demand, with or without interest. There is often the impression
that the goldsmith notes required payment in **specie**—gold and
silver coins issued by a government or some private institution. And
payment in native or foreign specie was probably common.

But it would have also been consistent with the usage of promissory
notes at the time for such payments to be made via different methods.
They may have been honored sometimes with **bullion**—gold and silver
in the shape of bars and ingots—or other valuables. In some cases, the
holders surely would have accepted credit money in an account or some
other credit instrument like a bill of exchange. By the middle of the
18^th^ century, payment via banknotes from the Bank of England, which
was founded in 1694, would have also become common.

In the second half of the 17^th^ century, these goldsmith notes had
started to circulate in London for making payments. That is, they were
functioning, at least to some extent, as "money". The goldsmith notes
were of two main types: those that could be transferred by endorsement
and delivery, and those that could be transferred merely by
delivery.[^33]

In the first type of note, the original recipient could, if desired,
transfer the ownership of the note to another person or entity by
specifying them as the recipient, making a signature, and handing it to
them. We refer to this process as **endorsement** and **delivery**.
These types of goldsmith notes could circulate by repeated endorsements.
The goldsmith note would from then on belong to that other person or
entity. These notes were usually of the form "To X or the order of".

While they did not contain the customary phrasing "To X or the order
of", the two earliest issued goldsmith notes on record of this type seem
to be from 1654.[^34] They were both handwritten and issued by Feild
Whorwood (in the 18^th^ century these notes increasingly became
printed). One made out to Sam Toffe and endorsed to Humphry Molson in
1655 reads as follows:[^35]

> Recd. 7th Dec. 1654 of Sam Toffe three score pounds wch I promise to
> repay upon demand witnesse my hand wth 2d. a day interest.
>
> Field Whorwood
>
> Endorsement: I assigne the Contents of this bill to Mr. Humphry Molson
> 7^th^ June 1655.
>
> Sam Toffe

The second type of goldsmith note was even easier to transfer. These
were of the form "To X or bearer", **bearer** here meaning whomever was
in physical possession of the note. The original recipient could, if
desired, transfer the ownership of the note to another person merely
through delivery. Notes could circulate merely by them just exchanging
hands among Londoners. An early example of such a handwritten note on
record is from 1684. It was issued Francis Child and Jno Rogers to Lord
North & Grey, and reads as follows:[^36]

November 28th 1684.

> I promise to pay unto the Rt Honble Ye Lord North & Grey or bearer
> ninety pounds at demand.

for Mr Francis Child & myself

Jno Rogers.

According to Davies,[^37] the earliest mention of such a note comes from
the diary of Samuel Pepys who was a naval administrator and politician
in the seventeenth century. His entry of February 29 in 1668 mentions
the transfer of a note from the goldsmith Colvill of the value of 600
pounds to his father.[^38]

In modern times, the goldsmith notes would have been subject to a
jurisdiction's laws on negotiable instruments. All jurisdictions will
have precise, technical definitions on legal instruments.[^39] But
loosely speaking, a **negotiable instrument** is a document which (1)
contains an unrestricted promise or order to pay a fixed sum of money,
potentially plus interest, by a certain date or upon demand, and (2)
which can be transferred to others so that they can become the rightful
holder of the instrument. Such a transfer can occur either through
delivery, or through endorsement and delivery. The body of laws on
negotiable instruments intends to make transfers easy and reliable. In
this way, markets are encouraged in which these instruments can be
bought and sold.

To illustrate why such a laws are necessary, consider the following
example. Suppose you borrow \$10,000 from your bank and sign a
promissory note to the "bank or bearer". The note specifies that you
will pay the original \$10,000 plus 5% interest in a year. The bank,
then, sells that note to a financial company. The bank, however, fails
to transfer to you the amount of the loan. After a year, the financial
company demands payment from you.

If regular contract law applied, you could resist the financial
company's demand to pay, as the amount of the loan was never
transferred. However, as long as the promissory note meets all the
conditions for being a negotiable instrument and was transferred
according to certain conditions—the financial company, for example,
could not have been aware that there were issues with the extension of
the loan at the time of purchase or that the bank was, in general, a bad
actor—then you cannot deny the claim of the financial company for
payment. You are required to pay and your only recourse is to sue the
bank in return.

As illustrated in this example, laws on negotiable instruments seek to
facilitate their sale on markets. The financial company is not required
perform extensive background checks on the circumstances of the origins
of the promissory note in order to have faith in payment. If it had
been, then the transfer would have been much more complicated.
Typically, the financial company would have purchased the note at a
discount—that is, for less than the dollar value of it, including the
face value plus any interest.

The emergence of goldsmith notes in the 17^th^ century reflected a
larger movement among merchants and bankers to make paper financial
instruments transferable. Notably, by the middle of the 17^th^ century
it had also become common to have bills of exchange made out to order or
to bearer. So-called **inland bills of exchange**—where the order to
pay was domestic—also became common in the second half of this
century, and were often made out to bearer or to order.[^40]
Importantly, however, these practices among merchants and bankers were
not occurring in the context of established laws on negotiable
instruments. In fact, the common law surrounding negotiable instruments,
primarily promissory notes and bills of exchange, was very much in
development during 17^th^ and 18^th^ century England.

Before the 17^th^ century, England had a variety of competing court
systems, including royal common law courts, the cannon law courts, the
royal maritime courts, and merchant courts, among others.[^41] Merchants
courts enforced what is known as "The Law Merchant" (or *lex
mercatoria*). This was a body of international law, which originally
began to develop in the late Middle Ages as clear rules of interaction
were required to support the increasing commerce and trade within
Europe. According to Benson,[^42] "Virtually every aspect of commercial
transactions in all of Europe (and in cases even outside Europe) were
'governed' by this body of law after the eleventh century".

The Law Merchant was produced and adjudicated in a bottom up fashion by
the merchants themselves, and success of enforcement largely relied on
the threat of ostracism for merchants that were unwilling to comply with
rulings. The laws were flexible, and their application was typically
speedy and efficient. After the early 1600s, however, these merchant
courts in England were supplanted by the common law courts in order to
centralize authority.[^43]

Among the Jewish financiers of Europe, the concept of a bearer
promissory note—in the sense of a bearer enforcing the terms in their
own right, not as an agent—was already understood and legally grounded
in the 13^th^ century.[^44] However, it seems that the wider judicial
system in the late Middle Ages was fairly resistant to the notion of
negotiability. While, occasionally, paper instruments with clauses that
mentioned a bearer or to the order of someone else were seen, those
clauses were generally interpreted as applying to agents of a principal.
They were not generally interpreted in the sense of negotiability.[^45]
A promissory note such as that from the goldsmiths could, therefore,
only have rightfully remained the property of the original beneficiary
and could not have circulated as a means of payment.

Evidence suggests that the legal movement towards granting the bearer of
a promissory note the same rights as the original creditor, was first
made in the Netherlands in the early 16^th^ century. According to Van
Der Wee,[^46] the key judicial verdict was in Antwerp in 1507. Other
Dutch cities, then, followed suit such as Bruges, Dordrecht, and
Utrecht, until an imperial edict in 1537 made the rules applicable to
the whole of the Netherlands. From here on out, a system of laws on
negotiable instruments was developed. In the second half of the 16^th^
century, Antwerp had a vibrant market in which promissory notes to
bearer were discounted and sold. The same is true for Hamburg at the end
of the century. When bills of exchange became more popular at the end of
the 16^th^ century, the need arose to bring transfer by endorsement into
the regime on negotiable instruments. This happened for both promissory
notes and bills of exchange to order in the early 17^th^ century.[^47]

According to Kohn,[^48] the development of laws on negotiable
instruments was developed first in the Netherlands due to the ban on
deposit banking that had been imposed by the Burgundian authorities at
the end of the 15^th^ century. Without deposit banking, negotiable
instruments facilitated payments.[^49]

So while the practices by the goldsmith bankers would have been
recognized by the Law Merchant, they were not yet established within
English common law. And when the common law courts in England were
confronted with cases pertaining to the negotiability of promissory
notes and bills of exchange—commonly from goldsmiths—they were slow
to react.[^50] Initially, these courts did not recognize the
transferability of such written instruments at all. Only by the end of
the 17^th^ century did the bill of exchange to order manage to carve out
an exception through a variety of court cases on the basis of merchant
customs. Promissory notes, however, were seen as part of contract law
and so continued to be non-negotiable. It was only by an act of
Parliament in 1704 that promissory notes were brought under the same
legal regime with regards to negotiability as as bills of exchange.[^51]
The negotiability of bearer promissory notes and bills of exchange
remained a point of confusion and was only firmly established by *Grant
v Vaughan* in 1764.[^52]

A key aspect to understand with regards to the goldsmith bankers is that
they also, naturally, loaned out their deposits. But they typically did
so by issuing goldsmiths' notes to borrowers, rather than directly
handing them over specie deposited in their vaults. Importantly, the
total value of the goldsmith notes issued in this manner plus those
issued by way of deposits, typically represented *more value* than
specie or other valuables than any goldsmith actually had in their
possession. So the goldsmiths were not just transforming one form of
money or value into another. They were actually *creating money*.

The goldsmith bankers probably also created money in another way. Bank
balances could also be used for payments within accounts across the same
bank and probably to other banks as well. Extending someone an
overdraft, thus, also constituted the creation of money. The same would
have been true for extending loans that were registered as account
balances, rather than extended with goldsmith notes. These practices
were common among deposit bankers elsewhere in the continent, so
probably also adopted by the goldsmith bankers.

The type of money system supported by the goldsmith bankers is often
called a **fractional reserve system**. Essentially, credit was extended
by offering the lenders promises to pay in gold and silver. Those
promises were either registered on a physical note, or as credit in a
bank account. And the goldsmith banks had less gold and silver available
than represented by all those outstanding claims. If the value of the
outstanding claims had been equivalent or smaller than the gold and
silver in the vaults, we would have called it a **full-reserve system**.

Full-reserve, or close to full-reserve, banks were certainly understood
and in existence at the time. The Amsterdam Bank of Exchange
("Amsterdamsche Wisselbank"), founded in 1609, seems to have essentially
been a full reserve bank, at least initially. And it is often lauded by
those who support full reserve banks. The bank's main purpose was to
alleviate via reliable credit money and book payments, the issues with
metal coins experienced in the city.[^53] The two public banks created
in Venice around the same time in response to a long history of failures
of private banks in the city, the Banco di Rialto (1587) and the Banco
del Giro (1619), also aspired to be full reserve banks.[^54]

While the goldsmiths notes indeed circulated throughout London in
exchange for goods and services, it is probably a stretch to say they
were money by the late 17^th^ century. The notes simply do not seem to
have enjoyed the general acceptability we commonly expect from
money.[^55] As noted by Horsefield,[^56] "it would seem that the users
of goldsmiths' notes might be likened to the players of a game such as
'musical parcels', in which the loser is the one holding the parcel when
the music stops". They would have been hampered, at least in part, by
the uncertain status of negotiability in English common law as well as
the higher turnover of the goldsmith bankers. Instead, the first
banknotes to have developed into full money appear to have been the
banknotes issued by the Bank of England, though only early into the
18^th^ century.[^57]

The goldsmiths' notes were the main source of inspiration for the
banknotes that became commonly issued by the Bank of England. They were,
thus, a forerunner of the modern banknote. However, they were hardly the
first promissory notes to circulate in this manner. The House of Fugger
was a prominent family of merchant bankers and venture capitalists in
15^th^ and 16^th^ century Europe. Already early in the 16^th^ century,
their promissory notes circulated freely in Antwerp, just as the
goldsmiths' notes had in 17^th^ century London, but with better legal
protections. According to Van Der Wee,[^58]

> Some firms were able to acquire such an image of financial strength
> and probity on the \[Antwerp\] Exchange that their writings obligatory
> enjoyed increasing confidence and passed freely and smoothly from hand
> to hand. The Fuggerbriefe were a striking example of this in Antwerp.
> Thus the way was opened for the transferable promissory notes—which
> were to have such a great future in the England of the seventeenth
> century.

Elsewhere in the world, the Chinese already appeared to have had
government bearer bonds that circulated like currency in the 9^th^
century; other types of debt instruments circulated in a similar
fashion.[^59] These experiences eventually led the Chinese to start
minting paper notes and declare them of value by royal decree. Thes were
the notes that so surprised Marco Polo when he came to China during the
Yuan Dynasty at the end of the 13^th^ century.[^60]

Going even further back, it seems that the Ancient Babylonians already
had bearer negotiable instruments—in the sense of the bearer having
the full rights of the original holder—four to five thousand years
before the goldsmiths.[^61] Supposedly any clay tables with an
acknowledgment of debt could be freely transferred and circulated like
cash. Read notes that, given the long and complicated path of the
introduction of negotiable instruments into our systems of laws and the
false presumption of the modernity of those ideas, "it is interesting to
note that they were an essential part of the commercial law of Babylonia
five thousand years ago".[^62]

The goldsmith banks could have potentially played a substantial role
during the English Industrial Revolution (ca. 1760 to 1830). But, as
convincingly argued by Temin and Voth,[^63] it was instead marginal.
They contributed little in terms of venture capital, working capital,
and other forms of capital investment into commerce and manufacturing.
The main reason was that the English government stifled the development
of private sector banks throughout the period.

After the overthrow of James II in the **Glorious Revolution**, William
and Mary ascended to the throne ruling over England, Scotland, and
Ireland. Unlike James II, William was not friendly to France, and his
reign ushered in more than a century of wars in which the rivalry
between England and France was a common theme. The constant state of war
only ended with the Napoleonic Wars (1803 – 1815), opening the way for
the **Pax Britannica** that lasted until the start of World War I.

Particularly in order to finance wars, substantial efforts were made to
establish a steady and attractive source of credit to the English
government.[^64] And these efforts to establish public finance on a
stable footing seem to have come at the expense of the development of
private finance. Notably, the government granted the Bank of England the
exclusive right to act as an incorporated bank in 1697,[^65] and
prohibited all partnership banks with more than six partners from
issuing bank notes in 1709.[^66] Another key factor was the reduction of
the maximum interest rate from 6% to 5% in 1714, where the lessening of
the debt burden from the Nine Years War (1688–1697) and the War of
Spanish Succession (1701–1714) was a key motivation.[^67]

The consequence of such measures to boost public finance was to suppress
private finance. As a result, economic growth was much less impressive
during the Industrial Revolution than it could have been. In the words
of Temin and Voth,

> The apparent paradox of slow growth in times of rapid technological
> change disappears when we examine the role of private finance.... It
> failed to fulfill its potential because of heavy-handed regulation and
> wartime shocks. Had wars not interfered, British economic growth might
> have been more rapid in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth
> centuries. Rates of profit, while naturally high in the beginning,
> would have fallen quickly; wages could have been higher. Instead of
> following a balanced growth path, on which rapid and widespread
> technological change should have led to faster growth and higher total
> factor productivity, the British economy was hit by a sequence of
> negative, war-related shocks that reduced aggregate demand.[^68]

That all said, the goldsmiths' notes that were made out to a bearer
really became the precursor to modern English banknotes. The earliest
known checks—at least those that could be made out to
anyone[^69]—also stem from the goldsmith bankers in the 17^th^
century.[^70]

Traces of the goldsmith banks are still within the English financial
system. C. Hoare & Company (1672) is an independent private bank owned
by direct descendants of the founder Sir Richard Hoare.[^71] Both Coutts
& Company (1692) and Drummonds Company (1712) were founded by goldsmiths
and are now part of the NatWest Group.[^72] Barclays became a
joint-stock bank in 1896, as the result of a merger of twelve banks from
London and the regions. They trace their history, at least in name, back
to one of the banks from the merger: Barclay, Bevan, Bening and Tritton.
This company was a descendant of the bank that was originally started in
the late 17^th^ century by two goldsmiths, John Freame and Thomas
Gould.[^73]

## 2. The nature of the goldsmith bankers

It is illustrative here to dismiss two common claims about the goldsmith
bankers. First, it is sometimes claimed that the goldsmith bankers
invented fractional reserve banking. Second, it is often claimed that
the goldsmith bankers were acting nefariously or illegaly through having
fractional reserves.

The first claim is grossly inaccurate. Deposit banking was well and
truly established throughout Europe when it finally arrived as a common
practice in England. According to Kohn, deposit banking in the late
Middle Ages probably sprang from Genoa in the 12^th^ century. From there
it spread to other Italian cities and the rest of Europe. According to
De Roover, in the fourteenth century, "every merchant, every broker,
every innkeeper, and every drapier had \[an account\], and probably also
good many high-class retailers such as mercers, furriers, and
goldsmiths, not to mention nobles and other persons who were not in
business at all".[^74] Within commercial centers, the use of deposit
banks was widespread.[^75]

And fractional reserve banking was really the norm with regards to
deposit banking. Credit money could be created through paper instruments
or through the creation of book money via loans and overdrafts. The
Banco di Rialto Banco and the Banco del Giro which we mentioned earlier,
for example, were founded with the intention of acting as full reserve
banks precisely to combat the long history of failures of private,
fractional reserve banks in the city.

In fact, we should note that that fractional reserve banking was
re-introduced or re-invented in the late Middles Ages, or perhaps just
became common again, rather than that it was invented. Fractional
reserve banking and the process of money creation through credit
operations also existed in the Ancient world. As noted by Gabrielson,
for example, during the Hellenestic period, a professional class of
financial middlemen known as **trapezites** offered the following
services:

> They accepted from clients both interest-bearing and
> non-interest-bearing deposits; they provided 'landed' and probably
> also 'maritime' credit; they expedited commercial transactions by
> issuing written confirmation of availability of unds in banks
> accounts; moreover, they executed payment orders (sometimes using
> interbank arrangements) through the procedure of *diagraphe*, i.e. the
> cashless settlement of debts through bank entries, thereby *creating
> 'unbacked fiat money' and credit*. Finally, they witness transactions,
> safeguarded documents and accepted valuables for safekeeping.[^76]

What Gabrielsen means here is that credit payments could be made using
overdrafts, in effect creating money. While trapezites seem to have
existed as early as the fifth century B.C. in Athens, similar
professionals settled in Rome at the end of the fourth century B.C. They
were known in Latin as *argentarii*.[^77] Other professionals also
engaged in deposit banking later in the Republican period and during the
Imperial period, including the *nummularii* and the *coactores
argentarii*. These deposit bankers were mostly located in Rome, but were
also found in the provinces.[^78] The evidence suggests that they
practised fractional reserve banking at a significant scale through the
circulation of certain "paper" instruments—in a manner akin to the
goldsmiths—as well as the creation of book money through lending and
overdrafts.[^79]

Historical evidence of deposit banking goes back much further than the
Athenians and Romans to the Ancient Babylonians and Egyptians. As noted
by Miklinski, "\[k\]nowledge of banking activities, including deposits,
is as old as the oldest sources of legal knowledge altogether".[^80]
While we are unaware of any sources that specifically mention fractional
reserve banking practices among those even more ancient societies, it
seems more likely than not that the creation of credit money through
lending and overdrafts would have existed before the Ancient Greeks. As
soon as some society adopts basic accounting methods and bookkeeping,
the step to creating credit money through lending is not too far off.

Overall, the evidence suggests that the history of banking is very much
the history of fractional reserve banking. The banks that have operated
on a full-reserve basis, or at least had the presumption of operating on
such a basis, seem to have been government-sponsored banks such as the
already mentioned Banco di Rialto Banco (1587), the Banco del Giro
(1619), and the Amsterdam Bank of Exchange (1609).[^81] Fractional
reserve banking has predominated banking throughout various types of
regimes, cultures, and time periods. The conditions necessary for that
practice to flourish just seem not have been present in England before
the 17^th^ century. As Kohn states, "\[t\]he London goldsmiths were
acting as custodians of cash and valuables as early as the fifteenth
century. When a demand for banking emerged in the seventeenth century,
they proved quite able to transform themselves into bankers".[^82]

A second claim that is commonly made is that the goldsmith bankers were
acting nefariously or illegally, as depositors were under the impression
that they were storing gold and silver with them for safekeeping. But
this seems very unlikely.[^83]

To start, as has already been shown, fractional reserve banking would
have been a long-standing historical practice before the goldsmith
bankers ever turned to it. While maybe it was not that common in
England, the idea that it would have been re-invented entirely in a
vacuum is not plausible. And at the time, European legal regimes
generally recognized the distinction between deposits for purely
safekeeping purposes and deposits that could be loaned out again. That
legal distinction goes back all the way to Ancient Rome, where deposits
placed in sealed bags or containers were treated differently than
unsealed deposits. The former were treated as objects for safekeeping,
while the latter were treated as a loan.[^84] What characterized bankers
as a profession was "receiving deposits and advancing credit. The banker
lent, not his own money, but at least some of the money that he had
received from his clients".[^85]

There are various additional reasons for why a conviction that the
goldsmith bankers were acting nefariously or even illegally at the time
seems wrong.

- Deposits commonly paid interest rates. While towards of the 17^th^
  century interest was often not paid on deposits, the bankers typically
  did not charge fees. How else could the goldsmith bankers have made
  money other than by lending it out?

- Deposits were typically registered through assignable and bearer
  promissory notes. None of these notes suggest there is a claim on some
  specific gold and silver, rather than just a general claim on gold and
  silver.

- No court cases or writings at the time suggestion that the act of
  fractional reserve banking was an issue. Even the incendiary pamphlet
  mentioned earlier, "Mystery of the new fashioned goldsmiths or
  bankers", does not mention the illegality of loaning out deposits
  intended for safekeeping as one of the crimes of the goldsmith
  bankers.
