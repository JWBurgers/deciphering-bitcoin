# Chapter 3: The Commercial Revolution

After the Fall of the Western Roman Empire, most of Europe became markedly less complex in its economic, cultural, and administrative organization. Compared to Roman Europe, society was predominantly agricultural, manorial, and self-sufficient. As the Early Middle Ages drew to a close, however, Europe began to enter into a long period of economic ascendancy that saw an expansion of trade, advances in financial and business practices, population growth, increasing urbanization, and the introduction of new technologies. With some interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of the Middle Ages and into the early modern period.

Our modern history of money, payments, and banking largely begins during Europe’s economic ascendancy in the High and Late Middle Ages. Various innovations and developments from that period are the subject of the next three chapters. This chapter focuses on explaining the transition in Europe from a petty capitalist to a commercial capitalist system, and two key developments needed to understand that transition: the invention of exchange contracts and the rise of the medieval fairs.  

In *Sections 1* and *2*, we first briefly describe the economic, political, and social conditions in the Early Middle Ages and the economic ascendancy that sets in around the year 1000. We, then, discuss exchange contracts and the rise of medieval fairs in *Sections 3* and *4*, respectively. We finally describe the transition of Europe from a petty capitalist to a commercial capitalist system in *Section 5*. 


## 1. The Early Middle Ages

From the end of the fourth century onwards, the Roman Empire was permanently divided into a Western Roman Empire and an Eastern Roman Empire, each with their own emperors.<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> At the time of the split, the western half of the Empire roughly covered the area of Continental Europe below the Rhine and Danube until modern-day Croatia and Bosnia-Herzegovina in the East, the island of Great Britain until Hadrian’s Wall, and the northern coastline of Africa stretching from modern-day Morocco to Egypt.  

The fall of the Western Roman Empire is typically dated to 476. In that year Odovacer—a commander of various groups of foederati, barbarian troops serving the Western Empire—deposed Romulus Augustus in Ravenna, and declared himself the King of Italy. But the ending of the empire was, of course, hardly a single event. 

Great Britain was left by Roman troops already in the early fifth century, ushering in an era of immigration by continental tribes—notably the Saxons, Angels, Jutes, and Frisians—that lasted well-into the sixth century. The Vandals had conquered the northern part of Africa by 439 and famously sacked the city of Rome in 455. And many areas in Roman Continental Europe had fully or largely already come, by 476, under the control of various tribes, including the Ostrogoths, Visigoths, Franks, Burgundians, Lombards, and others. Many of these tribes had been displaced into Western and Central Europe by the extremely violent incursions of the Huns—a people who originated somewhere deep from within the Eurasian Steppe—during the late fourth and fifth centuries. 

When the final emperor, Romulus Augustus, was deposed, Europe had, therefore, to a large extent already transformed into a collection of kingdoms. These kingdoms were only loosely associated with certain regions and the “inhabitants” probably identified more with charismatic warrior kings and leaders, rather than certain territories.  

The period of European history from the fall of the Western Roman Empire until the year 1000 is standardly called the Early Middle Ages. Traditionally, it has often also been called the Dark Ages. That label was inspired by the supposed political, societal, and economic collapse of Roman Europe in the fifth century and the region’s subsequent period of prolonged stagnation. It should be noted that modern historians and archaeologists have varied judgements on the severity and speed of the impact of the events of the fifth century, and the degree to which a characterization of the subsequent period as the “Dark Ages” is justified.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup> Overall, however, scholars do broadly agree that the European territories formerly within the Western Roman Empire were markedly different by the ninth and tenth centuries than under Roman rule.  

Roman political, commercial, and social life had centered around its towns. But during the Early Middle Ages, their populations dwindled, often drastically. Many towns such as Trier and Tongeren were practically abandoned for a long period.<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup> While Rome might have housed almost a million inhabitants in the early part of the millennium, by the early ninth century that number had dropped to a few tens of thousands.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup> By the ninth century, European building standards were much less sophisticated. Scholarship and literacy was much less widespread and mostly confined to the monasteries. Administrative and taxation systems were generally less sophisticated. And the economy was largely manorial, self-sufficient, and less specialized than in Roman times.   

In particular, any trade—understood here broadly as all the voluntary economic exchange of goods—was generally at a local level. Long-distance trade was, by contrast to Roman times, a minor economic activity.<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup> According to De Roover,<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> “\[w\]hat little \[long-distance trade\] survived was carried on by groups of travelling merchants who catered for the rich by selling them luxuries or who exploited the poor by charging high prices for necessities in times of famine or distress”. Given the hazardous conditions, a merchant “had to possess a great deal of physical endurance and be ready to face many hardships”.<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> In addition, “moral stamina and some intellectual baggage were indispensable prerequisites to success”.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup>

The fall of the Western Roman Empire did not impact all parts of Roman Europe equally. The overall decline seems to have been most significant in the north of Europe.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup> In what had once been Roman Britain, for example, few remnants of classical civilization apparently survived the start of the Early Middle Ages, as pagan Anglo-Saxon control over the region spread in the fifth and sixth centuries.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup> Despite the overall stronger regression in the north, Dean and Waley still offer the following reflection on the transformation that took place in Italy in the Early Middle Ages:<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup>

>> Between the fifth and tenth centuries AD \[municipal\] institutions were shattered and the very nature of political and social authority was reshaped by a series of events which included the conquests and settlements of Goths and Lombards. The gradual but profound transformation of these centuries involved a great contraction in city life and an almost complete break in urban government. A few of the larger cities, among them Rome itself, and Milan, survived as important centers of population, but most of the ancient municipalities must have declined into sordid if picturesque decay, villages in a setting of classical ruins.

The area of Europe outside of the Western Roman Empire was predominantly inhabited by agricultural and tribal societies. This very much remained that way after the events of the fifth century. In general, these peripheral areas were also marked by very limited urbanization, little long-distance trade, unspecialized economic activities, low rates of literacy, and so on. The regions in scope here are those north of the Rhine, as well as north and north-east of the Danube; modern-day Scandinavia; and the parts of the British Isles that were not settled by the Romans, including modern-day Ireland and Scotland. Notably, excluded from the overall picture of an economically undeveloped and rural Europe during the Early Middle Ages were the Iberian Peninsula and Byzantium.

The Visigoths, a Germanic tribe, notably sacked Rome in 410 and then in the early fifth century established a kingdom centered around southwestern Gaul, a Roman name for a region that encompassed much of Western Europe including modern-day France and Belgium. The Visigoths then increasingly expanded into the Iberian Peninsula. After a decisive defeat by the Franks at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, the Visigoths largely retreated into the Iberian Peninsula, eventually establishing their capital in Toledo. The experience of Visigothic Spain was much the same as the rest of Roman Europe at the beginning of the Early Middle Ages. However, in 711 the region was invaded by Arab Muslims with profound consequences. 

From the early seventh century onwards, Arab Muslim forces rapidly expanded their territory from their base in Medina, first under the leadership of Mohammed (570 – 632), later under the leadership of the Rashidun (632 – 661) and Umayyad (661 – 750) caliphs. They invaded the Iberian Peninsula in 711 and were only stopped near Paris at the Battle of Tours in 732 by the Franks under the military leadership of Charles Martel (the “hammer”). From then onwards the Muslim occupation of Europe was largely confined to the Iberian Peninsula. After the takeover of the Caliphate by the Abbasids (750 – 1258), the Umayyads actually fled to Spain and established the Emirate of Córdoba, the first independent Islamic state. Under Muslim rule, the region—then known as Al Andalus—appears to have been much more urbanized and developed than the rest of Europe until the end of the Early Middle Ages. 

Next, the Eastern Roman Empire was much less affected by the barbarian migrations and invasions that marked the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries, and continued to exist after Romulus Augustus was deposed in the West. While we often also call it Byzantium—after the Greek colony that was originally established at Constantinople—the inhabitants themselves would have probably just seen themselves as Romans and their polity as the continuation of the Roman Empire. After 476, Byzantium continued to thrive, even re-conquering much of the Mediterranean under the leadership of Justinian I in the sixth century. Another notable way in which Byzantium diverged from Europe in this period is by Greek replacing Latin as the primary language.  

Byzantium shrank significantly in the seventh century as a result of the Arab Muslim invasions. Notably, it lost much of the Levant and adjacent territories, as well as the territories on the north African coast. From the early eighth century onwards, the boundaries of the Byzantine Empire continued to shift substantially until the end of the Early Middle Ages. If we largely confine ourselves, however, to the core region of Byzantium, namely Constantinople, Anatolia, and Greece, the empire seems to have been, in general, substantially more wealthy and urbanized than the rest of Europe during the Early Middle Ages. 

A key aspect about Europe during the Early Middle Ages were the frequent waves of raiding, migration, and invasion. We have already mentioned the Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, Franks, Saxons, and other tribes that transformed Roman Europe in the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries. While Islam was confined to the Iberian Peninsula by the middle of the eighth century, Muslims nevertheless continued to intensively raid Mediterranean Europe until the tenth century. Other notable waves of raiding, migration, and invasion during the Early Middle Ages came from the Magyars and Vikings. 

From the late ninth until the middle of the tenth century, much of Western and Central Europe was the subject of intense and extremely violent cavalry raids by the Magyars, a people that originally came from the steppes east of the Carpathian mountains. At the Battle of Lechfeld (955), the Magyars suffered a heavy defeat against the East Frankians under the leadership of King Otto I. The raids ended and the Magyars settled in the region of modern-day Hungary.  

The Vikings, in turn, is a collective name for the various peoples from Denmark, Norway, and Sweden at the time. At the turn of the ninth century, they began raiding the British Isles and much of Continental Europe, predominantly along the coastlines and river routes. Later in the ninth century, they also began to migrate and settle in certain areas. Notable lasting Viking settlements and integration with local populations occurred in modern-day Scotland, Ireland, England, France, and in an area that spans across modern-day Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia.   

In the region of modern-day England, under the leadership of the king of Wessex, Alfred the Great, the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms united to halt the progress of the Vikings by the end of the ninth century. The territory in Eastern and Northern England settled by Vikings and governed by their laws—known as Danelaw (865–954)—was eventually integrated back into Anglo-Saxon territory under the leadership of Alfred’s son Edward the Elder. The entire unified territory increasingly became known as “England” and its people as the “English”. Supposedly, Athelstan (ca. 894 – 937), son of Edward the Elder, was the first to use the title “King of the English”. In the early eleventh century, Cnut the Great (ca. 995–1035), son of the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard (ca. 960 – 1014), briefly established the so-called North Sea Empire, consisting of England (1016), Denmark (1018), and Norway (1028). Supposedly, he was the first to use the title “King of England”. Shortly after Cnut the Great’s death, in 1042, England came back under Anglo-Saxon rule with the ascendancy of Edward the Confessor to the throne. 

In Ireland, Scotland, and other parts of the British Isles, the Vikings also made lasting settlements, notably founding cities such as Dublin, Cork, and Dundee. In Western Europe, the Viking leader Rollo in 911 made a deal with Charles the Simple, the Carolingian king of West Francia (or simply Francia). In exchange for the territory of Normandy—or “land of the Northmen”—Rollo would convert to Christianity, swear allegiance to Charles, and defend West Francia against other Viking raiders. In 987, Hugh Capet assumed the throne of West Francia. During the early years of the Capetian dynasty (987–1328), (West) Francia increasingly became known as France.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup>

Finally, a group of mostly Swedish Vikings known as the Varangians established a large state known as Kyivan Rus in the late ninth century. The territory eventually spanned the modern-day territory of Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia. The state originally had its capital at Kyiv, but eventually started to splinter into different principalities in the eleventh century. The Varangians that settled in the region mixed with the Slavs, Finnic, and other peoples, and eventually the inhabitants of the region largely adopted the Slavic identity.

In the Early Middle Ages, fairly large territories were, at times, united under single rulers. The Ostrogoths ruled Italy and neighboring areas between 493 and 553. In the sixth century, Justinian I, emperor of the Eastern Roman Empire or Byzantium, once again regained control over much of southern Europe. The Anglo-Saxons largely unified England by the end of the 10th century, and Kyivan Rus was also a fairly large state by the end of the tenth century. But most notable in this regard are certainly the Franks. 

Clovis I (466–511), king of the Salian Franks, united the various Frankish tribes during his reign across a territory roughly equivalent to Roman Gaul, a region that encompassed much of Western Europe including modern-day France and Belgium. After his death, Clovis’ territory was split into several separate Frankish kingdoms. The Merovingian dynasty that ruled these various kingdoms—named after Clovis’ grandfather Merovech—lasted until 751. At this point, the Merovingians were deposed and the Franks united under the son of Charles Martel, Pepin the short.

Pepin rapidly began expanding the territory of the united Franks. That expansion continued under the reign of his son, Charles the Great (742–814). The latter was famously crowned Holy Roman Emperor by Pope Leo III in 800 to revive the idea of the Western Roman Empire, at least in the mind of the pope. Charlemagne’s kingdom covered practically all of Western and Central Europe. He was succeeded by his son Louis the Pious (814–843). After the death of Louis, the empire was divided among his sons into three large kingdoms through the Treaty of Verdun (843): West Francia, roughly the region of modern France and Belgium; East Francia, roughly the region of modern Germany and some surrounding territories; and Middle Francia, a large strip of land from the low countries into northern Italy. 

As already mentioned, East Francia was the precursor state to modern France. The Carolingian dynasty lasted until 987 when it made way for the Capetian dynasty. While the main line of the Capetians ended with the death of Charles IV in 1328, cadet branches essentially ruled France until the French Revolution (1792) and briefly in the nineteenth century.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup> During the early years of this dynasty, East Francia or Francia became increasingly known as France. Middle Francia, in turn, quickly disintegrated into various regions after the Treaty of Verdun, and the Carolingian rulers dissappeared in the tenth century. Finally, in East Francia, the Carolingian dynasty would last until 911. During the tenth century, the people would increasingly be called Teutons or Germans, and the territory would increasingly come to be called the “Kingdom of Germany”, particularly by outsiders. The German King Otto I (912–973) was, in 962, crowned Holy Roman Emperor by the pope. The Holy Roman Empire with the Kingdom of Germany at its core only ceased as a political entity in 1806, after Napoleon had crushed Austrian, Russian, and English forces at the Battle of Austerlitz (1805).   

While many polities during the Early Middle Ages, thus, covered large territories, it is important to note that these sizeable polities were often really little more than loosely united and usually not closely governed and administered. Throughout the period, influence was instead largely with local and regional rulers such as counts and princes. The rule of Charlemagne over the united Franks and various other peoples might have been more organized and one of the few such exceptions to the general decentralized political character of the Early Middle Ages. In any case, nothing like the organized rule of the Western Empire over Roman Europe was achieved in the period.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup>


## 2. Economic expansion

While politically and administratively, Europe was generally very decentralized during the end of the Early Middle Ages, what did increasingly unite the continent was Christianity and the church. 

During the fourth and fifth centuries, many of the barbarian tribes turned to Christianity. However, many adopted a particular variant known as Arianism, a Christian doctrine which had arisen in the fourth century and saw Christ as not being co-eternal or consubstantial with God.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup> This conviction was, in contrast, to the official doctrine set at the church’s first ecumenical council at Nicaea (325), which decreed that God, the holy spirit, and Christ were co-eternal and consubstantial. 

By contrast to the other Barbarian tribes, Clovis I in 508 converted to Catholic Christianity. Various other continental tribes subsequently began converting to Catholic Christianity from Arianism or Paganism, including the Visigoths, Lombards, and Burgundians. The Irish supposedly converted to Catholic Christianity during the fifth and sixth centuries. According to legend, much of the effort was led by St. Patrick. The Anglo-Saxons in England mostly converted to Roman Catholic Christianity from Paganism in the seventh century. Charlemagne forced the Saxons in modern-day Germany to convert at the end of the eighth century. By the end of the ninth century, Christianity began spreading among Viking settlements and in Scandinavia. In the late tenth century, the first ruler of the Polish state, Mieszko I, was baptized. In the early eleventh century, the Hungarian king Stephen I began promoting Christianity among his people. 

So, by the end of the tenth century, Europe had largely Christianized under the banner of the Roman Catholic Church, though some regions had also converted under the auspices of Byzantium. Under Charlemagne’s reign particularly, much effort had been expended on standardizing practices and customs in churches and monasteries. He had also standardized the Roman alphabet in his effort to preserve books, manuscripts, and learning. In addition to the spread of Christianity, by the end of the tenth century, the raids, migrations, and invasions that had so marked the Early Middle Ages also had largely subsided. Only Eastern Europe would experience another such event, namely the invasion of the Mongols in the thirteenth century. 

It is in this environment of relatively more order and stability that Europe enters into a long period of economic ascendancy in the High Middle Ages (1000–1300). This period saw an expansion of trade, advances in financial and business practices, population growth, increasing urbanization, and the introduction of new technologies. With some interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of the Middle Ages and into the early modern period (1500–1800). The key era of economic development was probably the twelfth to early fourteenth century, after which economic expansion was interrupted by wars, famines, and epidemics, notably the Black Death (1347–1351). While traditionally most of the Late Middle Ages (1300–1500) was seen as one of abject depression by historians, it now appears the interruptions to economic progress were not nearly as severe and long-lasting as originally envisioned.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup>

Data on population growth and urbanization can give some sense of the developments during the High and Late Middle Ages. A modern degree of accuracy for such data is beyond our reach. But, indicatively, it has been estimated that, from 1000 to 1300, the population of Europe roughly doubled and the percentage of inhabitants in towns over 5,000 more than doubled (to over 10%). Supposedly the rate of urbanization approached 30% in northern Italy and the Low Countries by 1300.<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup> While the Black Death decimated the European population in the fourteenth century, by 1500, the population size seems to have more than recovered.<sup>[18](#footnote18)</sup> Also, indicatively, Van Werveke identifies a number of cities in Italy that by the middle of the fourteenth century had around 50,000 inhabitants or more: Milan, Venice, Florence, Bologna, Genoa, Rome, Naples, and Palermo. Elsewhere in Europe at that time, he estimates populations of around 50,000 for Ghent; 35,000 for Bruges; 35,000 to 45,000 for London; 40,000 for Cologne; and 80,000 for Paris.<sup>[19](#footnote19)</sup>

Economic growth rates at this time were moderate compared to modern growth rates. According to Maddisson, the economic growth rate for Europe between 1000 and 1500 was, on average, around a quarter of a percentage point.<sup>[20](#footnote20)</sup> Throughout most of human history, economic growth has largely been absorbed into population growth. Per capita GDP growth and living standards would, therefore, have generally increased very little, a phenomenon known as the malthusian trap. The same was largely true for Europe in the High and Late Middle Ages, though two regions start to show noticeable—though still moderate by modern standards—per capita GDP growth in the thirteenth century: Holland and Flanders. 

Throughout the early modern period, moderate per capita GDP growth is then mostly seen in Holland, England, and, from the seventeenth century onwards, in the American Colonies.<sup>[21](#footnote21)</sup> It is only during the Industrial Revolution in the early nineteenth century that per capita GDP growth rates start increasing to modern levels, first in England and then throughout Europe and the United States, as the efficiency gains from knowledge and technology improvements start to far outpace population growth.<sup>[22](#footnote22)</sup>

What was the main driver of the economic expansion throughout much of the High and Late Middle Ages and into the early modern period? 

Naturally, a variety of explanations exist for such a complex transformation and we cannot do justice to all of them here. That said, many historians broadly place the expansion of trade and the activities of the medieval merchants, made possible by the more stable conditions at the beginning of the High Middle Ages, at the forefront of explanations on the economic expansion that lasted into the early modern period.<sup>[23](#footnote23)</sup>

Throughout the period, agricultural production, artisanry, and basic manufacturing was typically centered around household enterprises that were based on the nuclear or extended family. Other than at a local level, these producers would rarely engage in trade directly. Instead, particularly trade at the regional level and beyond was the domain of professional merchants. They bought goods wholesale and sold them to other merchants and to end-consumers. They generally traded a great diversity of goods, and took advantage of whatever opportunities were present. Throughout the period, merchants were not really involved in production and mostly limited themselves to trade.<sup>[24](#footnote24)</sup> That said, they increasingly seem to have been involved in production during the Late Middle Ages.<sup>[25](#footnote25)</sup>

Trade until the early modern era was primarily in foodstuffs, textiles, and textile raw materials. But it also extended to timber, tar, metals, peat, coal, jewelry, arms, and many other products.<sup>[26](#footnote26)</sup> Output at that time lacked standardization and quality guarantees, so goods were rarely shipped to order on the basis of descriptions or samples;<sup>[27](#footnote27)</sup> instead, merchants transported goods from place to place, so that inspections and purchases could be made on the spot. This venturing nature to trade was situated in an environment of slow transport times and risks from pirates, bandits, government authorities, and not always reliable transport services. Merchants were largely operating as venturers well into the early modern period.<sup>[28](#footnote28)</sup> This venturing nature to trade explains why many early English regulated and joint-stock companies have “adventurers” or “merchant adventurers” in their names.     

Kohn offers a detailed explanation for how the expansion of trade transformed Europe during the High and Late Middle Ages.<sup>[29](#footnote29)</sup> He argues that the key factor behind the expansion of trade was the decreased costs to trading, including in transaction costs, transportation costs, and financing costs.<sup>[30](#footnote30)</sup> And the resultant growth in trade was accompanied by population growth, urbanization, advances in finance and business practices, and technological progress. There were certainly substantial feedback loops at play. Expansion in trade, notably, generated investments in institutional and physical infrastructure, increased specialization in production, and further divisions of labor. These developments fostered technological progress. All of these factors, in turn, further expanded trade. 

From the start of the High Middle Ages into the early modern era, Europe generally consisted of two main economic zones: northern Italy and northwestern Europe. Different regions within these zones—understood broadly as the areas concentrated around towns of a few thousand inhabitants and upwards—traded with each other. The two zones also traded with each other as well as with Asia and, starting around the beginning of the early modern period, with the Americas. While long-distance trade between the zones has often been the subject of historical discussion, the bulk of trade in the Late and High Middle Ages was actually local and regional. Long-distance trade and to a lesser extent interregional trade was quantitatively much less important.<sup>[31](#footnote31)</sup>

Long-distance trade, however, was more crucial than its moderate quantitative scale might suggest, in particular because it led to the introduction of new types of goods, technologies, and ideas. Trade with Asia introduced Europe, for example, to new industries such as paper-making, gunpowder, cotton and silk textiles, glass, soap, ceramics and porcelain, sugar, and rice. Long-distance trade also introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to the son of a Pisan customs official in Algeria, Leonardo da Pisa (ca. 1170–1250), better known as Fibonacci. His books, most famously his *Liber Abaci* (1202), were instrumental in spreading awareness throughout Europe about the Hindu-Arabic counting system, though it took several hundred years for the system to be really embraced.<sup>[32](#footnote32)</sup> Interregional trade, in turn, seems to have had the most extensive impact on the specialization of production, the division of labor, and technological progress in manufacturing and agriculture.<sup>[33](#footnote33)</sup>


## 3. Exchange contracts  

With regards to long-distance payments, commercial credit, and financial arbitrage and speculation, the single most important financial innovation that emerged among Italian merchants during the High Middle Ages was that of the exchange contract. It was a very popular financial instrument from around 1200 well into the early modern period. We briefly discuss the history and mechanics of exchange contracts here, and then turn to their functions more elaborately elsewhere. 

According to Baumgartl,<sup>[34](#footnote34)</sup> merchants at the Fairs of Champagne—key international trade and financial events that were particularly important from the end of the twelfth century until about 1300—could already make purchases cashless and on credit through two types of financial instruments at the end of the twelfth century: the *lettre de foire* and the *instrumentum ex causa cambii* or *cambium* contract. Both documents contained essentially promises to pay a debt at a later time. The *lettre de foire* was a certificate only issued by the court of the fairs, and confirmed the promise of a buyer to pay a seller at a later fair. The cambium contract was, instead, written up by a merchant and officially notarized. It contained a promise to pay at a later date and place, but was not bound to the fairs in this regard. Often repayment was in a different currency. 

Others maintain roughly similar dates as Baumgartl for the emergence of the *cambium* contract. According the Kohn,<sup>[35](#footnote35)</sup> for example, the earliest record of one dates to 1220, though it probably indeed already existed in the twelfth century. And according to De Roover, the *cambium* contract was certainly popular in the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. Many examples exist from Genoese notarial records of the time, where money would be borrowed in Genoa through a *cambium* contract and repaid at one of the fairs in Champagne.<sup>[36](#footnote36)</sup>

The bill of exchange, initially called the *lettera di pagamento* and later the *lettera di cambio*, was the successor to the *cambium* contract. We cannot say exactly when it emerged. Our oldest surviving examples come from the early fourteenth century.<sup>[37](#footnote37)</sup> Indirectly, however, the records certainly indicate that it was already used in thirteenth century.<sup>[38](#footnote38)</sup> At Avignon, for instance, statutes referencing bills of exchange were already passed in 1253.<sup>[39](#footnote39)</sup> The Venetians, in turn, already had statutes pertaining to bills of exchange by 1272.<sup>[40](#footnote40)</sup> Keiley interestingly argues that the bill of exchange originated with the expulsion of the Jews from France in 1181 by Phillip II. As it was forbidden to take or send money out of the kingdom, Jews sold their personal property to trusted parties in France. They, then, settled in Lombardy and sold early versions of bills of exchange to Italian merchants who, then, had a claim on the proceeds kept in France.<sup>[41](#footnote41)</sup>

So what exactly is the bill of exchange?

The bill of exchange, unlike the *cambium* contract, is not a promise to pay, but a payment order. Also unlike the earlier *cambium* contract, the bill of exchange was not officially notarized. Instead, bills of exchange were written up by the merchants themselves. Consequently, they fell under the jurisdiction of merchant courts—specialized courts that enforced merchant customs<sup>[42](#footnote42)</sup>—rather than civil courts. 

The lack of notarization represented a substantial break with standard Italian business practices. During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, notaries were utilized for practically every kind of business contract in Italy, unless the transaction proceeded through a cash transfer. This included contracts for partnership agreements, capital investments from non-partners, loans, credit sales, banking operations, leases, labor and apprenticeship contracts, and so on.<sup>[43](#footnote43)</sup> The practice of writing up contracts among merchants naturally would have been supported with the increasing literacy and general education of merchants during the High Middle Ages, including through the *abbaco* schools, trade schools which first emerged in thirteenth century Italy.<sup>[44](#footnote44)</sup> It would have also been incentivized by the growing volume of trade and the time investment and hassle required for notarization. 

The recognition of handwriting on bills of exchange was extremely important. In the fifteenth century, for example, the Medici Company in Florence maintained lists of people authorized to write out bills of exchange for their foreign branches as well as for any companies with which they regularly executed exchange transactions. They also kept verified handwriting samples for these people. If handwriting on a bill of exchange was not recognized, it could lead to the rejection of the bill.<sup>[45](#footnote45)</sup>

Lets now turn to the mechanics of the bill of exchange. The standard version of the instrument involves four logically distinct parties in two locations. They are standardly known as the deliverer, the taker (or drawer), the payer (or drawee), and the payee. Essentially, in location A, the taker receives a sum of money from the deliverer; then, at a later agreed upon time, the payer gives a sum of money to the payee in location B. Sometimes the payer and payee agreed on the collection of the money at an earlier time against a small discount. Typically the two legs of the transaction involved two different currencies, usually those local to locations A and B. 

The deliverer and the taker in a bill of exchange were in some type of relationship with, respectively, the payee and payer in location B. If the deliverer were a merchant bank, for instance, the payee might have been a representative of the bank’s branch in location B or a representative from a different merchant bank acting as an agent. In such a case, the taker in location A was often a merchant, while the payer in location B was often a sales agent and banker for that merchant. The nature of the relationships involved differed. Most generally, we can describe the payee as a correspondent of the deliverer, and the payer as a correspondent of the taker. The term correspondent here obviously originates from the fact that these long-distance relationships were managed by correspondence.  

In some cases a bill of exchange might be due upon sight. Commonly repayment was also set to the date of an international fair.<sup>[46](#footnote46)</sup> In many cases, however, repayment was just set at a number of days from the issue of the bill, often at what was known as usance.<sup>[47](#footnote47)</sup>

Much of the traffic in bills of exchange occurred between large towns and cities that functioned as financial centers. In the Late Middle Ages, for instance, these included, to varying degrees, Bologna, Florence, Genoa, Milan, Naples, Palermo, Pisa, Rome, and Venice in Italy. Outside of Italy, they included, to varying degrees, Barcelona, Palma de Majorca, Valencia, Avignon, Montpellier, Bruges, Paris, and London.<sup>[48](#footnote48)</sup>

A bill of exchange between any two such towns and cities had a standardized period after which they were due from the moment of issuance. That period was known as usance. The usance between Florence and London, for instance, was 90 days in the Late Middle Ages, while it was 60 days between Florence and Bruges.<sup>[49](#footnote49)</sup> From London to Antwerp, Amsterdam, Middleburg, or Hamburg in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was 30 days.<sup>[50](#footnote50)</sup> Bills between towns and cities that functioned as financial centers were commonly due at usance, though recent evidence suggests that it may have been less frequently used than originally thought by historians.<sup>[51](#footnote51)</sup>

The key aspect about the bill of exchange, as well as the earlier *cambium* contract, is that money “transfers” from the deliverer in location A to the payee in location B *without requiring any shipment of specie, bullion, or other types of valuables*. By specie here, we mean gold and silver coins issued by a government or some private institution. By bullion, we mean gold and silver in the shape of bars and ingots. Other types of valuables might have been commodities such as grain or salt. So, no value, at least not in the physical sense, moved between locations A and B with a bill of exchange. 

To really understand the mechanics of bills of exchange, it is best to explore an actual example. Consider the following bill of exchange discussed by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli. It is from 1447 and translated from Italian.<sup>[52](#footnote52)</sup>

>> \[In the name of\] Jesus on the 7th of March 1447, in Venice. Pay by this first letter at usance to ser Allessandro Borromeo, son of messer Antonio, 400 ducats at the rate of exchange of 43.5 sterlings per ducat and this sum was received \[in Venice by Michele Zon\] from ser Giovanni Borromei & Partners. \[Addressed to\] Jacopo Salviati & Partners in London.

The religious opening here was extra encouragement for the taker and their correspondent to honor the bill. While the writing is brief and somewhat archaic, the details are fairly easy to comprehend. 

To start, the bill of exchange was written in Venice on March 7, 1447 by Michele Zon. He acknowledges to have received a payment from Giovanni Borromei & Partners of 400 Venetian ducats. In the fifteenth century, the Borromei family had three family lines that operated banking operations: a Milanese, a Florentine, and a Venetian line.<sup>[53](#footnote53)</sup> Giovanni Borromei & Partners is the name of the bank owned and operated by the Venetian line of the family. It also had branches in Bruges and London. So, here, Michele Zon is the taker (or drawer) and the Venetian Borromei bank the deliverer. 

The bill, then, instructs the Salviati company in London—who here, thus, have the role of the payer and are a correspondent of Michele Zon—to pay a sum of 400 Venetian ducats at the rate of 43.5 sterlings per ducat. At the time, London employed a system of account where a pound was equivalent to twenty shillings, and each shilling was equivalent to twelve pence. These units were denoted, respectively, by £, s, and d. The sterling unit in the bill refers to English pence. So the bill actually instructs the Salviati company to pay 17,400 pence, or £72 10s English pence. 

While we typically associate pounds with English currency, the system of pounds, shillings, and pence above was actually commonly employed for currency amounts throughout Europe during the Middle Ages and the early modern period. The system originated with the Romans who used the *libra* (pound), *solidus* (shilling), and *denarius* (penny) as units for weights and coins. 

The payee for the sum of money, £72 10s English pence, is a correspondent of the Venetian Borromei bank in London, Allessandro Borromeo. The latter was actually running the London branch of the bank. The payment was to be made at usance, which at the time was 90 days between Venice and London. So the payment was to be completed on June 5, 1447. 

The Salviati company and Allessandro Borromei would have been informed about the bill of exchange through correspondence. First, it would have likely been drawn up in multiple copies by Michele Zon. Then, in order to guarantee receipt, the Venetian Borromei bank probably sent two or more copies by different channels to Allessandro Borromeo. Michele Zon, in turn, may have sent several copies of a letter of advice (or *aviso*) to the Salviati company, instructing them to honor the bill of exchange. Alternatively, he may have included the matter of the bill in a more general business letter. 

Once Allessandro Borromeo received a copy of the bill of exchange from Venice, he would have presented it to the Salviati company in London. The latter would have “accepted” the bill under normal circumstances by writing *acetta* across it. On June 5, Allessandro Borromeo would, then, have returned with the accepted bill to collect his money from the Salviati company.  

Importantly, unlike the description of this situation might suggest, it is unlikely that any specie, bullion, or other form of physical value ever changed hands in either Venice or London. Instead, in Venice, Michele Zon likely just received a credit of 400 ducats with the Venetian Borromei bank. He probably also held an account with the Salviate company that would have been debited by £72 10s to facilitate the payment. In any case, both legs of the payment likely proceeded through credit transfers. 

While physical money or value did not move between locations A and B, the essence of a bill of exchange, it was also uncommon for any physical money or value to be involved *at* locations A and B. Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli, for instance, studied around 23,000 transactions from the bank branches in Bruges and London owned by the Milanese, and not the Venetian, line of the Borromei family. They conclude that “cash payments amounted to a very limited share of all transactions, even at a local level, with credits and debits transferred from one account holder to the other or to and from the bank”.<sup>[54](#footnote54)</sup> For another example, Kohn mentions that between 1456 and 1459 a bank in Genoa received 160,000 lire in payments from bills of exchange, of which 92.5% was settled in bank credit. Only the remaining 7.5% was in the form of cash settlement.<sup>[55](#footnote55)</sup>

The example above has shown the mechanics of the bill of exchange. While the form of the earlier *cambium* contract was different, the mechanics were very similar. One party would give a sum of money to another party at location A. The contract, then, specified that the recipient or their correspondent would pay a sum of money to the party that gave the sum or their correspondent at a later date in location B. A *cambium* contract typically also involved two different currencies. 

Due to the similarities, the “bill of exchange” in the literature sometimes refers to both the *lettera di pagamento* and the *lettera di cambio*, as well as the earlier *cambium contract*. For clarity, it is convenient to generally call them and similar instruments exchange contracts.  

A key aspect to exchange contracts is that they contained an inherent credit aspect. A sum of money was given in one place to be paid back at a later date in another place. However, interest was never explicitly charged within such contracts. Instead, interest rates were incorporated indirectly into the currency exchange rates. 

The Middle Ages and the early modern period were marked by highly restrictive usury laws—that is, laws which impose restrictions on the charging of interest rates for loans. While usury laws still exist today, they are generally much less strict. Sometimes usury laws banned charging interest outright, as in England until 1545.<sup>[56](#footnote56)</sup> More commonly, however, they imposed a maximum interest rate. Usury laws received much support from biblical and theological arguments and the church was often a key driving force behind them, though arguments from economists and other scholars also privded an intellectual foundation for them. Perhaps the key intellectual argument against usury laws that precipitated their lossening in recent history is Jeremy Bentham’s *Defense of Usury*, originally published in 1787.<sup>[57](#footnote57)</sup>

Throughout the Middle Ages and into the early modern period, exchange rates between different currencies were usually floating: that is, they fluctuated on the basis of market activities. In most cases where exchange contracts were used, these floating exchange rates presented a risk to the incorporated interest rates. As a consequence, exchange contracts typically fell outside of the scope of usury laws.<sup>[58](#footnote58)</sup> De Roover has often emphasized the evasion of usury restrictions for the popularity of exchange contracts. Others have also emphasized other factors, noting, for example, that the bill of exchange remained popular even after usury restrictions decreased.<sup>[59](#footnote59)</sup>


## 4. The Fairs of Champagne

The expansion of trade during the High and Late Middle Ages was accompanied by many institutional and technical advancements. One key institutional advancement was the increasing importance of the medieval fair. 

By a medieval fair, historians typically mean a certain type of periodic gathering for the purposes of trade and finance. Every fair was integrated into a larger network of such events for supporting interregional and interzonal trade. Fairs were mostly attended by professional merchants and marked by wholesale trade. They were also important financial markets, and sometimes they were really more financial than commercial markets. The Fairs of Besançon-Piacenza,<sup>[60](#footnote60)</sup> most prominent towards the end of the Middle Ages and at the beginning of the early modern period, were set up by Genoese merchant bankers purely for the purposes of serving as financial markets. 

By contrast, when historians of the period speak of “markets”, they more commonly refer to the periodic gatherings that focused on serving local and regional trade. While not devoid of wholesale trade, markets were generally more oriented towards consumers.<sup>[61](#footnote61)</sup>

Fairs and markets usually took place outside or in a courtyard.<sup>[62](#footnote62)</sup> They operated on the basis of privileges granted by ruling authorities who specified their rights and regulations, including specifications on dates, times, and location. Ruling authorities benefitted from fairs and markets through taxes, fees, and, certainly with regards to fairs, the broader financial opportunities these brought to their realm.<sup>[63](#footnote63)</sup> Fairs typically took place a few times per year, while markets could be weekly, monthly, or even annual.    

The famous medieval fair that took place at St. Denis, near Paris, had a history that can be traced back to the seventh century. But into the ninth century, there is scarcely any further information about similar events (though markets were certainly common). A sharp growth in the size and the number of fairs is, then, seen in the eleventh and twelfth centuries.<sup>[64](#footnote64)</sup> The most important fairs to emerge out of this period, already mentioned in the previous section, are certainly the Fairs of Champagne. 

In their heyday, the Fairs of Champagne consisted of six gatherings per year in four different towns, including Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and Bar-sur-Aube. There is some variation in the literature with regards to the supposed duration of these fairs. But indicatively, Sangster concludes each of them seems to have lasted between 46 and 52 days.<sup>[65](#footnote65)</sup>

Merchants from France, Flanders, Brabant, Spain, Germany, England, and elsewhere came to buy and sell their goods, including cloths and wool, silk, leather, furs, linens, spices, sugar, wax, grain, wines, and more. The key participants in these fairs were above all else the Italian merchants who used them foremost to purchase northern cloths in exchange for spices, silk, and other luxury items. Those northern cloths were subsequently distributed throughout the Mediterranean world.<sup>[66](#footnote66)</sup>

The four towns involved in the Fairs of Champagne already had trade events by the early twelfth century.<sup>[67](#footnote67)</sup> But, at that time, these events were just local or regional markets. During the twelfth century, however, they transformed into hubs for international trade and finance. While the fairs had already grown substantially in size by the middle of the century, a key development would have been the arrival of the Italian merchants in the last quarter of the century.<sup>[68](#footnote68)</sup> From the late twelfth century to around 1300, the Fairs of Champagne really served as the center of international trade and finance, after which they went into a quick decline in importance.<sup>[69](#footnote69)</sup>

The Fairs of Champagne were, at least by Medieval standards, colossal and intricately organized events. As illustratively described by Verlinden,<sup>[70](#footnote70)</sup>

>> The lords of the fairs were at first the counts of Champagne, and later the kings of France. They derived considerable revenues from the fairs: taxes on the residences and stalls of the merchants, entry and exit tolls, levies on sales and purchases, dues upon weights and measures, justice and safe-conduct charges upon the Italians and Jews. The chief officers attached to the fairs were the wardens of the fair who were responsible for order and justice and controlled the fair seal, at least until 1318 when a chancellor or keeper of the fair seal was appointed. Under them were the wardens’ lieutenants or clerks, together with sergeants-at-arms to maintain order. It should not be forgotten that, conjointly with the special jurisdiction set up for the fairs, the town magistrature and the local ecclesiastical dignitaries also exercised a certain authority over the merchants and their transactions. The court of the fair wardens was, however, a tribunal of capital importance as can be seen especially by the number of sergeants it employed. In 1317 there were no less than 140, although the Châtelet of Paris, about this time, had no more than 150. There were sixty notaries at the Châtelet and forty at the fairs. 

There is widespread conviction among historians that the Fairs of Champagne, in their heydays, operated with fairly sophisticated payment systems and also served as international financial markets.<sup>[71](#footnote71)</sup> With regards to both these aspects of the fairs, the *cambium* contracts and the *lettres de foire*, as mentioned in the previous section, would have been important financial instruments. Later in the thirteenth century and into the early fourteenth century, bills of exchange would have also played some role.  

Let’s first turn to the payment systems that operated at the fairs.  

The general impression is that relatively little specie actually changed hands during commercial transactions at the fairs. Many purchases of goods, instead, were made through credit in some form or another.<sup>[72](#footnote72)</sup> It is also quite clear that each fair functioned as a clearing house after all the commercial transactions had been completed, that is, as an institution which nets transactions to make a more simplified set of transactions. After determining a more simplified set of transactions, the remaining obligations would be settled.<sup>[73](#footnote73)</sup> This clearing and settlement system was regulated by the fair authorities.<sup>[74](#footnote74)</sup> While the operational details of the system are largely absent from the literature, we can broadly piece together how it might have operated.

To start, surely not every credit-based commercial transaction at a fair would have been processed by the clearing and settlement system. In some cases, sellers probably just agreed to extend credit to buyers beyond the period of the fair, perhaps to be settled at a next fair or at some other date and location. These transactions would have mostly proceeded through paper financial instruments (i.e., the *cambium* contract, the *lettre de foire*, or the bill of exchange). In some cases, merchants might have also directly extended credit beyond the period of a fair merely through book entries. In these credit-based commercial transactions, the merchants would have generally used witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical authorities.<sup>[75](#footnote75)</sup>

So, while some credit-based commercial transactions during a particular fair would have fallen outside of the scope of its clearing and settlement system, the majority of such transactions probably fell within its scope. It seems that merchants, in fact, kept track of the liabilities and rights incurred by the latter transactions, at least at some point, through documents called *carte*.<sup>[76](#footnote76)</sup> The credit-based commercial transactions at a particular fair processed by its clearance and settlement system would have been of two main types: those where the merchants extended credit directly, and those were an intermediary deposit banker was involved. 

When merchants arranged commercial exchanges on credit directly, in many cases they probably just made book entries for the transaction to be settled at the end of the fair.<sup>[77](#footnote77)</sup> They would have, then, created a *carte* as evidence for the debt under the presence of witnesses. In some cases perhaps only drawing up a *carte* was sufficient without making corresponding book entries. It is unlikely that the merchants used paper financial instruments for direct credit transactions that fell under the scope of the clearing and settlement system.  

Many credit-based commercial transactions would have instead utilized an intermediary deposit banker.<sup>[78](#footnote78)</sup> The Fairs at Champagne were marked by the presence of specialized proprietors, mostly Italian, that focused on (physical) currency exchange and deposit banking. Rather than the merchants arranging a credit relationship directly, the deposit banker would assume responsibility for the obligation to the creditor, while the buyer would become a debtor to the deposit bank for the value involved. Evidence of the transaction was probably also recorded with a *carte*.  

Trade at the Fairs of Champagne proceeded in structured segments. Notably, the northern merchants, mostly from Flanders and France, first sold their wool and cloths to the Italian merchants, before the latter sold their spices, silks, and other luxury goods to them. As the Italian merchants were the first to make purchases, their initial credit transactions via deposit bankers would have only been as debtors. Contrary to what one might expect, it does not appear that the Italian merchants deposited any specie at the beginning of a fair with the bankers to reduce the risks for the latter. The deposit bankers, in other words, were extending the Italian merchants significant credit in the early stages. Later in each fair, any Italian merchant could reduce their debt burden, and potentially build themselves up to a credit position, through the sale of their own goods. 

The appearance of deposit bankers at these fairs should not surprise us that much. The merchants also made credit arrangements with each other directly. But the bankers, as argued by Sangster,<sup>[79](#footnote79)</sup> generally would have enjoyed more trust within an environment with strangers. They would have reduced the financial risks for the merchants. Credit transactions were probably also conducted more efficiently via deposit bankers. Merchants directly engaging in a credit relationship would have probably placed a heavier burden on involving witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical authorities. 

At the end of each fair, all these commercial transactions that involved credit merely for the duration of the fair were, then, cleared and settled. While we do not have any detailed description of the procedures involved, it is not difficult to imagine how this clearing and settlement period might have roughly progressed. 

When deposit bankers were involved in a credit-based commercial transaction, they would have just recorded it in their accounting journal. In modern accounting, we keep running overviews of accounts using the accounting ledger. But such overviews were not yet used in the late thirteenth century.<sup>[80](#footnote80)</sup> So the deposit bankers probably just tallied all the transactions for their clients only at the end of the fair. With final positions for all clients in hand, the bankers would have compared each other’s books. As the bankers surely also had accounts with each other, they could have further simplified the obligations at the end of the fair. The bankers and the merchants probably would have also included credit-based transactions made directly between the merchants as part of the overall simplification process. 

Much time was probably spent not just on simplifying resultant transactions, but on verifying and confirming the records held by merchants and deposit bankers. Any debts outstanding at the end of the clearing period between the merchants and bankers could, then, be settled by various methods. 

According to Kohn,<sup>[81](#footnote81)</sup> if a merchant had a debt to a banker, he might settle it in cash, but more likely would carry it over to the next fair as an overdraft or borrow deposits from another merchant with a credit balance. For merchants with a credit balance, they could have been paid in specie or allowed to loan their credit to another merchant in debt to the deposit banker. Surely, as suggested by both Face and Sangster,<sup>[82](#footnote82)</sup> many of these outstanding debts between merchants and bankers as well as between merchants themselves would have also been settled with paper financial instruments (i.e., *cambium* contracts, *lettres de foire*, and bills of exchange), typically due at the next fair. 

Let’s now turn to subject of the Fairs of Champagne as international financial markets. 

First, obligations from paper financial instruments were often settled at the fairs (and probably from mere book credit as well). In some cases, these obligations may have arisen just out of a need for a general loan on the part of the borrower. But particularly with merchants, many of these debts were incurred during the regular course of business. A Genoese merchant in the thirteenth century, for example, was much more likely to buy silk, spices, and other wares through being the taker on a *cambium* contract than with cash. The merchants would have agreed on a total price and just specified on the contract that the merchant accepted goods for the value of the price. Given the focal point of the Fairs of Champagne for trade, the merchants often agreed to settle such debts at one of the upcoming fairs, not in the least because that is typically when the wares just bought on credit would be sold. 

Probably, the clearing and settlement system at a fair did not just process the majority of the credit-based commercial transactions incurred specifically during the course of the fair. More than likely, other outstanding debts due at the fairs would have been processed as well. Given all these various debts and obligations, and the potential for conflicts in the records to arise, it is hardly surprising that the clearing and settlement period of each fair has been estimated to take several weeks.<sup>[83](#footnote83)</sup>

The Fairs of Champagne were also an attractive venue for obtaining loans. Sometimes these loans might have been extended to merchants that required capital, either for business at the fair or elsewhere. It is also clear, however, that loans were acquired at the fairs by outsiders. These would have normally been aristocrats, clergy, or other high-status individuals. The counts in Champagne frequently guaranteed such loans, as the merchants and bankers were hesitant to extend loans without such guarantees.<sup>[84](#footnote84)</sup> On the flip side, people that had excess funds came to the fairs to loan funds to the bankers and merchants who, in turn, would have loaned them out to others. Loans incurred in this manner, as has already been suggested above, were probably often settled during the clearing and settlement phase of some subsequent fair.

An interesting and educational question is how the loans above were granted exactly. Currency exchange was a substantial part of the business of the deposit bankers, and loans would certainly also have been extended by them through specie. Illustratively, Bautier notes that in the second half of the thirteenth century and into the fourteenth century, merchants sometimes would use the fairs to obtain loans in specie from the bankers and then travel to Flanders to purchase cloths in cash from producers. Those cloths would, then, be transported to the next fair or, particularly later on, to Paris, from where they would be transported to Italy.<sup>[85](#footnote85)</sup> The debts corresponding to these specie loans could have been managed through the books of the banker or via formal paper instruments. Merchants at the fairs, not just the bankers, would have probably also extended specie loans either through book debts or, more likely, paper instruments.   

But surely not all loans would have been granted in specie. Merchants and bankers could have also created a corresponding credit in their books for the borrower, instead of handing over specie. The utility of such credits to a borrower would have depended on the financial and commercial network of the merchant or banker, and the purposes of the loan. In any case, we know that in later fairs, loans were increasingly extended in the form of book credit, rather than specie. The same is true for deposits. Looking at the books of the Salviati firm at the Lyon Fairs between 1544 and 1549, for example, Matringe finds that deposits and loans by the bankers only involved specie 7.3 percent and 7 percent of the time, respectively.<sup>[86](#footnote86)</sup>


## 5. The Commercial Revolution

At the end of the Dark Ages, much of the practical activity of the merchants consisted of travelling with their goods. Especially over longer distances, travel typically occurred within larger groups, often armed, due to the hazards and risks involved. Merchants may have had one or two assistants, and formed partnerships with other merchants typically for the length of a single expedition. We can obtain a view of the nature of trading from Kohn’s description of that activity in early twelfth-century Genoa:<sup>[87](#footnote87)</sup>

>> Genoa traded by sea with the eastern Mediterranean and by land with the Low Countries and the Fairs of Champagne. In both cases, the trade was conducted by traveling merchants. The Mediterranean trade was in the hands of the Genoese themselves. Ships for Syria, for example, left Genoa in the fall and returned in the Spring: one recorded fleet of three ships carried some one hundred merchants. The overland trade was largely in the hand of merchants from Arras, who traveled south, and of merchants from Asti, who traveled north. Both groups bought cloth in the North and carried it south in caravans to Genoa. There they sold it to Genoese merchants who specialized in the export of cloth to various Mediterranean destinations. The caravan merchants then purchased Mediterranean goods, mainly spices and silks, and returned with them to the North.

This is not to say that, at the end of the Early Middle Ages, every merchant involved in a trade expedition actually travelled with the caravans. Retired merchants probably formed partnerships with younger merchants merely to contribute capital, connections, and expertise. Similarly, even working merchants probably sometimes acted only as investors through such partnerships. We certainly see such relationships already by the late eleventh century,<sup>[88](#footnote88)</sup> and they more than likely existed earlier. The main point is that, towards the end of the Early Middle Ages, goods were transported largely by merchants in caravans, and any active merchant would have spent a substantial amount of their time traveling in such caravans.   

Certainly by the end of the thirteenth century, however, our view of commercial activities would be far too simple with only the image of the traditional caravan merchant in our toolbox. Richard Face offers an illuminating discussion in this regard.<sup>[89](#footnote89)</sup> According to him, certainly by the end of the thirteenth century and probably much earlier, the traditional notion of the caravan merchant is inadequate to describe the organization of trade at the Fairs of Champagne, particularly with regards to the northern Italian merchants. 

Specifically, by the end of the thirteenth century, the Italian merchants frequently employed resident partners (often family), employees, and agents to manage trade activities at the fairs on their behalf. The ability to conduct business over distance through partners and agents was supported by improved communication networks. By the second half of the thirteenth century, various courier services—operated in-house, by guilds, or towns—were running between the Italian business centers and the fairs. These transported market news, credit instruments, and other information in about half the time required for a caravan of goods to travel the distance. The transportation of goods was also increasingly facilitated by specialized service providers, rather than personally managed by the merchants. That transport occurred predominantly overland by crossing the Alps. Finally, trade through credit at the fairs, as we have already seen, was pervasive. Face notes the following on the record of *cambium* contracts due at the Fairs of Champagne:<sup>[90](#footnote90)</sup>

>> In the contracts which record loans in the form of exchanges to be repaid at the fairs of Champagne, there appears a freak once in a while which specifies that repayment should be made in gold or silver coin and not in credits at a banker’s table. But the vast majority of these documents took for granted payment by paper.

All these factors ensured much less intense travel to the Fairs of Champagne by the merchants themselves than we would expect given just the picture of the traditional caravan merchant. 

The developments at the Fairs of Champagne as illustrated by Face were transforming trade throughout Europe. By the end of the thirteenth century, trade in Europe was increasingly making way for the sedentary merchant. Rather than travelling from place to place with their own wares, these merchants operated from their headquarters in a particular city or town and used partners (often family members), employees, and agents located elsewhere to manage interregional and long-distance trade.<sup>[91](#footnote91)</sup>

The first sedentary merchants were mostly Italians. Piacentene and Sienese trading companies already began to maintain representatives in Genoa, Marseilles, Bruges, Paris, and elsewhere from the early thirteenth century onwards.<sup>[92](#footnote92)</sup> Supposedly, the first Italian representatives were operating in London by 1220.<sup>[93](#footnote93)</sup> And by the end of the thirteenth century, many of the major Italian companies had branch offices in Paris, so that representatives could easily travel to the nearby Fairs of Champagne.<sup>[94](#footnote94)</sup>

By contrast, at the end of the thirteenth century, the merchants from particular towns and cities in Flanders still commonly congregated in the traditional caravans to travel together to the fairs. These caravans were accompanied by armed men, a commander, and a standard-bearer, though the nature of the procession may have become more ceremonial than a necessity, as the roads had become much safer to travel by that time.<sup>[95](#footnote95)</sup>

The establishment of representation in Flanders by the Italian companies apparently came somewhat late, namely in the early fourteenth century.<sup>[96](#footnote96)</sup> Various factors probably contributed to the decline of the Fairs of Champagne. It seems, for example, that policies by the public authorities were the major factor both in the rise of the fairs and their initial decline around 1300.<sup>[97](#footnote97)</sup> One common argument, however, is that the establishment of permanent trade networks between northern Italy and Flanders also reduced their importance.<sup>[98](#footnote98)</sup>

According to Norman Gras, Europe transformed from a petty capitalist system to that of a commercial capitalist system (or mercantile capitalist system) during the High and Late Middle Ages. The key figure in this new capitalist system was the sedentary merchant. He describes the transition and the role of the sedentary merchant as follows:<sup>[99](#footnote99)</sup>

>> \[Petty capitalism\] was a system of small shopkeepers, storekeepers, hucksters, pedlars, and traveling merchants.... The new mercantile capitalism grew upon the backs of petty capitalists. It was a system of wider trade, larger amounts of capital, and unprecedented growth of control, of policy-formulation, and of management, all centering in one office—a counting house. The central figure was the sedentary merchant, a merchant too wise, too occupied, too economical to travel. His distant connections were maintained by agents, traveling or resident. This sedentary merchant grew up in the ancient period but apparently had a short life in that era. In the later period he flourished from about 1300 to 1800. The outstanding sedentary merchants were called merchant princes. Some mercantile families actually attained political power and princely status. Their tastes and culture were the object of public admiration and their friendship sought by princes and emperors far and wide. Holding the purse-strings, they were often the arbiters of considerable international contests. 

Gras argues further that it was really the sedentary merchant’s knack for business administration that supported the advancements and civilization of the Late Middle Ages and the early modern period.<sup>[101](#footnote101)</sup> The period of commercial capitalism only came to an end with the Industrial Revolution. 

The transformation from the traveling to the sedentary merchant was so profound that it has been dubbed a commercial revolution by Raymond De Roover: “a complete or drastic change in the methods of doing business or in the organization of business enterprise just as an industrial revolution means a complete change in the methods of production, for example, the introduction of power-driven machinery”.<sup>[102](#footnote102)</sup> According to De Roover, various factors enabled the rise of the sedentary merchant, including the formation of long-term partnerships, the invention of the bill of exchange, greater security for both land and sea travel, the introduction of maritime insurance, and the development of bookkeeping techniques, particularly double entry accounting.<sup>[103](#footnote103)</sup>

While the establishment of permanent trade networks between Flanders and northern Italy may have partially contributed to the decline of the Fairs of Champagne, it is important to note that fairs certainly did not disappear entirely from the landscape for trade and finance. Quite the contrary. While permanent trade and financial networks became increasingly important, we continue to see the medieval fairs thrive: a testament to the expansion of trade and finance in the period. 

After the decline of the Fairs of Champagne, large fairs that facilitated interzonal and interregional trade and that served as financial markets continued to be held throughout Europe. These include, notably, the Geneva fairs that were prominent in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Lyon fairs that were prominent in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the Frankfurt fairs that were prominent in the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. 

These large international fairs seem to have increasingly performed a financial function, the broad lines of which were already set out at the Fairs of Champagne. As noted by Denzel,<sup>[104](#footnote104)</sup> in the sixteenth century, the timing of international fairs was coordinated in such a way so that they provided a “permanent ‘pan-European’ money, credit, and exchange market in which merchant-bankers could conduct their business and financial transactions almost all year round”. Baumgartl, in turn, concludes that “\[a\]t the height of their importance from around 1580 to 1620, the Bisenzone fairs were probably the largest capital market of the time and also the clearing center for European payment transactions”.<sup>[105](#footnote105)</sup>

At the end of the fourteenth and into the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, there was also an explosion of regional fairs throughout the continent, despite the impact on the population from wars, famines, and epidemics in the Late Medieval Period. Epstein attributes this growth in regional fairs to increasing economic specialization and the provision of the right institutional support by the increasingly powerful late medieval states.<sup>[106](#footnote106)</sup> Around the year 1500, an estimated 5,000 periodic markets and fairs were taking place in roughly 1,500 towns and cities in the Holy Roman Empire.<sup>[107](#footnote107)</sup>
