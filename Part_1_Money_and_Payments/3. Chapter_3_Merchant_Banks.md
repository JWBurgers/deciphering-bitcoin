# Merchant Banks

From the High Middle Ages until into the early modern period, the story of money, payments, and banking largely revolves around two groups of financial institutions: merchant banks and deposit banks. 

The merchant banks were, at least by the standards of the time, large international companies. They were owned and operated predominantly by powerful families from northern Italian cities such as Genoa, Milan, Venice, and Florence. They processed long-distance payments and extended commercial and sovereign credit. Deposit banks were, instead, usually small, locally owned operations, often proprietorships. They had a key role in facilitating local payments and credit. Unlike the merchant banks, deposit banks were generally heavily regulated. While merchant banks indeed sometimes also owned deposit banks or ran such operations, deposit banking was not really their core business. 

In this chapter, we turn first to the merchant banks. We begin in *Section 1* by describing the economic expansion during the High and Late Middle Ages. In *Sections 2* and *3*, we present two key developments from that period: the emergence of exchange contracts and the rapid growth of the medieval fair. In *Section 4*, we describe the commercial revolution of the thirteenth century: the shift from the caravan merchant to the sedentary merchant. With this background in place, our discussion then turns to the organization and activities of merchant companies and merchant banks in *Sections 5* and *6*. In *Section 7*, we briefly offer some reflections on the Hanseatic League. 


## 1. Economic expansion

The decline and eventual fall of the Roman Empire—usually dated to 476 AD—commenced a long period of decline in Europe. This period, the Early Middle Ages (ca. 476 to 1000), is, therefore, often appropriately called the Dark Ages. Roman political, commercial, and social life had centered around its towns. But during the Dark Ages, their populations dwindled, accompanied by an overall decline in trade as well as cultural and civic life. Many towns, such as, for instance, Trier and Tongeren, were practically abandoned for a long period in the Early Middle Ages.<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> The overall decline seems to have been most significant in the north of Europe.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup> Nevertheless, Dean and Waley still offer the following reflection on that process in Italy:<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup>

> Between the fifth and tenth centuries AD \[municipal\] institutions were shattered and the very nature of political and social authority was reshaped by a series of events which included the conquests and settlements of Goths and Lombards. The gradual but profound transformation of these centuries involved a great contraction in city life and an almost complete break in urban government. A few of the larger cities, among them Rome itself, and Milan, survived as important centers of population, but most of the ancient municipalities must have declined into sordid if picturesque decay, villages in a setting of classical ruins.

By the ninth century, towns had little importance and much of the economy in Europe was manorial and self-sufficient. Any trade—understood here broadly as all the voluntary economic exchange of goods—was generally at a local level. Long-distance trade was a minor economic activity.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup> According to De Roover,<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup> “\[w\]hat little \[long-distance trade\] survived was carried on by groups of travelling merchants who catered for the rich by selling them luxuries or who exploited the poor by charging high prices for necessities in times of famine or distress”. Given the hazardous conditions, a merchant “had to possess a great deal of physical endurance and be ready to face many hardships”.<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> In addition, “moral stamina and some intellectual baggage were indispensable prerequisites to success”.<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> 

Towards the end of the Dark Ages, however, Europe began to enter into a long period of economic ascendancy that saw an expansion of trade, advances in financial and business practices, population growth, increasing urbanization, and the introduction of new technologies. With some interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of the Middle Ages and into the early modern period. The key era was the twelfth to early fourteenth century, after which economic expansion was interrupted by wars, famines, and epidemics, notably the Black Death (1347–1351). While traditionally most of the Late Middle Ages was seen as one of abject depression by historians, it now appears the interruptions to economic progress were not quite as severe and long-lasting as originally envisioned.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup>

Data on population growth and urbanization can give some sense of the economic progress in the High and Late Middle Ages. A modern degree of accuracy for such data is beyond our reach. But, indicatively, it has been estimated that, from 1000 to 1300, the population of Europe roughly doubled and the percentage of inhabitants in towns over 5,000 more than doubled (to over 10%). Supposedly the rate of urbanization approached 30% in northern Italy and the Low Countries by 1300.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup> Also, indicatively, Van Werveke identifies a number of cities in Italy that by the middle of the fourteenth century had around 50,000 inhabitants or more: Milan, Venice, Florence, Bologna, Genoa, Rome, Naples, and Palermo. Elsewhere in Europe at that time, he estimates populations of around 50,000 for Ghent; 35,000 for Bruges; 35,000 to 45,000 for London; 40,000 for Cologne; and 80,000 for Paris.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup>

What was the cause of the economic expansion throughout much of the High and Late Middle Ages and into the early modern period? What, in other words, brought Europe out of the Dark Ages?

Naturally, a variety of explanations exist for such a complex transformation and we cannot do justice to all of them here. That said, many historians broadly place the expansion of trade and the activities of the medieval merchants at the forefront of explanations on the economic expansion from High Middle Ages into the early modern period.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup>

Throughout the period, agricultural production, artisanry, and basic manufacturing was typically centered around household enterprises that were based on the nuclear or extended family. Other than at a local level, these producers would rarely engage in trade directly. Instead, particularly trade at the regional level and beyond was the domain of professional merchants. They bought goods wholesale and sold them to other merchants and to end-consumers. They generally traded a great diversity of goods, and took advantage of whatever opportunities were present. Throughout the period, merchants were not really involved in production and mostly limited themselves to trade.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup> That said, they increasingly seem to have been involved in production during the Late Middle Ages.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup>

Trade until the early modern era was primarily in foodstuffs, textiles, and textile raw materials. But it also extended to timber, tar, metals, peat, coal, jewelry, arms, and many other products.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup> Output at that time lacked standardization and quality guarantees, so goods were rarely shipped to order on the basis of descriptions or samples;<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup> instead, merchants transported goods from place to place, so that inspections and purchases could be made on the spot. This venturing nature to trade was situated in an environment of slow transport times and risks from pirates, bandits, government authorities, and not always reliable transport services. Merchants were largely operating as venturers well into the early modern period.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup> This venturing nature to trade explains why many early English regulated and joint-stock companies have “adventurers” or “merchant adventurers” in their names.

Kohn offers a detailed explanation for how the expansion of trade transformed Europe during the High and Late Middle Ages.<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup> He argues that the key factor behind the expansion of trade was the decreased costs to trading, including in transaction costs, transportation costs, and financing costs.<sup>[18](#footnote18)</sup> And the resultant growth in trade was accompanied by population growth, urbanization, advances in finance and business practices, and technological progress. There were certainly substantial feedback loops at play. Expansion in trade, notably, generated investments in institutional and physical infrastructure, increased specialization in production, and further divisions of labor. These developments fostered technological progress. All of these factors, in turn, would have further expanded trade. 

From the start of the High Middle Ages into the early modern era, Europe generally consisted of two main economic zones: northern Italy and northwestern Europe. Different regions within these zones—understood broadly as the areas concentrated around towns of a few thousand inhabitants and upwards—traded with each other. The two zones also traded with each other as well as with Asia and, starting around the beginning of the early modern period, with the Americas. While long-distance trade between the zones has often been the subject of historical discussion, the bulk of trade in the Late and High Middle Ages was actually local and regional. Long-distance trade and to a lesser extent interregional trade was quantitatively much less important.<sup>[19](#footnote19)</sup>

Long-distance trade, however, was more crucial than its moderate quantitative scale might suggest, in particular because it led to the introduction of new types of goods, technologies, and ideas. Trade with Asia introduced Europe, for example, to new industries such as paper-making, gunpowder, cotton and silk textiles, glass, soap, ceramics and porcelain, sugar, and rice. Long-distance trade also introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to the son of a Pisan customs official in Algeria, Leonardo da Pisa (ca. 1170–1250), better known as Fibonacci. His books, most famously his *Liber Abaci* (1202), were instrumental in spreading awareness throughout Europe about the Hindu-Arabic counting system, though it took several hundred years for the system to be really embraced.<sup>[20](#footnote20)</sup> Interregional trade, in turn, seems to have had the most extensive impact on the specialization of production, the division of labor, and technological progress in manufacturing and agriculture.<sup>[21](#footnote21)</sup>


## 2. Exchange contracts  

With regards to long-distance payments, commercial credit, and financial arbitrage and speculation, the single most important financial innovation that emerged among Italian merchants during the High Middle Ages was that of the exchange contract. It was a very popular financial instrument from around 1200 well into the early modern period. We briefly discuss the history and mechanics of exchange contracts here, and then turn to their functions more elaborately in later sections. 

According to Baumgartl,<sup>[22](#footnote22)</sup> merchants at the Fairs of Champagne—key international trade and financial events that were particularly important from the end of the twelfth century until about 1300—could already make purchases cashless and on credit through two types of financial instruments at the end of the twelfth century: the *lettre de foire* and the *instrumentum ex causa cambii* or *cambium* contract. Both documents contained essentially promises to pay a debt at a later time. The *lettre de foire* was a certificate only issued by the court of the fairs, and confirmed the promise of a buyer to pay a seller at a later fair. The *cambium* contract was, instead, written up by a merchant and officially notarized. It contained a promise to pay at a later date and place, but was not bound to the fairs in this regard. Often repayment was in a different currency. 

Others maintain roughly similar dates as Baumgartl for the emergence of the *cambium* contract. According the Kohn,<sup>[23](#footnote23)</sup> for example, the earliest record of one dates to 1220, though it probably indeed already existed in the twelfth century. And according to De Roover, the *cambium* contract was certainly popular in the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. Many examples exist from Genoese notarial records of the time, where money would be borrowed in Genoa through a *cambium* contract and repaid at one of the fairs in Champagne.<sup>[24](#footnote24)</sup>

The bill of exchange, initially called the *lettera di pagamento* and later the *lettera di cambio*, was the successor to the *cambium* contract. We cannot say exactly when it emerged. Our oldest surviving examples come from the early fourteenth century.<sup>[25](#footnote25)</sup> Indirectly, however, the records certainly indicate that it was already used in thirteenth century.<sup>[26](#footnote26)</sup> At Avignon, for instance, statutes referencing bills of exchange were already passed in 1253.<sup>[27](#footnote27)</sup> The Venetians, in turn, already had statutes pertaining to bills of exchange by 1272.<sup>[28](#footnote28)</sup> Keiley interestingly argues that the bill of exchange originated with the expulsion of the Jews from France in 1181 by Phillip II. As it was forbidden to take or send money out of the kingdom, Jews sold their personal property to trusted parties in France. They, then, settled in Lombardy and sold early versions of bills of exchange to Italian merchants who, then, had a claim on the proceeds kept in France.<sup>[29](#footnote29)</sup>

So what exactly is the bill of exchange?

The bill of exchange, unlike the *cambium* contract, is not a promise to pay, but a payment order. Also unlike the earlier *cambium* contract, the bill of exchange was not officially notarized. Instead, bills of exchange were written up by the merchants themselves. Consequently, they fell under the jurisdiction of merchant courts—specialized courts that enforced merchant customs<sup>[30](#footnote30)</sup>—rather than civil courts. 

The lack of notarization represented a substantial break with standard Italian business practices. During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, notaries were utilized for practically every kind of business contract in Italy, unless the transaction proceeded through a cash transfer. This included contracts for partnership agreements, capital investments from non-partners, loans, credit sales, banking operations, leases, labor and apprenticeship contracts, and so on.<sup>[31](#footnote31)</sup> The practice of writing up contracts among merchants naturally would have been supported with the increasing literacy and general education of merchants during the High Middle Ages, including through the *abbaco* schools, trade schools which first emerged in thirteenth century Italy.<sup>[32](#footnote32)</sup> It would have also been incentivized by the growing volume of trade and the time investment and hassle required for notarization. 

The recognition of handwriting on bills of exchange was extremely important. In the fifteenth century, for example, the Medici Company in Florence maintained lists of people authorized to write out bills of exchange for their foreign branches as well as for any companies with which they regularly executed exchange transactions. They also kept verified handwriting samples for these people. If handwriting on a bill of exchange was not recognized, it could lead to the rejection of the bill.<sup>[33](#footnote33)</sup>

Lets now turn to the mechanics of the bill of exchange. The standard version of the instrument involves four logically distinct parties in two locations. They are standardly known as the deliverer, the taker (or drawer), the payer (or drawee), and the payee. Essentially, in location A, the taker receives a sum of money from the deliverer; then, at a later agreed upon time, the payer gives a sum of money to the payee in location B. Sometimes the payer and payee agreed on the collection of the money at an earlier time against a small discount. Typically the two legs of the transaction involved two different currencies, usually those local to locations A and B. 

The deliverer and the taker in a bill of exchange were in some type of relationship with, respectively, the payee and payer in location B. If the deliverer were a merchant bank, for instance, the payee might have been a representative of the bank’s branch in location B or a representative from a different merchant bank acting as an agent. In such a case, the taker in location A was often a merchant, while the payer in location B was often a sales agent and banker for that merchant. The nature of the relationships involved differed. Most generally, we can describe the payee as a correspondent of the deliverer, and the payer as a correspondent of the taker. The term correspondent here obviously originates from the fact that these long-distance relationships were managed by correspondence.  

In some cases a bill of exchange might be due upon sight. Commonly repayment was also set to the date of an international fair.<sup>[34](#footnote34)</sup> In many cases, however, repayment was just set at a number of days from the issue of the bill, often at what was known as usance.<sup>[35](#footnote35)</sup>

Much of the traffic in bills of exchange occurred between large towns and cities that functioned as financial centers. In the Late Middle Ages, for instance, these included, to varying degrees, Bologna, Florence, Genoa, Milan, Naples, Palermo, Pisa, Rome, and Venice in Italy. Outside of Italy, they included, to varying degrees, Barcelona, Palma de Majorca, Valencia, Avignon, Montpellier, Bruges, Paris, and London.<sup>[36](#footnote36)</sup>

A bill of exchange between any two such towns and cities had a standardized period after which they were due from the moment of issuance. That period was known as usance. The usance between Florence and London, for instance, was 90 days in the Late Middle Ages, while it was 60 days between Florence and Bruges.<sup>[37](#footnote37)</sup> From London to Antwerp, Amsterdam, Middleburg, or Hamburg in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was 30 days.<sup>[38](#footnote38)</sup> Bills between towns and cities that functioned as financial centers were commonly due at usance, though recent evidence suggests that it may have been less frequently used than originally thought by historians.<sup>[39](#footnote39)</sup>

The key aspect about the bill of exchange, as well as the earlier *cambium* contract, is that money “transfers” from the deliverer in location A to the payee in location B *without requiring any shipment of specie, bullion, or other types of valuables*. By specie here, we mean gold and silver coins issued by a government or some private institution. By bullion, we mean gold and silver in the shape of bars and ingots. Other types of valuables might have been commodities such as grain or salt. So, no value, at least not in the physical sense, moved between locations A and B with a bill of exchange. 

To really understand the mechanics of bills of exchange, it is best to explore an actual example. Consider the following bill of exchange discussed by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli. It is from 1447 and translated from Italian.<sup>[40](#footnote40)</sup>

> \[In the name of\] Jesus on the 7th of March 1447, in Venice. Pay by this first letter at usance to ser Allessandro Borromeo, son of messer Antonio, 400 ducats at the rate of exchange of 43.5 sterlings per ducat and this sum was received \[in Venice by Michele Zon\] from ser Giovanni Borromei & Partners. \[Addressed to\] Jacopo Salviati & Partners in London.

The religious opening here was extra encouragement for the taker and their correspondent to honor the bill. While the writing is brief and somewhat archaic, the details are fairly easy to comprehend. 

To start, the bill of exchange was written in Venice on March 7, 1447 by Michele Zon. He acknowledges to have received a payment from Giovanni Borromei & Partners of 400 Venetian ducats. In the fifteenth century, the Borromei family had three family lines that operated banking operations: a Milanese, a Florentine, and a Venetian line.<sup>[41](#footnote41)</sup> Giovanni Borromei & Partners is the name of the bank owned and operated by the Venetian line of the family. It also had branches in Bruges and London. So, here, Michele Zon is the taker (or drawer) and the Venetian Borromei bank the deliverer. 

The bill, then, instructs the Salviati company in London—who here, thus, have the role of the payer and are a correspondent of Michele Zon—to pay a sum of 400 Venetian ducats at the rate of 43.5 sterlings per ducat. At the time, London employed a system of account where a pound was equivalent to twenty shillings, and each shilling was equivalent to twelve pence. These units were denoted, respectively, by £, s, and d. The sterling unit in the bill refers to English pence. So the bill actually instructs the Salviati company to pay 17,400 pence, or £72 10s English pence. While we typically associate pounds with English currency, this system of pounds, shillings, and pence was actually commonly employed for currency amounts throughout southern and western Europe during the Middle Ages and the early modern period. 

The payee for the sum of money, £72 10s English pence, is a correspondent of the Venetian Borromei bank in London, Allessandro Borromeo. The latter was actually running the London branch of the bank. The payment was to be made at usance, which at the time was 90 days between Venice and London. So the payment was to be completed on June 5, 1447. 

The Salviati company and Allessandro Borromei would have been informed about the bill of exchange through correspondence. First, it would have likely been drawn up in multiple copies by Michele Zon. Then, in order to guarantee receipt, the Venetian Borromei bank probably sent two or more copies by different channels to Allessandro Borromeo. Michele Zon, in turn, may have sent several copies of a letter of advice (or aviso) to the Salviati company, instructing them to honor the bill of exchange. Alternatively, he may have included the matter of the bill in a more general business letter. 

Once Allessandro Borromeo received a copy of the bill of exchange from Venice, he would have presented it to the Salviati company in London. The latter would have “accepted” the bill under normal circumstances by writing *acetta* across it. On June 5, Allessandro Borromeo would, then, have returned with the accepted bill to collect his money from the Salviati company.  

Importantly, unlike the description of this situation might suggest, it is unlikely that any specie, bullion, or other form of physical value ever changed hands in either Venice or London. Instead, in Venice, Michele Zon likely just received a credit of 400 ducats with the Venetian Borromei bank. He probably also held an account with the Salviate company that would have been debited by £72 10s to facilitate the payment. In any case, both legs of the payment likely proceeded through credit transfers. 

While physical money or value did not move between locations A and B, the essence of a bill of exchange, it was also uncommon for any physical money or value to be involved at locations and B. Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli, for instance, studied around 23,000 transactions from the bank branches in Bruges and London owned by the Milanese, and not the Venetian, line of the Borromei family. They conclude that “cash payments amounted to a very limited share of all transactions, even at a local level, with credits and debits transferred from one account holder to the other or to and from the bank”.<sup>[42](#footnote42)</sup> For another example, Kohn mentions that between 1456 and 1459 a bank in Genoa received 160,000 lire in payments from bills of exchange, of which 92.5% was settled in bank credit. Only the remaining 7.5% was in the form of cash settlement.<sup>[43](#footnote43)</sup>

The example above has shown the mechanics of the bill of exchange. While the form of the earlier *cambium* contract was different, the mechanics were very similar. One party would give a sum of money to another party at location A. The contract, then, specified that the recipient or their correspondent would pay a sum of money to the party that gave the sum or their correspondent at a later date in location B. A *cambium* contract typically also involved two different currencies. 

Due to the similarities, the “bill of exchange” in the literature sometimes refers to both the *lettera di pagamento* and the *lettera di cambio*, as well as the earlier *cambium* contract. For clarity, it is convenient to generally call them and similar instruments exchange contracts.  

A key aspect to exchange contracts is that they contained an inherent credit aspect. A sum of money was given in one place to be paid back at a later date in another place. However, interest was never explicitly charged within such contracts. Instead, interest rates were incorporated indirectly into the currency exchange rates. 

The Middle Ages and the early modern period were marked by highly restrictive usury laws—that is, laws which impose restrictions on the charging of interest rates for loans. While usury laws still exist today, they are generally much less strict. Sometimes usury laws banned charging interest outright, as in England until 1545.<sup>[44](#footnote44)</sup> More commonly, however, they imposed a maximum interest rate. Usury laws received much support from biblical and theological arguments and the church was often a key driving force behind them, though arguments from economists and other scholars also privded an intellectual foundation for them. Perhaps the key intellectual argument against usury laws that precipitated their lossening in recent history is Jeremy Bentham’s *Defense of Usury*, originally published in 1787.<sup>[45](#footnote45)</sup>

Throughout the Middle Ages and into the early modern period, exchange rates between different currencies were usually floating: that is, they fluctuated on the basis of market activities. In most cases where exchange contracts were used, these floating exchange rates presented a risk to the incorporated interest rates. As a consequence, exchange contracts typically fell outside of the scope of usury laws.<sup>[46](#footnote46)</sup> De Roover has often emphasized the evasion of usury restrictions for the popularity of exchange contracts. Others have also emphasized other factors, noting, for example, that the bill of exchange remained popular even after usury restrictions decreased.<sup>[47](#footnote47)</sup>


## 3. The Fairs of Champagne

The expansion of trade during the High and Late Middle Ages was accompanied by many institutional and technical advancements. One key institutional advancement was the increasing importance of the medieval fair. 

By a medieval fair, historians typically mean a certain type of periodic gathering for the purposes of trade and finance. Every fair was integrated into a larger network of such events for supporting interregional and interzonal trade. Fairs were mostly attended by professional merchants and marked by wholesale trade. They were also important financial markets, and sometimes they were really more financial than commercial markets. The Fairs of Besançon-Piacenza,<sup>[48](#footnote48)</sup> most prominent towards the end of the Middle Ages and at the beginning of the early modern period, were set up by Genoese merchant bankers purely for the purposes of serving as financial markets.

By contrast, when historians of the period speak of “markets”, they more commonly refer to the periodic gatherings that focused on serving local and regional trade. While not devoid of wholesale trade, markets were generally more oriented towards consumers.<sup>[49](#footnote49)</sup>

Fairs and markets usually took place outside or in a courtyard.<sup>[50](#footnote50)</sup> They operated on the basis of privileges granted by ruling authorities who specified their rights and regulations, including specifications on dates, times, and location. Ruling authorities benefitted from fairs and markets through taxes, fees, and, certainly with regards to fairs, the broader financial opportunities these brought to their realm.<sup>[51](#footnote51)</sup> Fairs typically took place a few times per year, while markets could be weekly, monthly, or even annual.

The famous medieval fair that took place at St. Denis, near Paris, had a history that can be traced back to the seventh century. But into the ninth century, there is scarcely any further information about similar events (though markets were certainly common). A sharp growth in the size and the number of fairs is, then, seen in the eleventh and twelfth centuries.<sup>[52](#footnote52)</sup> The most important fairs to emerge out of this period, already mentioned in the previous section, are certainly the Fairs of Champagne. 

In their heyday, the Fairs of Champagne consisted of six gatherings per year in four different towns, including Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and Bar-sur-Aube. There is some variation in the literature with regards to the supposed duration of these fairs. But indicatively, Sangster concludes each of them seems to have lasted between 46 and 52 days.<sup>[53](#footnote53)</sup>

Merchants from France, Flanders, Brabant, Spain, Germany, England, and elsewhere came to buy and sell their goods, including cloths and wool, silk, leather, furs, linens, spices, sugar, wax, grain, wines, and more. The key participants in these fairs were above all else the Italian merchants who used them foremost to purchase northern cloths in exchange for spices, silk, and other luxury items. Those northern cloths were subsequently distributed throughout the Mediterranean world.<sup>[54](#footnote54)</sup>

The four towns involved in the Fairs of Champagne already had trade events by the early twelfth century.<sup>[55](#footnote55)</sup> But, at that time, these events were just local or regional markets. During the twelfth century, however, they transformed into hubs for international trade and finance. While the fairs had already grown substantially in size by the middle of the century, a key development would have been the arrival of the Italian merchants in the last quarter of the century.<sup>[56](#footnote56)</sup> From the late twelfth century to around 1300, the Fairs of Champagne really served as the center of international trade and finance, after which they went into a quick decline in importance.<sup>[57](#footnote57)</sup>

The Fairs of Champagne were, at least by Medieval standards, colossal and intricately organized events. As illustratively described by Verlinden,<sup>[58](#footnote58)</sup>

> The lords of the fairs were at first the counts of Champagne, and later the kings of France. They derived considerable revenues from the fairs: taxes on the residences and stalls of the merchants, entry and exit tolls, levies on sales and purchases, dues upon weights and measures, justice and safe-conduct charges upon the Italians and Jews. The chief officers attached to the fairs were the wardens of the fair who were responsible for order and justice and controlled the fair seal, at least until 1318 when a chancellor or keeper of the fair seal was appointed. Under them were the wardens’ lieutenants or clerks, together with sergeants-at-arms to maintain order. It should not be forgotten that, conjointly with the special jurisdiction set up for the fairs, the town magistrature and the local ecclesiastical dignitaries also exercised a certain authority over the merchants and their transactions. The court of the fair wardens was, however, a tribunal of capital importance as can be seen especially by the number of sergeants it employed. In 1317 there were no less than 140, although the Châtelet of Paris, about this time, had no more than 150. There were sixty notaries at the Châtelet and forty at the fairs. 

There is widespread conviction among historians that the Fairs of Champagne, in their heydays, operated with fairly sophisticated payment systems and also served as international financial markets.<sup>[59](#footnote59)</sup> With regards to both these aspects of the fairs, the *cambium* contracts and the lettres de foire, as mentioned in the previous section, would have been important financial instruments. Later in the thirteenth century and into the early fourteenth century, bills of exchange would have also played some role.  

Let’s first turn to the payment systems that operated at the fairs.  

The general impression is that relatively little specie actually changed hands during commercial transactions at the fairs. Many purchases of goods, instead, were made through credit in some form or another.<sup>[60](#footnote60)</sup> It is also quite clear that each fair functioned as a clearing house after all the commercial transactions had been completed, that is, as an institution which nets transactions to make a more simplified set of transactions. After determining a more simplified set of transactions, the remaining obligations would be settled.<sup>[61](#footnote61)</sup> This clearing and settlement system was regulated by the fair authorities.<sup>[62](#footnote62)</sup> While the operational details of the system are largely absent from the literature, we can broadly piece together how it might have operated.

To start, surely not every credit-based commercial transaction at a fair would have been processed by the clearing and settlement system. In some cases, sellers probably just agreed to extend credit to buyers beyond the period of the fair, perhaps to be settled at a next fair or at some other date and location. These transactions would have mostly proceeded through paper financial instruments (i.e., the *cambium* contract, the *lettre de foire*, or the bill of exchange). In some cases, merchants might have also directly extended credit beyond the period of a fair merely through book entries. In these credit-based commercial transactions, the merchants would have generally used witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical authorities.<sup>[63](#footnote63)</sup>

So, while some credit-based commercial transactions during a particular fair would have fallen outside of the scope of its clearing and settlement system, the majority of such transactions probably fell within its scope. It seems that merchants, in fact, kept track of the liabilities and rights incurred by the latter transactions, at least at some point, through documents called *carte*.<sup>[64](#footnote64)</sup> The credit-based commercial transactions at a particular fair processed by its clearance and settlement system would have been of two main types: those where the merchants extended credit directly, and those were an intermediary deposit banker was involved. 

When merchants arranged commercial exchanges on credit directly, in many cases they probably just made book entries for the transaction to be settled at the end of the fair.<sup>[65](#footnote65)</sup> They would have, then, created a *carte* as evidence for the debt under the presence of witnesses. In some cases perhaps only drawing up a *carte* was sufficient without making corresponding book entries. It is unlikely that the merchants used paper financial instruments for direct credit transactions that fell under the scope of the clearing and settlement system.  

Many credit-based commercial transactions would have instead utilized an intermediary deposit banker.<sup>[66](#footnote66)</sup> The Fairs at Champagne were marked by the presence of specialized proprietors, mostly Italian, that focused on (physical) currency exchange and deposit banking. Rather than the merchants arranging a credit relationship directly, the deposit banker would assume responsibility for the obligation to the creditor, while the buyer would become a debtor to the deposit bank for the value involved. Evidence of the transaction was probably also recorded with a *carte*.  

Trade at the Fairs of Champagne proceeded in structured segments. Notably, the northern merchants, mostly from Flanders and France, first sold their wool and cloths to the Italian merchants, before the latter sold their spices, silks, and other luxury goods to them. As the Italian merchants were the first to make purchases, their initial credit transactions via deposit bankers would have only been as debtors. Contrary to what one might expect, it does not appear that the Italian merchants deposited any specie at the beginning of a fair with the bankers to reduce the risks for the latter. The deposit bankers, in other words, were extending the Italian merchants significant credit in the early stages. Later in each fair, any Italian merchant could reduce their debt burden, and potentially build themselves up to a credit position, through the sale of their own goods. 

The appearance of deposit bankers at these fairs should not surprise us that much. The merchants also made credit arrangements with each other directly. But the bankers, as argued by Sangster,<sup>[67](#footnote67)</sup> generally would have enjoyed more trust within an environment with strangers. They would have reduced the financial risks for the merchants. Credit transactions were probably also conducted more efficiently via deposit bankers. Merchants directly engaging in a credit relationship would have probably placed a heavier burden on involving witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical authorities. 

At the end of each fair, all these commercial transactions that involved credit merely for the duration of the fair were, then, cleared and settled. While we do not have any detailed description of the procedures involved, it is not difficult to imagine how this clearing and settlement period might have roughly progressed. 

When deposit bankers were involved in a credit-based commercial transaction, they would have just recorded it in their accounting journal. In modern accounting, we keep running overviews of accounts using the accounting ledger. But such overviews were not yet used in the late thirteenth century.<sup>[68](#footnote68)</sup> So the deposit bankers probably just tallied all the transactions for their clients only at the end of the fair. With final positions for all clients in hand, the bankers would have compared each other’s books. As the bankers surely also had accounts with each other, they could have further simplified the obligations at the end of the fair. The bankers and the merchants probably would have also included credit-based transactions made directly between the merchants as part of the overall simplification process. 

Much time was probably spent not just on simplifying resultant transactions, but on verifying and confirming the records held by merchants and deposit bankers. Any debts outstanding at the end of the clearing period between the merchants and bankers could, then, be settled by various methods. 

According to Kohn,<sup>[69](#footnote69)</sup> if a merchant had a debt to a banker, he might settle it in cash, but more likely would carry it over to the next fair as an overdraft or borrow deposits from another merchant with a credit balance. For merchants with a credit balance, they could have been paid in specie or allowed to loan their credit to another merchant in debt to the deposit banker. Surely, as suggested by both Face and Sangster,<sup>[70](#footnote70)</sup> many of these outstanding debts between merchants and bankers as well as between merchants themselves would have also been settled with paper financial instruments (i.e., *cambium* contracts, lettres de foire, and bills of exchange), typically due at the next fair. 

Let’s now turn to subject of the Fairs of Champagne as international financial markets. 

First, obligations from paper financial instruments were often settled at the fairs (and probably from mere book credit as well). In some cases, these obligations may have arisen just out of a need for a general loan on the part of the borrower. But particularly with merchants, many of these debts were incurred during the regular course of business. A Genoese merchant in the thirteenth century, for example, was much more likely to buy silk, spices, and other wares through being the taker on a *cambium* contract than with cash. The merchants would have agreed on a total price and just specified on the contract that the merchant accepted goods for the value of the price. Given the focal point of the Fairs of Champagne for trade, the merchants often agreed to settle such debts at one of the upcoming fairs, not in the least because that is typically when the wares just bought on credit would be sold. 

Probably, the clearing and settlement system at a fair did not just process the majority of the credit-based commercial transactions incurred specifically during the course of the fair. More than likely, other outstanding debts due at the fairs would have been processed as well. Given all these various debts and obligations, and the potential for conflicts in the records to arise, it is hardly surprising that the clearing and settlement period of each fair has been estimated to take several weeks.<sup>[71](#footnote71)</sup>

The Fairs of Champagne were also an attractive venue for obtaining loans. Sometimes these loans might have been extended to merchants that required capital, either for business at the fair or elsewhere. It is also clear, however, that loans were acquired at the fairs by outsiders. These would have normally been aristocrats, clergy, or other high-status individuals. The counts in Champagne frequently guaranteed such loans, as the merchants and bankers were hesitant to extend loans without such guarantees.<sup>[72](#footnote72)</sup> On the flip side, people that had excess funds came to the fairs to loan funds to the bankers and merchants who, in turn, would have loaned them out to others. Loans incurred in this manner, as has already been suggested above, were probably often settled during the clearing and settlement phase of some subsequent fair.

An interesting and educational question is how the loans above were granted exactly. Currency exchange was a substantial part of the business of the deposit bankers, and loans would certainly also have been extended by them through specie. Illustratively, Bautier notes that in the second half of the thirteenth century and into the fourteenth century, merchants sometimes would use the fairs to obtain loans in specie from the bankers and then travel to Flanders to purchase cloths in cash from producers. Those cloths would, then, be transported to the next fair or, particularly later on, to Paris, from where they would be transported to Italy.<sup>[73](#footnote73)</sup> The debts corresponding to these specie loans could have been managed through the books of the banker or via formal paper instruments. Merchants at the fairs, not just the bankers, would have probably also extended specie loans either through book debts or, more likely, paper instruments.   

But surely not all loans would have been granted in specie. Merchants and bankers could have also created a corresponding credit in their books for the borrower, instead of handing over specie. The utility of such credits to a borrower would have depended on the financial and commercial network of the merchant or banker, and the purposes of the loan. In any case, we know that in later fairs, loans were increasingly extended in the form of book credit, rather than specie. The same is true for deposits. Looking at the books of the Salviati firm at the Lyon Fairs between 1544 and 1549, for example, Matringe finds that deposits and loans by the bankers only involved specie 7.3 percent and 7 percent of the time, respectively.<sup>[74](#footnote74)</sup>
 

## 4. The Commercial Revolution

At the end of the Dark Ages, much of the practical activity of the merchants consisted of travelling with their goods. Especially over longer distances, travel typically occurred within larger groups, often armed, due to the hazards and risks involved. Merchants may have had one or two assistants, and formed partnerships with other merchants typically for the length of a single expedition. We can obtain a view of the nature of trading from Kohn’s description of that activity in early twelfth-century Genoa:<sup>[75](#footnote75)</sup>

> Genoa traded by sea with the eastern Mediterranean and by land with the Low Countries and the Fairs of Champagne. In both cases, the trade was conducted by traveling merchants. The Mediterranean trade was in the hands of the Genoese themselves. Ships for Syria, for example, left Genoa in the fall and returned in the Spring: one recorded fleet of three ships carried some one hundred merchants. The overland trade was largely in the hand of merchants from Arras, who traveled south, and of merchants from Asti, who traveled north. Both groups bought cloth in the North and carried it south in caravans to Genoa. There they sold it to Genoese merchants who specialized in the export of cloth to various Mediterranean destinations. The caravan merchants then purchased Mediterranean goods, mainly spices and silks, and returned with them to the North.

This is not to say that, at the end of the Dark Ages, every merchant involved in a trade expedition actually travelled with the caravans. Retired merchants probably formed partnerships with younger merchants merely to contribute capital, connections, and expertise. Similarly, even working merchants probably sometimes acted only as investors through such partnerships. We certainly see such relationships already by the late eleventh century,<sup>[76](#footnote76)</sup> and they more than likely existed earlier. The main point is that, towards the end of the Dark Ages, goods were transported largely by merchants in caravans, and any active merchant would have spent a substantial amount of their time traveling in such caravans.   

Certainly by the end of the thirteenth century, however, our view of commercial activities would be far too simple with only the image of the traditional caravan merchant in our toolbox. Richard Face offers an illuminating discussion in this regard.<sup>[77](#footnote77)</sup> According to him, certainly by the end of the thirteenth century and probably much earlier, the traditional notion of the caravan merchant is inadequate to describe the organization of trade at the Fairs of Champagne, particularly with regards to the northern Italian merchants. 

Specifically, by the end of the thirteenth century, the Italian merchants frequently employed resident partners (often family), employees, and agents to manage trade activities at the fairs on their behalf. The ability to conduct business over distance through partners and agents was supported by improved communication networks. By the second half of the thirteenth century, various courier services—operated in-house, by guilds, or towns—were running between the Italian business centers and the fairs. These transported market news, credit instruments, and other information in about half the time required for a caravan of goods to travel the distance. The transportation of goods was also increasingly facilitated by specialized service providers, rather than personally managed by the merchants. That transport occurred predominantly overland by crossing the Alps. Finally, trade through credit at the fairs, as we have already seen, was pervasive. Face notes the following on the record of *cambium* contracts due at the Fairs of Champagne:<sup>[78](#footnote78)</sup>

> In the contracts which record loans in the form of exchanges to be repaid at the fairs of Champagne, there appears a freak once in a while which specifies that repayment should be made in gold or silver coin and not in credits at a banker’s table. But the vast majority of these documents took for granted payment by paper.

All these factors ensured much less intense travel to the Fairs of Champagne by the merchants themselves than we would expect given just the picture of the traditional caravan merchant. 

The developments at the Fairs of Champagne as illustrated by Face were transforming trade throughout Europe. By the end of the thirteenth century, trade in Europe was increasingly making way for the sedentary merchant. Rather than travelling from place to place with their own wares, these merchants operated from their headquarters in a particular city or town and used partners (often family members), employees, and agents located elsewhere to manage interregional and long-distance trade.<sup>[79](#footnote79)</sup>

The first sedentary merchants were mostly Italians. Piacentene and Sienese trading companies already began to maintain representatives in Genoa, Marseilles, Bruges, Paris, and elsewhere from the early thirteenth century onwards.<sup>[80](#footnote80)</sup> Supposedly, the first Italian representatives were operating in London by 1220.<sup>[81](#footnote81)</sup> And by the end of the thirteenth century, many of the major Italian companies had branch offices in Paris, so that representatives could easily travel to the nearby Fairs of Champagne.<sup>[82](#footnote82)</sup>

By contrast, at the end of the thirteenth century, the merchants from particular towns and cities in Flanders still commonly congregated in the traditional caravans to travel together to the fairs. These caravans were accompanied by armed men, a commander, and a standard-bearer, though the nature of the procession may have become more ceremonial than a necessity, as the roads had become much safer to travel by that time.<sup>[83](#footnote83)</sup>

The establishment of representation in Flanders by the Italian companies apparently came somewhat late, namely in the early fourteenth century.<sup>[84](#footnote84)</sup> Various factors probably contributed to the decline of the Fairs of Champagne. It seems, for example, that policies by the public authorities were the major factor both in the rise of the fairs and their initial decline around 1300.<sup>[85](#footnote85)</sup>  One common argument, however, is that the establishment of permanent trade networks between northern Italy and Flanders also reduced their importance.<sup>[86](#footnote86)</sup>

According to Norman Gras, Europe transformed from a petty capitalist system to that of a commercial capitalist system (or mercantile capitalist system) during the High and Late Middle Ages. The key figure in this new capitalist system was the sedentary merchant. He describes the transition and the role of the sedentary merchant as follows:<sup>[87](#footnote87)</sup>

> \[Petty capitalism\] was a system of small shopkeepers, storekeepers, hucksters, pedlars, and traveling merchants.... The new mercantile capitalism grew upon the backs of petty capitalists. It was a system of wider trade, larger amounts of capital, and unprecedented growth of control, of policy-formulation, and of management, all centering in one office—a counting house. The central figure was the sedentary merchant, a merchant too wise, too occupied, too economical to travel. His distant connections were maintained by agents, traveling or resident. This sedentary merchant grew up in the ancient period but apparently had a short life in that era. In the later period he flourished from about 1300 to 1800. The outstanding sedentary merchants were called merchant princes. Some mercantile families actually attained political power and princely status. Their tastes and culture were the object of public admiration and their friendship sought by princes and emperors far and wide. Holding the purse-strings, they were often the arbiters of considerable international contests. 

Gras argues further that it was really the sedentary merchant’s knack for business administration that supported the advancements and civilization of the Late Middle Ages and the early modern period.<sup>[89](#footnote89)</sup> The period of commercial capitalism only came to an end with the Industrial Revolution. 

The transformation from the traveling to the sedentary merchant was so profound that it has been dubbed a commercial revolution by Raymond De Roover: “a complete or drastic change in the methods of doing business or in the organization of business enterprise just as an industrial revolution means a complete change in the methods of production, for example, the introduction of power-driven machinery”.<sup>[90](#footnote90)</sup> According to De Roover, various factors enabled the rise of the sedentary merchant, including the formation of long-term partnerships, the invention of the bill of exchange, greater security for both land and sea travel, the introduction of maritime insurance, and the development of bookkeeping techniques, particularly double entry accounting.<sup>[91](#footnote91)</sup>

While the establishment of permanent trade networks between Flanders and northern Italy may have partially contributed to the decline of the Fairs of Champagne, it is important to note that fairs certainly did not disappear entirely from the landscape for trade and finance. Quite the contrary. While permanent trade and financial networks became increasingly important, we continue to see the medieval fairs thrive: a testament to the expansion of trade and finance in the period. 

After the decline of the Fairs of Champagne, large fairs that facilitated interzonal and interregional trade and that served as financial markets continued to be held throughout Europe. These include, notably, the Geneva fairs that were prominent in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Lyon fairs that were prominent in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the Frankfurt fairs that were prominent in the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. 

These large international fairs seem to have increasingly performed a financial function, the broad lines of which were already set out at the Fairs of Champagne. As noted by Denzel,<sup>[92](#footnote92)</sup> in the sixteenth century, the timing of international fairs was coordinated in such a way so that they provided a “permanent ‘pan-European’ money, credit, and exchange market in which merchant-bankers could conduct their business and financial transactions almost all year round”. Baumgartl, in turn, concludes that “[a]t the height of their importance from around 1580 to 1620, the Bisenzone fairs were probably the largest capital market of the time and also the clearing center for European payment transactions”.<sup>[93](#footnote93)</sup>

At the end of the fourteenth and into the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, there was also an explosion of regional fairs throughout the continent, despite the impact on the population from wars, famines, and epidemics in the Late Medieval Period. Epstein attributes this growth in regional fairs to increasing economic specialization and the provision of the right institutional support by the increasingly powerful late medieval states.<sup>[94](#footnote94)</sup> Around the year 1500, an estimated 5,000 periodic markets and fairs were taking place in roughly 1,500 towns and cities in the Holy Roman Empire.<sup>[95](#footnote95)</sup>


## 5. Merchant companies

In the High Middle Ages, various contracts were utilized to reduce the transportation and commercial risks present in trade expeditions. Rare twelfth-century notarial records from Genoa clearly suggest that the two main contracts for reducing such risks at the time were the *commenda* and the maritime loan.<sup>[96](#footnote96)</sup>

The *commenda* was a type of partnership contract utilized for short commercial ventures, particularly trade expeditions. We have a notarial act for such a contract from as early as the eleventh century.<sup>[97](#footnote97)</sup> Various earlier contract forms from the Mediterranean region—going back all the way to the Roman *societas* of labor and capital—probably influenced the *commenda*’s development.<sup>[98](#footnote98)</sup>

The *commenda* came in two basic forms: the unilateral form and the bilateral form. In law and practice, they were generally treated as two forms of the same general contract, rather than as separate types of contracts.<sup>[99](#footnote99)</sup> Pryor describes the two standard versions of the *commenda* as follows:<sup>[100](#footnote100)</sup>

* Both forms of the *commenda* had a sedentary investor, known as the *commendator* or stans, and an active merchant, known as the tractator. 
* In a unilateral commenda, the tractor did not contribute capital to the venture. In a bilateral commenda, the *tractator* contributed one-third of the capital and the *commendator* two-thirds of it.
* The *tractator* put the capital to use in a trade expedition with varying degrees of support from the commendator.
* After the completion of an expedition or the expiration of a certain time period, the *tractator* and the *commendator* would divide the proceeds from the venture.
* With a unilateral commenda, after accounting for costs, the *tractator* would receive one-fourth of the profits, while the *commendator* would receive three-fourths of it. In case of losses, the *commendator* just received the proceeds from the venture. The latter, in other words, carried the entire capital loss. There was no separate compensation for the labor of the *tractator* in either case.
* In a bilateral commenda, after accounting for costs, the *tractator* and *commendator* would each receive half of the profits. In case of a loss, the remaining proceeds would be allocated according to the original two-thirds and one-third ratios of the respective capital investments. There was no separate compensation for the labor of the *tractator* in either case.

Above describes the two standard forms of the *commenda* in cities such as Genoa, Venice, Amalfi, Barcelona, and Marseilles. Obviously, any specific contract could deviate from its standard form, and in some localities different standards were also maintained. The two forms of the contract also travelled by various names. In Genoa, the term “commenda” was reserved strictly for the unilateral form, while the bilateral form was normally called the “societas maris”. In Venice, both forms of the contract were usually called “collegantia”. In Pisa, the unilateral *commenda* contract was normally called the “compagnia”, while the bilateral form was usually called the “societas”. 

Sometimes a merchant might enter into a *commenda* contract with only one *commendator* for an expedition.<sup>[101](#footnote101)</sup> Often, however, merchants entered into multiple *commenda* for an expedition. Sometimes a merchant traded on his own account next to the honoring of these contracts. Many remaining records of *commenda* specifically allow for that privilege. In the twelfth century, the capital contributed in these contracts was often in the form of goods rather than money. In the thirteenth century, however, the *commenda* increasingly became an instrument for raising financial capital. 

While a *commendator* was sometimes a retired merchant and an actively involved investor, the *commendator* was also frequently a passive investor. Passive investors already had a role in *commenda* contracts at the end of the twelfth century.<sup>[102](#footnote102)</sup> But they would have become increasingly common as markets developed for the commenda, increasingly turning it into an instrument for raising financial capital. Highly developed markets for *commenda* contracts existed in various Mediterranean cities by the end of the thirteenth century, notably in Venice. And “[t]he investors were often non-merchants, and they came from all walks of life—landed aristocrats, clergy, artisans, housewives, widows and orphans, and foreign investors”.<sup>[103](#footnote103)</sup>

Another key contract to reduce the risks of expeditions for merchants in the High Middle Ages was that of the maritime loan.<sup>[104](#footnote104)</sup> While not purely an insurance contract, it certainly had an important insurance function. Maritime loans are usually described as loans extended to merchants—and sometimes other involved parties such as ship owners or captains—that were only repaid upon the safe arrival of the goods or vessel at the destination port and, sometimes, also on the condition of the safe return of the vessel. If the journey was negatively affected due to general perils of navigation (e.g., storms, pirates), the loan did not have to be repaid or only partially repaid under certain circumstances.

Due to the risk element, the interest rates for these loans could be freely determined, unlike for standard loans which were subject to usury laws. The rates were high. De Roover, for example, notes that rates of forty to fifty percent were not uncommon for expeditions from Italy or Constantinople to Alexandria or Syria.<sup>[105](#footnote105)</sup> While the lender covered transportation risks, the commercial risks of an expedition still technically fell on the borrower. The separation was imperfect in practice, however, as adverse commercial conditions could also cause a borrower to default on the maritime loan. 

Maritime loans were already popular in the Greco-Roman period. The concept of wrapping an insurance function into a loan potentially even far precedes that period. The Code of Hammurabi indicates that the ancient Babylonians legally mandated leniency on repayment of debt contracts in certain circumstances in order to facilitate commerce. According to law 48 of the Code, for example, “If a man has incurred a debt and a storm has flooded his field or carried away the crop, or the corn has not grown because of drought, in that year he shall not pay his creditor. Further he shall post-date his bond and shall not pay interest for that year”.<sup>[106](#footnote106)</sup> Similarly, law 102 states that, if an agent has accepted money from a merchant for a trade expedition “and an enemy has forced him to give up some of the goods he was carrying, then the agent shall specify the amount on oath and shall be acquitted.”<sup>[107](#footnote107)</sup> These legal protections were surely considered in determining the interest rates for these loans.  

Charles Trenerry has actually argued that the Code illustrates that the Babylonians were already working with a precursor to the maritime loan for land expeditions. After a review of the available evidence, Trenerry concludes as follows:

> It is shown that in all essentials the idea of the contract of loans on Bottomry was present, *mutatis mutandis*, in the laws governing the loans for trading purposes made by the Babylonians from 4000-5000 years ago, as shown in the *Code of Hammurabi*, dating from *circa* 2250 B.C.<sup>[108](#footnote108)</sup>

In 1236, Pope Gregory IX condemned the sea loan as usurious. Subsequently, merchants increasingly shifted to other types of arrangements for managing transportation risks. One other type of contract which already existed at the time of the decree, the *cambium maritimum* (or *cambium nauticum*), became increasingly popular. Essentially, this was just an exchange contract, where the merchant acted as the taker, and repayment by the merchant or their correspondent in another location with a different currency was conditional on the arrival of the vessel or cargo. As with any other exchange contract, the interest rate was incorporated into the exchange rates. 

A big milestone in risk management occurred with the advent of modern marine insurance contracts in the fourteenth century.<sup>[109](#footnote109)</sup> With these contracts, a merchant or someone else involved in trade would be promised a payment in case of the materialization of certain transportation risks against an insurance premium paid in advance. So, unlike the maritime loan and the *cambium maritimum*, these marine insurance contracts fully separated casualty risks from credit risks, in a form that is broadly consistent with modern insurance contracts. These contracts were generally organized by brokers and typically had multiple underwriters. 

Marine insurance contracts developed in northern Italy during the fourteenth century. The earliest known examples are from Genoa, dated 1343 and 1347.<sup>[110](#footnote110)</sup> From northern Italy, the popularity of marine insurance contracts spread to the rest of the Mediterranean and Western Europe during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. It continued to be used mostly by Italians, however, well-into the early modern period. In the early seventeenth century, marine insurance was still used for no more than 1% of Dutch shipping and 10% of merchandise.<sup>[111](#footnote111)</sup>

Despite the short-term contracts that surrounded trade expeditions, most merchants were operating in an environment of some stability in the High Middle Ages. Many, of course, participated in guilds and other such associations that were more enduring. Merchants would have often also repeatedly entered into trade expeditions with certain other merchants that were connected via the extended family or from the same city. Most importantly, however, just as artisan and agricultural operations, merchant operations were typically structured around family households, which formed a stable base of operations.<sup>[112](#footnote112)</sup>

In some cases a merchant might indeed be “independent” and casually supported by other members of the household (e.g., his wife, his teenage son). In other cases, however, one household might include two or more merchants (e.g., two brothers or a father and son). A very large merchant household in the thirteenth century might have even included half a dozen merchants or more, normally bound by nuclear or extended family ties, living in a compound or palace. Two or more households might have also combined to form a stable base of operations; standardly any such alliances would also have been family-based.  

These stable units of merchant operations based around households and family ties are usually referred to as merchant houses. Traditionally, merchant houses were not really organized formally as a legal business entity. Starting in the thirteenth century, however, particularly the Italian merchant houses were increasingly formally organized as merchant companies.<sup>[113](#footnote113)</sup> In Venice that organization was in the form of the *fraterna*. In Genoa and Tuscany that legal organization was through the *compagnia* (here not to be confused with the Pisan name for the unilateral *commenda* mentioned above). Whereas the *fraterna* was a default legal form in Venice, the *compagnia* was an explicit contractual relationship. As mentioned in *Chapter 1*, the *compagnia* was an early form of the general partnership, one that became increasingly replaced by the *accomandita*, a form of limited partnership, in the fifteenth century.  

As the thirteenth century progressed, it was particularly the Italian merchant companies that were the face of the commercial revolution. Increasingly, they began operating with permanent branches of their outfits in important cities for trade and finance, such as Avignon, Paris, Barcelona, and Bruges.<sup>[114](#footnote114)</sup> Branches and outposts of trading companies outside of Italy were largely populated with Italians, often sent over by the company’s headquarters. Sometimes they were led by one of the senior partners of the company or a senior representative known as a factor (*fattori*). The Medici and other companies also had branches that were led by junior partners.

In cities and towns without a branch, a merchant company could leverage agents on the basis of commission. The branches of the larger Italian merchant companies often acted as such agents in places like Bruges, Paris, Barcelona, and London. The advent of the Italian-led commercial revolution in the thirteenth century did not eliminate the caravan merchant, or at least the nomadic merchant, entirely from the commercial landscape of the Late Middle Ages, not even in Italy. Many merchants, particularly those of lesser importance, would have continued to lead fairly nomadic lifestyles.<sup>[115](#footnote115)</sup> That said, even independent merchants utilized commission-based agents to conduct their business in a sedentary fashion.<sup>[116](#footnote116)</sup>

So, the commercial revolution was very much an Italian affair. Their leadership in commerce was supported by advancements in partnership contracts, accounting, bills of exchange, and other aspects of business administration in the Late Middle Ages. Their success crowded out merchants elsewhere, particularly in Western Europe. Many of the towns and cities that had some commercial and financial prominence during the Late Middle Ages and early modern period such as Avignon, Paris, Barcelona, Bruges, and Antwerp became populated with colonies of predominantly Italian merchants. 

During fourteenth and fifteenth century Bruges, for instance, the native upper class “was not made up of merchants, save for a few exceptions, but of brokers, inn-keepers, drapiers, and commission agents”.<sup>[117](#footnote117)</sup> Native merchants were largely absent from the upper class because international trade through the city was facilitated by companies from Venice, Genoa, Florence, Lucca, Sienna, and elsewhere in Italy. This was quite different than the picture at the end of the thirteenth century when the Flemish merchants still congregated in caravans to sell their wares at the Fairs of Champagne. For another example, Lyon in 1557 apparently had 183 foreign merchants permanently residing there. Over eighty percent of them were Italians, over a quarter of the Italian merchants coming from Florence.<sup>[118](#footnote118)</sup>

It is commonly noted that during the commercial revolution partnership contracts became of increasingly longer duration.<sup>[119](#footnote119)</sup> The main mechanisms behind that observation were probably twofold. 

First, certain merchant companies would grown in size and capital. With increasing representation abroad through partners, factors, or agents, they increasingly would have financed their own trade. Second, long-term partnerships formed outside the scope of a single merchant house became increasingly common. Certain merchant houses fused for all or some of their activities. Members of a merchant house increasingly also formed longer-term secondary partnerships outside of it for certain purposes.<sup>[120](#footnote120)</sup> The family would have still remained important, but less so as we progress from the thirteenth century. 

These two trends would have come at the expense of venture contracts that involved equity capital, notably the *commenda*. The latter would have also been impacted by other trends, such as the development of marine insurance. 


## 6. Merchant banks

Many of the merchant companies that marked the European landscape from the thirteenth century onwards, particularly the larger ones, were not just engaged in trade, but also in financial services. Those merchant companies are known as merchant banks. Merchant banking first seems to have appeared around the exchange business that was needed to facilitate trade at the Fairs of Champagne in the thirteenth century. The business was controlled mostly by the Siennese throughout the century, before they gave way to the Florentines who dominated the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.<sup>[121](#footnote121)</sup>

What exactly warrants the label of merchant “bank”?

Merchants then, as now, would have frequently sold goods on credit to their clients as well as bought goods on credit from their suppliers. That extension of commercial credit hardly warrants the label of “bank”.<sup>[122](#footnote122)</sup> Instead, the merchant banks were “banks” because they extended credit beyond their own clients, processed international payments, and engaged in other types of financial activities. While many merchants in this period might have occasionally dabbled in financial services, for the merchant bankers that was truly a line of business in its own right. Trade remained important to merchant banks. Certainly for the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, we have no examples of merchant banks that transformed purely into financial service providers.<sup>[123](#footnote123)</sup>

Just as most other merchant companies, the merchant banks were typically organized around certain powerful families from the north of Italy. Some of the most well-known companies from the Late Middle Ages and early modern period include the Bardi, Peruzzi, Acciaiuoli, Alberti, and Medici from Florence; the Grimaldi and Spinola from Genoa; and the Borromei from Milan. It was particularly the Florentine firms that dominated the merchant banking landscape. Merchant banking was not exclusively Italian, of course. Notably, in the sixteenth century, south Germany saw the presence of several powerful merchant banking houses like the Fuggers and the Welsers. 

The merchant banks of the Late Middle Ages and early modern period were nowhere near the size of modern international banks. According to De Roover,<sup>[124](#footnote124)</sup> in the early fourteenth century, the Acciaiuoli, one of the main merchant banking companies at the time, only employed 41 factors excluding those in the home office of Florence. The rest of the company would have consisted of some partners and lower-level employees such as messengers and apprentices. Kohn mentions that the Peruzzi at their peak in the fourteenth century had around 15 branches from Western Europe to the Levant, employing around 120 partners and factors.<sup>[125](#footnote125)</sup> The Medici at their peak in the fifteenth century seem to have employed around 40 to 50 factors, and 10 or 11 branch managers.<sup>[126](#footnote126)</sup>

While small operations according to modern standards, these famous merchant banks certainly would have been impressive in the Late Middle Ages and early modern period. They would have far exceeded in size and complexity other types of business operations. Of course, many less famous merchant banks would have been substantially smaller in size. 

The main financial business of the merchant bankers consisted of commercial lending, sovereign lending, the processing of long distance payments, and arbitrage and speculation on currency exchange rates. The key financial instrument to all these activities—other than sovereign lending—was the exchange contract, first in the form of the *cambium* contract but mostly in the form of the bill of exchange. Essentially, the business of the banker was the exchange business. Illustratively, the Italian expression to “run a bank” (*fare il banco*) was at the time synonymous to the expression “to deal in exchange” (*fare il cambio*),<sup>[127](#footnote127)</sup> while in Elizabethan England, the term “banker” was synymous with the term “exchanger”.<sup>[128](#footnote128)</sup>

To obtain an impression of the exchange business, lets first explore how exchange contracts were utilized to extend commercial loans. We utilize for that purpose an example from the Datini archives.<sup>[129](#footnote129)</sup>

> In the name of God, amen.
December 12, 1399
Pay at usance by this first of exchange to Domenico Sancio six hundred écus at 10s. 5d. \[barcelonese\] per ▽, which ▽600 at 10s. 5d. per écu are for the value received \[here\] from Jacopo Gosco, and charge them to our account. God be with you.
Giovanni Orlandini and Piero Benizi & Co.
In Bruges
 
On the backside of the note was written the name of the firm “Francesco da Prato & Co. in Barcelona”. This would have referred to a company of the famous merchant banker, Francesco di Marco Datini (1335–1410). The downward triangle on the note is an editorial convention to replace a mark denoting the écu.

The bill was written on December 12, 1399. It specifies that, in Bruges, Jacopo Gosco (the deliverer) paid a sum of money to the company of Orlandini and Benizi (the taker). The amount was 600 écus. The écu here did not refer to the famous French gold coin, but was purely a unit of account that was equivalent to 22 Flemish silver pence. Many places in southern and western Europe, including in Barcelona and Bruges, used the system of account mentioned earlier, where a pound was equivalent to 20 shillings, and each shilling was equivalent to 12 pence. So the sum of money delivered, 600 écus, was equal to £55 of Flemish pence.   

The company of Orlandini and Benizi orders the Datini company in Barcelona to make a payment to Domenico Sancio. The exchange rate is listed as 10s 5d Barcelonese per écu, or 125 Barcelonese per écu. The 600 écus would, thus, have been worth 75,000 Barcelonese, or £312 10s Barcelonese. The bill was accepted on January 11, 1400 and the payment was made on February 11, 1400, in line with the usance of 60 days between Bruges and Barcelona. 

Note that the firm of Orlandini and Benizi requests that payment of 600 écus to Domenico Sancio be made by “charging them to our account”. The Datini company in Barcelona would have likely also credited the amount of the bill to Domenico Sancio’s account. As already mentioned in *Section 2*, physical money often did not change hands with the two payments required for an exchange contract. As stated by De Roover,<sup>[130](#footnote130)</sup>

> It would be a mistake to believe that bills were usually paid in specie. The records of the Datini branch in Barcelona disclose that such was not the case and that bills of exchange were often paid by assignment in bank or by book transfer of one kind or another.  

What was the commercial context to the bill of exchange above?

The firm of Orlandini and Benizi was a merchant company. It was in the business of buying Flemish wool and selling it in places like Barcelona. Likely, they used the 600 écus to purchase wool, which they then transported to Barcelona. It may, then, have been sold by the Datini company or perhaps another commercial agent. The funds from the sale, after any commissions, would have, then, been deposited with the account of Orlandini and Benizi’s company with the Datini Company. That deposit would have covered the upcoming payment due to Domenico Sancio from the bill of exchange. In case the timing of the deposit was too late, the Datini company would have likely extended credit to Orlandini and Benizi’s company. 

Jacopo Gosco, the deliverer of the 600 écus, was a Venetian merchant banker. Essentially, he lent funds to the company of Orlandini and Benizi to buy inventory. The repayment of the loan occurs two months later in Barcelona. However, the repayment has been made to Domenico Sancio, another merchant banker and a correspondent of Jacopo Gosco. How does Gosco now obtain his profits from the loan? 

One common practice would have been for Sancio to buy a second bill of exchange and that is exactly what happened. A translation of that bill is seen below.<sup>[131](#footnote131)</sup>

> In the name of God, amen 12th of February 1400.
Pay at usance by this first letter of exchange to Jacopo Goscio \[the sum of\] 625 écus at 10s 0d Barcelonese per écu, which 625 écus at 10s 0d per écu are for the value received here from Domenico Sancio; and charge this amount to our account.
God be with you.
Francesco del Tovaglia and Co. in Barcelona

On the backside of the note was written “Giuliano Zaccheria and Co. in Bruges”. 

Sancio used the services of a merchant bank in Barcelona, the Tovaglia company, to send Gosco’s £312 10s Barcelonese pence, converted into Flemish money, back to Bruges. Given that the exchange rate in Barcelona was 10s 0d Barcelonese per écu, or 120 Barcelonese per écu, the total paid out in Bruges was 625 écus. That payment was honored by the Zaccheria company, a merchant bank that was a correspondent of the Tovaglia company. The bill was accepted by the Zaccheria company on March 11, 1400 and paid out on April 11, 1400. 

In about four months, Gosco made a return of 25 écus on his original loan of 600 écus to Orlandini and Benizi’s company. Considered annually, the interest rate on the loan is 12.5 percent. The interest on the loan stems from the differences in the two exchange rates between Barcelonese and écus. In the original bill, the exchange rate was 10s 5d Barcelonese per écu; in the return bill, the exchange rate was 10s 0d Barcelonese per écu. In other words, Gosco received more Barcelonese per écu from the first bill, then he was required to pay for reverting back into écus in the second bill. The presence of an interest rate on Gosco’s loan was, thus, the product of Barcelonese being relatively less valuable in Bruges than in Barcelona.

None of this was a happy accident. In order for bankers like Gosco to make money by short-term loans, there had to be a financial incentive through interest. And that interest was captured through exchange rate spreads, precisely by currencies being relatively more valued in their home base. That is, a healthy lending market required that francs were relatively more desirable in Paris, groats relatively more desirable in Bruges, ducats relatively more desirable in Venice, and so on. The larger the favorable exchange spread between two places, the larger the interest rate on exchange contracts. Evidence on medieval interest rates suggests that this supposition about market equilibrium was indeed correct.<sup>[132](#footnote132)</sup>

Exchange rates, of course, could be negatively affected due to currency devaluations, wars, and other types of events that shocked the market. So the merchant bankers did not always make a profit on the extension of loans through exchange contracts. But overall, the profits of that activity outweighed the losses. Note that it was precisely these risks to exchange rates that largely placed exchange dealings outside of the scope of usury laws. 

The example above involving Jacopo Gosco as the lender seems to have been a fairly common way to extend commercial credit using bills of exchange.<sup>[133](#footnote133)</sup> The exact mechanics for extending commercial credit, however, could vary substantially. Kohn,<sup>[134](#footnote134)</sup> for example, suggests that Lucchese silk merchants in the thirteenth century utilized commercial credit via the following mechanism:

* They would purchase silk from Genoa by selling exchange contracts to Genoese merchant bankers. These contracts would be due at a fair in Champagne.
* Once they sold the silk in Lucca, the merchants would, in turn, buy exchange contracts due at the same fair in Champagne with the proceeds. They could even buy these contracts from the same merchant bankers as to which they sold contracts earlier in Genoa.
* At the next fair in Champagne, the agents of the Lucchese merchants would, then, use the proceeds from the bills they had purchased in Lucca to settle the bills they sold in Genoa. As mentioned in *Section 3*, these contracts were probably settled during the clearing and settlement phase of the fair. 

The types of arrangements for utilizing commercial credit as described above worked well for merchant companies that had correspondents abroad. But without such correspondents, it was difficult to sell a bill of exchange and loan money. The purpose of dry exchange (*cambio secco*) was precisely to make credit available to such merchants. A dry exchange was just the extension of a local loan disguised through two exchange transactions.<sup>[135](#footnote135)</sup> It was called “dry exchange” because no trade was palpably involved.<sup>[136](#footnote136)</sup>

To understand dry exchange, lets consider an easy to follow hypothetical example. Suppose that Paolo, a merchant, needs 100 florins in Florence, and that the Medici bank decides to help him through a dry exchange with its subsidiary in Venice. The exchange rates are 20 florins per ducat in Florence and 21 florins per ducat in Venice, as required for a healthy lending market. One common method for dry exchange known as “exchange and rechange” (*cambium et recambium*) would have roughly proceeded as follows:

* Paolo accepts 100 florins and sells a bill of exchange in which the Medici bank in Venice is both the payer and the payee. The payment due in Venice is for 5 ducats given the exchange rate of 20 florins per Ducat in Florence.
* The Medici bank in Venice honors the bill at its due date by crediting the Medici bank in Florence with 5 ducats on its permanent account. It temporarily opens an account for Paolo and debits the account for 5 ducats.
* Next, the Medici bank in Venice writes a second bill, where Paolo is the payer and the Medici bank in Florence the payee. The amount of the bill is for 5 ducats worth of florins, or 105 florins given the exchange rate of 21 florins per ducat. The Medici Bank in Venice credits 5 ducats to Paolo’s account and closes it. It also debits 5 ducats to the running account of the Medici bank in Florence.
* At the due date, Paolo pays the sum of 105 florins to the Medici Bank in Florence.

At the end of this two-way exchange, the Medici bank in Venice is in the same position as before. Paolo receives his loan of 100 florins, and the Medici bank in Florence receives 5% interest over the loan (which according to usance would have been repaid after 20 days). No actual commerce has visibly taken place. There were various other methods for dry exchange than that illustrated above.<sup>[137](#footnote137)</sup> In a “fictitious exchange” (*cambio fittizio*), the first bill was not even sent to the other city, and the deliverer relied on the exchange rate information relayed by correspondents to draw up a second bill. 

In many cases of dry exchange, such as in the example above, exchange rate risks were still present. Some cases exist, however, in which the rate for the second bill of exchange was agreed upon beforehand. Such dry exchanges would have eliminated any exchange rate risks entirely.<sup>[138](#footnote138)</sup> While many dry exchanges did not differ in substance with regards to exchange rate risks from regular multi-exchange transactions involving commerce, dry exchange was generally frowned upon much more with respect to usury laws.<sup>[139](#footnote139)</sup> The absence of any palpable commercial transaction at the second location would have surely increased suspicions. Recent evidence presented by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli on account ledgers from the Borromei of Milan suggests that dry exchange was, probably as to be expected given these considerations, uncommon.<sup>[140](#footnote140)</sup>
 
Exchange contracts originated as instruments for making international payments and certainly continued to be used that way throughout the Late Middle Ages and early modern period. In those situations, the merchant banker would act as a taker, rather than a deliverer. While merchant bankers sometimes also charged fees for processing bills of exchange, this seems to have been a small part of their overall business model. So, how would a merchant banker make money on processing an international payment, particularly given our discussion on exchange rate spreads between different towns and cities above?

Consider again the initial exchange contract involving Jacopo Gosco, the Venetian merchant banker, and the Orlandini and Benizi company in Bruges. On December 12, 1399, Gosco lent a sum of money of 600 écus. While Gosco’s exchange rate for any return bill was still uncertain, the first bill utilized an exchange rate of 10s 5d Barcelonese per écu. Suppose now that earlier in the day Gosco was requested to play precisely the opposite role, namely that of a taker for a bill on Barcelona of precisely 600 écus. The deliverer could have been another merchant, an aristocrat, a church official, and so on who needed to make an international payment. Suppose that for this transaction, Gosco also used his correspondent, Domenico Sancio, but as a payer.  

If Gosco played his cards right, he would have arranged a rate of Barcelonese per écu that was lower on the earlier bill. It’s easy to see why. Suppose that, indeed, Gosco had played his cards right and had charged 10d 3s per écu. As a result of this bill for an international payment, Gosco would have owned 600 écus in Bruges. In two months time, he would also have had a debt to Sancio of £307 10s Barcelonese when the bill was due in Barcelona. While the 600 écus would have been lent to Orlandini and Benizi’s firm later in the day, that transaction would have also resulted in a credit of £312 10s with Sancio two months later. As both bills would have been due on the same day two months later, Gosco would have ended up with a positive balance of £5 Barcelonese on his account with Sancio in Barcelona. 

Once both exchange contracts were completed, Gosco might have asked Sancio to remit the £5 Barcelonese profit back to him at the rate of 10s 0d per écu, as we know from the actual record was the exchange rate at that time. This bill would have yielded him a total of 10 écus profit in Bruges two months later. While Gosco now seemingly has less profit than in the original exposition of the actual exchange, where the profit was 25 écus, it adds some realism to the situation. In order to lend out 600 écus in the first place, Gosco would have needed to obtain them from somewhere. At least one common method was to do what any good banker does: borrow at a low rate and lend out at a high rate. 

There are, of course, various other ways in which Gosco could have had 600 écus at his disposal to lend that led to the actual exchange in the records. Capital for lending was partially supplied by the partners through the initial required investment, or the *corpo*. That was, however, generally only a fraction of the available capital. 

A normally much larger amount was known as the *sopracorpo* and consisted of three sources of funds: (1) time deposits from the partners that were renumerated at a fixed interest rate; (2) time deposits from outsiders, including other merchants, aristocrats, and clergymen, but also small investors, typically also Italian;<sup>[141](#footnote141)</sup> and retained earnings, which were generally not withdrawn until the end of the partnership. Time deposits generally circumvented usury laws by making the interest payment voluntary in the form of a gift, though such payments were surely essential to maintaining credibility.<sup>[142](#footnote142)</sup> The *sopracorpo* as well as borrowing at favorable rates through processing international payments would have constituted the most significant sources of capital for lending.<sup>[143](#footnote143)</sup>

The role played by the merchant bankers in any particular bill of exchange market, such as in Bruges or Barcelona, was that of a market maker. They ensured that any particular market for exchange contracts had liquidity, by being ready to both buy bills and sell them. Obviously, however, the merchant bankers were only willing to play market makers because they could maintain a favorable exchange rate spread. More specifically, the merchant bankers would buy bills, or lend money, at an exchange rate that valued the countercurrency as relatively less than when they would sell bills of exchange, or borrow money.

To sum it up. Bankers also profited from fees. But it is the spread in exchange rates that seems to have driven the market for exchange contracts. The merchant bankers maintained a spread in exchange rates locally, where the countercurrency was valued relatively less for lending than for borrowing activities. Additionally, across cities and towns, equilibrium required for any currency to be relatively more valuable in its place of use. While the spread in exchange rates between cities and towns could temporarily be disrupted by devaluations, wars, and so on, the need for international payments and credit would have eventually readjusted market conditions to a favorable spread.    

Markets where securities are bought and sold that have a maturity of less than one year are commonly called money markets. Into the early modern period, bills of exchange could only be bought and sold at their initial issue, and they were not tradeable. That is in sharp contrast to, say, modern stocks or bonds which are tradeable on exchanges. The exchangeability of bills of exchange only really developed in early seventeenth century Antwerp,<sup>[144](#footnote144)</sup> before it spread as a general feature of bills of exchange throughout Europe. That said, we can still call all the cities, towns, and fairs where markets developed for exchange contracts as “money markets”.

As already mentioned, highly developed money markets for exchange contracts really only existed in a select number of towns and fairs into the early modern period. The towns and cities were known as banking places.<sup>[145](#footnote145)</sup> They included, to varying degrees at varying times, places like Florence, Venice, Rome, Naples, Bruges, Barcelona, Antwerp, and London. Prominent money markets were also seen at international fairs such as those at Champagne, Geneva, Lyon, and Besançon-Piacenza. Given the concentration of merchant bankers in these locations, the competition and pressure on exchange rate spreads in them was relatively high. Each market was typically facilitated by brokers that served as intermediaries between buyers and sellers for exchange contracts on commission. The competitive pressures would have often made indirect payment routes more efficient than direct transactions.<sup>[146](#footnote146)</sup>

Outside of a major money market, transactions involving exchange contracts would have been impossible, or in any case more difficult and costly. It presented a major obstacle, for example, to the Papacy in collecting payments throughout Europe. In the words of De Roover,<sup>[147](#footnote147)</sup>

> In outposts, such as Scandinavia or Poland, or even relatively backward countries, such as Germany where there were no banking facilities, papal transfers suffered long delays and were attended with great risks and difficulties. Often papal agents would have to rely on casual menas such as traveling merchants, pilgrims, or students who would undertake to carry money or goods to the nearest banking center. Transfers from Poland sometimes took six months or more, whereas funds received in Bruges or London were made available in Rome within a month or less by a simple letter of advice. The bankers provided expeditious and efficient service. The smooth operation of this system depended, of course, on the existence of a regular money market, and this was found only in a few trading centers where the volume of business was large enough to make it worth while for the Italian banking houses to maintain branch offices. 

Besides their role in payment and credit operations, exchange contracts were also used in arbitrage and speculative activities. A merchant in Bruges might, for instance, move funds to Barcelona purely in anticipation of a favorable shock to exchange rates there, or for use in relatively high-interest time deposits. The mechanics and business incentives behind all the different payment, credit, speculative, and arbitrage activities could fill an entire book. As noted by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli,<sup>[148](#footnote148)</sup> “as much paper and ink has probably been expended on discussing the functions of bills of exchange as was used in the writing of the hundreds of thousands of these brief documents in the later middle ages”. Our purpose here has been merely to offer an impression of how the instrument was used for payments and credit operations. 

The last activity of the merchant bankers that deserves attention here is sovereign lending. The merchant bankers were for centuries an important source of capital to municipal, territorial, and national rulers. During the Middle Ages and into the modern period, those loans were largely personal. It is only during the early modern period that loans increasingly became the responsibility of a government rather than a ruler.<sup>[149](#footnote149)</sup> These loans were, just as commercial loans, short-term, but frequently rolled over. 

Sometimes sovereign lending was also arranged via exchange contracts. A notable example is the sixteenth century system of *asientos* loans to the Spanish crown from primarily Genoese merchant bankers.<sup>[150](#footnote150)</sup> Typically, however, sovereign loans were organized via promissory notes, not exchange contracts. These loans would have also been influenced by usury laws. So, often, rather than charging interest, such loans would be extended without interest and in exchange for monopoly rights on trade. It is in this manner, for example, how certain Italian merchant banks came to control the grain trade in Naples and Sicily and the wool trade in England.<sup>[151](#footnote151)</sup> Another popular method of extending sovereign loans was through the promise of future revenues from taxes and duties. Given the administrative knack of the Italian merchant banks, they, hence, often acted as tax farmers for sovereigns in these arrangements.  

Lending to sovereigns was an extremely risky businesses, one that the Medici, for example, as a consequence tried to avoid. As noted by De Roover,<sup>[152](#footnote152)</sup>

> Once engaged in making advances to princes, it was impossible to pull out and to retrench without courting disaster. Inevitably the lender was sucked deeper and deeper into the whirlpool, as the Medici found out to their detriment. And a banker who had been pressed dry was mercilessly dropped like an orange whose juice has been squeezed out. 


## 7. The Hanseatic League

The Italians dominated trade and finance in the Mediterranean region and Western Europe. In northern Europe, trade was dominated by Germans through the Hanseatic League.<sup>[153](#footnote153)</sup> The term hansa referred to certain associations of travelling merchants doing business in foreign cities. In the High Middle Ages, increasing cooperation between these associations eventually grew into a federation of towns known as the Hanseatic League. The League lasted into the early modern period, but its peak occurred during the Late Middle Ages. 

There were various degrees of membership and the pool of member towns obviously changed throughout the league’s existence. In general, however, members were in northern Germany and the rest of the baltic region, as well as in Western Germany and the Netherlands. Core members were towns such as Lübeck, Rostock, Hamburg, and Cologne. Most of the merchants and associates that operated within the league were, naturally, German. The league also had trading outposts in many towns that were not members. These were called *kontors*, and included such places as London, Bruges, Bergen, and Novgorod. Often the merchants operated within particular areas of the city, and they would have formed colonies similar to the Italians. Particularly in some of the *kontors*, trading activities would have been conducted under fairly risky circumstances.  

The merchants of the Hanseatic League were in many regards not quite as developed as their Italian counterparts. While trade indeed became more sedentary over time, that transformation was less developed than among the Italian merchants. Large trading companies did not arise. In general, the business techniques of the Hanseatic merchants also remained behind those of the Italians, at least until the sixteenth century. Notably, banking and money markets in the Baltic region were much less developed than in the Mediterranean and much of Western Europe. As a consequence, moving funds within and outside of the Hanseatic region often occurred through shipments of specie.
