# Chapter 1: Banks and banking

Banks are at the core of money and payments. Like it or not, they are some of the most fundamental institutions that make our society run (and sometimes come to a screeching halt). To understand money and payment practices in the United States, we, therefore, require starting with a background on banks and banking. 

We start in *Section 1* by characterizing, specifically, commercial banks. They are by far the most important type of bank in the United States. After setting out a broad characterization of commercial banks, we examine their legal foundations in *Section 2*. Finally, in *Section 3*, we complete our description of the banking landscape in the United States with a discussion of savings associations, credit unions, and other types of deposit banks.       


## 1. Commercial banks

We can distinguish between various types of banks. But by the far the most important type of bank in the global financial landscape is what in the United States is called the commercial bank. 

Commercial banks are key credit providers to consumers and businesses. They offer many products and services for payments, savings, and investments. They employ a great number of people. And a somewhat less well-known fact, commercial banks play a crucial role in determining the overall money supply. Unsurprisingly, their activities and financial performance have a massive impact on the overall state of an economy. 

In the United States, the commercial banking sector is largely controlled by a few household names such as Citibank, JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, and the Bank of America. Most other countries and regions across the world are similarly dominated by a handful of commercial banks. 

* In France, the behemoths include BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, and Société Générale, and in Spain they include Banco Santander, BBVA, and CaixaBank. 
* The United Kingdom is dominated by Lloyd’s, Barclays, HSBC, and NatWest. These institutions are often also called **high-street banks** because they are commercial banks that maintain a network of physical branches. 
* Australian commercial banking is largely controlled by the Commonwealth Bank, ANZ, WestPac, and NAB. 
* Japan’s banking landscape is dominated by several megabanks, including MUFG Bank, SMBC, and Mizuho Bank. Because these commercial banks are active primarily in urban areas, they are more specifically called **city banks**. While the name “commercial bank” is common in France, Spain, Australia, and the United Kingdom, commercial banks in Japan are, instead, usually known as **ordinary banks** (futsu ginko).    

Besides these well-known giants, there are thousands of smaller commercial banks that operate in the global financial landscape. 

Most commercial banks in the United States are legally required to be members of the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** (**FDIC**). This is a government-owned corporation which insures the money that consumers and businesses keep in banks, at least up to a certain amount. Each quarter, the FDIC publishes a report on the financial performance of its member banks. According to the quarterly report from September 2024,<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> the United States counted a whopping 3,966 commercial banks with over $23 Trillion in combined assets. Most of these were smaller community and regional banks, often in rural areas. 

A similar picture emerges in Europe. While the number of credit institutions—mostly consisting of commercial banks—has been consolidating in recent years, the twenty-seven members of the European Union still counted more than 4,614 credit institutions that were natively incorporated in 2020. Some member states show much more variety than others. Germany, for example, has a much greater number of credit institutions than France and Spain, even accounting for differences in population size.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup>

So what exactly is a commercial bank? And what distinguishes it from other types of banks and financial institutions? 

While commercial banks offer a wide variety of services and, particularly the larger ones, are often complex organizations, a fairly basic characterization suffices. Specifically, **commercial banks** are *for-profit* financial institutions that are marked by the following five characteristics. 

1. They provide bank accounts to consumers and businesses that have a balance in credit money, in particular accounts that offer withdrawals without notice. The latter type of account is standardly utilized for making and receiving payments.  
2. They extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses.
3. They are typically incorporated. 
4. They require special approval from the government to operate and are subject to restrictive laws and regulations.
5. They affect the money supply, primarily through their lending activities. 

First, the money that sits in the checking and savings accounts of consumers and businesses is normally called **credit money**. The other main form of money for the general public is called **currency**, which is money in the form of coins and notes. 

The balance of credit money in a bank account is determined by deposits, withdrawals, and, if applicable, interest payments and fees. 

Account owners can increase their balance of credit money through deposits. A **deposit** here is just an amount of credit money that is added to a bank account as a result of a transfer in credit money from another account—say a payment for goods and services, a gift, or a tax return—or an exchange of cash with the bank. The other type of credit money transfer that can add to the balance of a bank account is an interest payment from the bank. 

Commercial banks notably offer bank accounts to consumers and businesses from which money can be *drawn without notice*. The main type of account here is what is typically called a **checking account** in the United States and a **current account** in the United Kingdom. 

Checking accounts, as we will call them, are those that consumers and businesses use on a day-to-day basis for receiving and making payments, typically through mobile or web-based software applications, or via physical or virtual payment cards. Money in these accounts can normally also be withdrawn in cash via a bank teller or more commonly from an **automated teller machine** (**ATM**). Usually checking accounts come with fees and receive relatively little or no interest. 

Besides checking accounts, commercial banks commonly provide various types of other accounts. Certain very restrictive accounts only allow a single deposit when you open them, and do not allow withdrawals until after several months or even years after the deposit has been made (except perhaps at a penalty). These accounts pay relatively high interest rates. In the English language, we see a variety of terms that pertain to such accounts, including **time deposit accounts**, **term deposit accounts**, and **certificate of deposit accounts**. The latter is a particularly common term in the United States.<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup>

Other types of very restrictive accounts offered by commercial banks are those specifically geared towards retirement. These accounts typically offer a means to save, invest, or a combination of both. In some cases, these **retirement accounts** are organized by employers or unions. Retirement accounts also exist that are organized by individuals independently, typically called **individual retirement accounts** (**IRAs**) in the United States and **personal pension accounts** in the United Kingdom. Retirement accounts come with various degrees of influence regarding savings and investment decisions. 
 
Both time deposits and savings-oriented retirement accounts are generally considered to be special, very restrictive types of **savings accounts**. There are many different account products that travel by this name, but a more standard savings account might look as follows:

* Money has to be withdrawn to a checking account, either at the same bank or a different bank, before it can be used. Sometimes this process is quick and without real limitations. In other cases, the process is somewhat more restricted. 
* The account normally comes without payment cards or check books. 
* The account usually enjoys a positive interest rate, at least in fairly normal economic times. Checking accounts, because they are less restrictive, will often have no interest, or at least a lower rate of interest.  
Another common type of account offered by commercial banks is the **investment account**, where deposited funds can be used to buy stocks, bonds, and other types of investment products. These accounts sometimes also offer interest rates on the credit money balance, and the funds have to be withdrawn to a checking account before they can be utilized more generally. Investment-oriented retirement accounts are really just restrictive investment accounts. 

The second main characteristic of commercial banks is that they are key institutions for lending to businesses and consumers. With respect to consumers, commercial banks mostly lend on a running basis through overdrafts on checking accounts and through credit cards. Commercial banks also furnish loans for, amongst other things, education, cars, and, most importantly, property purchases and renovations. With respect to businesses, commercial banks give a variety of loans that can be used for large investments and working capital. Many well-run businesses have a generous line of credit through overdrafts on their bank accounts and credit cards. For large commercial projects, commercial banks and other financial institutions will often join forces in extending loans. The latter types of loans are typically called **syndicated loans**. 

You will commonly hear the practice of lending referred to as “the extension of credit.” The term **credit** in this expression just means a loaned sum of money. In Latin, “credit” means “one believes.” So the expression “the extension of credit” emphasizes the faith the lender has in the borrower to pay back the loan. A person or institution lending money is often called a **creditor**. Commercial banks extend credit through formal loans as well as through overdrafts on accounts and credit cards. 

Other than denoting a loaned sum of money, the term “credit” also takes on various other interrelated meanings in finance. A person or institution’s **credit**, **credit rating**, or **credit score**, for instance, is an indication of their general trustworthiness with regards to paying back loans, as in the expression “That person has good credit.” To **credit** someone’s account means just to add money to the balance of that account. And we have already mentioned, credit money is money that exists as an accounting entry, particularly in our checking and savings accounts. 

Next, commercial banks are standardly incorporated entities and require special approval from the government to operate as a bank. Most certainly in developed economies, operating as a commercial bank normally comes with strict regulatory and legal requirements that are much more extensive than those for other types of companies. The body of laws and regulations that applies to commercial banks as well as various other financial institutions is usually known as a jurisdiction’s **banking laws**. So-called **compliance departments** as well as legal departments typically ensure that a commercial bank meets all these requirements. 

The costs of compliance on modern commercial banks are typically very high. Many would argue that these costs are too high and that they hamper product development, customer service, and real innovation. A particularly salient target of criticism here are the laws and regulations which aim to prevent **money laundering**: that is, the introduction of money obtained from criminal activities into the commercial and financial system, typically with significant efforts to conceal the criminal origins of that money. Financial crime expert Ronald Pol, for example, has recently suggested that the global anti-money laundering regime might be the “world’s least effective policy experiment.”<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup>

Finally, commercial banks affect the money supply. The most important mechanism in this regard is that they can affect the supply of credit money in our checking and savings accounts through their lending activities. Central banks attempt to steer the overall supply of credit money in banks through **monetary policy**. Government bodies and central banks also seek to affect the credit money supply through the regulatory regime, particularly through capital and liquidity requirements on commercial banks. 

We should, at this point, make a small clarification on the term credit money. It is often used synonymously with **bank money**, that is, money which is specifically issued by banks. But the two concepts are not entirely equivalent. 

First, while banks have a practical monopoly over credit money—as it mostly sits in our checking and savings accounts—it is not a complete monopoly. Credit “money” can also exist outside of banks, properly speaking, such as within **community time banks**, where participants receive time credits for their labor, which can, in turn, be used to purchase goods and services within the community. Second, while banks can indeed issue credit money, they can also issue currency, particularly paper currency. While this practice is rare nowadays, some jurisdictions, such as Scotland and Hong Kong, still allow banks to issue paper currency. 

So, in sum, characteristically, commercial banks are for-profit financial institutions which (1) provide bank accounts to consumers and businesses, notably checking accounts; (2) extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses; (3) are typically incorporated entities; (4) require special approval from the government to operate and are subject to restrictive laws and regulations; and (5) substantially affect the money supply, primarily through their lending activities.

Importantly, the characterization of commercial banks offered here pertains to what we commonly call “commercial banks” in everyday language. It is not intended to have prescriptive implications. Some, for example, might balk at the suggestion that commercial banks can affect the money supply, or that they need special approval from the government to operate and are heavily regulated. The purpose here is not to express a strong opinion on those matters, at least not for now, but merely to provide a baseline conceptualization of a modern commercial bank that allows for further discussion.

It is also important to realize that the terms “banks”, “banking”, and “bankers” are used with a fairly wide variety of broadly interrelated meanings in everyday language. An investment bank, for example, is quite a different type of institution than a commercial bank, even though they are commonly called “banks”. 

For clarity, therefore, we will sometimes speak of **deposit banks**, which is the collective name for institutions that accept deposits from the public into credit money accounts; facilitate payments with that credit money, including in the form of credit money transfers; make loans to consumers and businesses; and affect the money supply, primarily through lending. A commercial bank as described above is really the most important type of deposit bank in the modern world. 

Of the five characteristics of commercial banks provided above, the first two should be fairly intuitive to anyone with a passing familiarity of those institutions. The last three characteristics are surely more obscure to laymen. 

The fact that commercial banks play such a substantial role within our money supply probably sounds rather juicy, or even scandalous, to the layman. Given that it is such an encompassing topic, however, we must defer discussion of it to later chapters, primarily *Chapters 2*, *3*, and *6*. Instead, we will for now only turn to two more mundane, though no less important, characteristics of commercial banks: that they are typically incorporated and subject to restrictive laws and regulations. More specifically, we will now turn to an exploration of the legal foundations of commercial banks in the United States.  

    
## 2. Legal foundations

To develop a good understanding of commercial banks, as well as banks and banking more broadly, we require some background on their legal foundations. We will start our discussion with the claim in *Section 1* that commercial banks are “typically incorporated.” What exactly does that mean?  

In practically every country, there are various types of **legal structures**, which businesses and organizations can adopt upon their instantiation. These are broad templates that describe the basic nature of the organization and have important legal and regulatory implications. As legal structures are only broad templates, two businesses or organizations with the same legal structure can still vary greatly with regards to their business or organizational model, governance and management arrangements, operational details, and so on.   

In the United States, the establishment of companies and organizations is largely the prerogative of the state governments in which they will come to reside. For companies, the common legal structures are proprietorships, general partnerships, limited liability partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability companies, S corporations, and C corporations. Nonprofit corporations and unincorporated associations are common legal structures in states for non-profit organizations. Sometimes collective activities are also managed via trust deeds, in which case no specific legal entity is created. Instead, the trust deed is just a contract that governs the relationship between existing legal entities. Businesses and organizations typically default to certain types of legal structures if no paperwork is filed (such as proprietorships, general partnerships, and unincorporated associations). 

We must carefully distinguish between the foundational legal structure of a business or organization and other legal distinctions that might be applied to it in the context of laws and regulations. For instance, a **venture capital fund**—broadly speaking, a company which invests money pooled from shareholders into startup companies—is a distinct type of investment company within the laws and regulations that are relevant to the work of the **US Securities and Exchange Commission** (**SEC**).<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup> However, it is not a foundational legal structure in our sense discussed here. Rather, the foundational legal structure of a venture capital fund is typically a limited partnership.<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup>

The US state governments typically offer three main corporate structures: two for-profit corporate structures—the **S corporation** and the **C corporation**—and the **nonprofit corporation**. All three structures have a key document to support legal establishment, which describes the business or organization’s main aims and rights. The document is known by various names, but we will refer to it generally as the **articles of association** (also **articles of incorporation** or the **corporate charter**). You might view the articles of association as the constitution of a corporation. 

A key principle of any corporation is that it is a separate legal person, distinct from the owners, executives, and employees. In both C and S corporations, ownership is determined by the relative ownership of **shares**, and the holders of these shares are called **shareholders**. Newly issued shares are sold by the company to raise capital. The market for the sale of such shares is known as the **primary market**. Any shares bought on the primary market can, then, be bought and sold with various degrees of restrictions in the **secondary market**. Occasionally companies will purchase and sell their own shares on the secondary market.   

The C Corporation is available in all 50 states, though Delaware is the most popular choice due to well-developed and business-friendly corporate laws. The S Corporation is also available in most states. S Corporations are a less common choice than C Corporations by for-profit corporations because of various restrictions on S Corporations, including a limit of 100 shareholders. Shareholders of C and S corporations only risk the capital they utilized to purchase the shares, not their other personal assets (except in extreme cases such as fraud). Corporations, in other words, are governed by the principle of **limited liability**. 

C corporations can be both **privately** and **publicly held companies**. In the former case, the shares are not traded on any public stock exchange and the company is not required to disclose extensive information to the public. Selling shares in a privately held company typically comes with restrictions, such as the right of first refusal (which dictates that any shares for sale should be offered to existent shareholders first). With a public company, shares are instead traded on one or more public exchanges and it is required to file extensive information publicly with the SEC. Note that, as S corporations can only have a maximum of 100 owners, it is not practical for them to go public.

Just as a C and S corporation, a non-profit corporation is also considered a legal person. It does not, however, have shares or pay dividends to owners. Essentially, there are no owners. Any profits cannot be distributed and are only to be used by the organization to promote their mission. The organization is led by a board of directors who monitors an executive board that conducts day-to-day operations. Public charities, foundations, and other types of nonprofit organizations are often legally set up as nonprofit corporations. As we will see in **Section 3**, credit unions which are similar to commercial banks, are also non-profit corporations. 

Jurisdictions outside of the United States (certainly within developed economies) will also offer their citizens a palette of legal structures for starting a business or organization. There are certainly important differences between jurisdictions. That said, any jurisdiction will typically have one or more legal structures for setting up corporations, or at least legal structures that are very similar in nature. We will not split hairs here about the exact meaning of the term corporation. Instead, we can understand that, for a business or organization to be **incorporated**, in broad lines, has three implications.   

* It means that the business or organization has its own legal identity, one that is separate from the owners, directors, and employees. The expression to “incorporate” pertains, thus, to the creation of a new legal identity.  
* It means there is a clear legal distinction between those who own the company—a group that can include individuals as well as other legal entities—and those who direct it. 
* Finally, incorporation means that the owners’ personal assets are protected from the debts and legal issues a corporation faces (except in certain extreme circumstances such as fraud). The owners only risk the money they have invested into the purchase of stock (either in the primary or secondary market). They, in other words, have limited liability.  

While the details of legal structures vary within jurisdictions, most will have one or more legal structures that broadly align with incorporation as described above. And commercial banks, as stated, are typically limited to for-profit corporate structures. 

Exceptions do exist, of course. The English banks C. Hoare & Company and Coutts & Company both trace their origins to goldsmith bankers in the 17th century (1672 and 1692, respectively).<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> They are both private unlimited companies. While that means they are still legally separate entities from their owners, there is no limited liability for the owners, a key aspect of incorporation. We will learn more about the goldsmith bankers in **Chapter 2**. 
   
In the United States, practically all commercial banks are incorporated. They can receive their charters—a concept which we will explain shortly—from either a particular state or the federal government.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup>

Commercial banks that operate under a state charter are designated as **state banks**. Those that are members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by it and the relevant state authorities. State banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by the FDIC and the relevant state authorities. In September 2024, roughly 20% of state banks were also members of the Federal Reserve.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup> Most states require their banks to also be members of the FDIC. A state bank must obtain a branch charter from any state other than the one of its incorporation if it seeks to have operations there.  

State banks are often organized as C corporations or S corporations with the rights to operate as a bank (via a charter). Larger banks are mostly structured as C Corporations. Smaller community banks are also frequently structured as S corporations.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup> Certain states also allow commercial banks to be structured as limited liability companies (LLCs). State chartered commercial banks which are structured as an LLC can also participate in the deposit insurance program from the FDIC, as long as they have four corporate characteristics: perpetual succession, centralized management, limited liability, and free transferability of interests.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup> In some cases, an entity might have already been operating with different purposes before the organizers of the commercial bank seek to establish it as one. In other cases, the corporate entity will be created upon the founding of the commercial bank. 

State banks can only operate legally if they have a bank charter. But what exactly is a bank charter? Is it just a license?

Roughly speaking, a bank charter is a license. But strictly speaking, a bank charter is more encompassing. The expression “to charter a corporation” means to establish its legal existence with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. To “charter a bank” similarly means to establish the bank with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. The key documents which contain these goals and rights—the articles of association and sometimes additional certificates—are known as the **bank charter**, at least once approved by the proper government or legislative authorities. A physical certificate is commonly extended that serves as evidence of the bank charter.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup>

A license can be extended to a business or organization without requiring fundamental changes in its aims and rights. Hence, that commercial state banks have to be chartered, really indicates that a new sort of business is established, even if it is an existing corporation that is granted a bank charter. The bank chartering process is extensive. Any state corporation that is granted a charter becomes subject to a whole new set of regulatory and legal requirements—that is, the state’s and certain federal banking laws—next to those that apply merely to corporations.  

Commercial banks can also be granted charters at the federal level from the **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency** (**OCC**), an independent bureau within the US Department of the Treasury. Commercial banks that are chartered and regulated by the OCC are called **national banks**. They are easily recognizable because they must include in their name the designation “national”, “national association”, or “N.A.” Commercial banks with a national bank charter are required to be members of the FDIC and the Federal Reserve. Unlike state banks, national banks are allowed to operate anywhere in the United States without requiring additional branch charters.

The national chartering process for commercial banks contains two main stages: the preliminary approval stage and the organization phase. The former is basically an approval of the main plans behind the new national bank, while the latter is the phase in which the national bank is actually organized (e.g., establish articles of association and an organization certificate, raise capital, develop policies and procedures, hire a number of executive officers). The OCC grants or rejects final approval at the end of the organization phase.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup>

An intriguing aspect to national banks is that they are chartered in the deepest sense of the word. National banks are not just state-established corporations with a federal license. They are actually a special type of federal corporation.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup> While their corporate form resembles that of the C corporate forms at the level of the states, it is a unique type of entity with special rules and regulations. Therefore, you cannot not just turn an existing corporation created at the state level into a national bank. You need to create a new type of legal entity at the federal level.   

Besides commercial banks, the OCC can also charter, in the full sense of the term, several other related types of for-profit corporations (including savings associations, industrial loan companies, special purpose banks, and others). Some of these entities are also officially called “national banks”. These chartering powers for the OCC are fairly extraordinary. 

To start, the legal establishment of businesses and organizations is largely the prerogative of the individual states. While many types of companies and organizations are *licensed* at a federal level—such as stock exchanges and investment companies—they are not usually “chartered,” in the full sense of the word, at the federal level. Additionally, while the federal government does occasionally charter, in the full sense of the word, for-profit enterprises, this occurs by special legislative acts in all other cases than those cases that fall under the scope of the OCC. The OCC is, thus, the only federal agency that has the prerogative to legally create for-profit enterprises.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup>

National banks are primarily governed by the federal law on national banks and administrative rulings by the OCC, the foundation of which rests with the **National Banking Act of 1864**.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup> That said, many of the laws and regulations mimic state practices in corporate law. National banks are also subject to federal judicial rulings, though at the end of the 20th century, the US Supreme Court decided to defer to the states on the general standard of care for the directors of national banks.<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup>

Regardless of the law, incorporation has substantial advantages for a commercial bank. Without limited liability, running a bank is an extremely risky venture. A bank that is incorporated also offers more stability and continuity than, say, a partnership. Historical experience in England and Wales certainly attests to the advantages of incorporation. 

In 1697, the Bank of England was given the exclusive monopoly right to act as an incorporated bank.<sup>[18](#footnote18)</sup> That, however, still granted a lot of room for banks to operate as partnerships. So, in 1709, all partnership banks were prohibited from issuing bank notes in England and Wales, if they had more than six partners.<sup>[19](#footnote19)</sup> The purpose was to stymie the competition from unincorporated competitor banks. These measures granted the Bank of England a practical monopoly on the issuance of bank notes, the primary activity of commercial banks at the time. 

These privileges were granted to the Bank of England in exchange for government access to credit from it.<sup>[20](#footnote20)</sup> While that may sound repugnant and corrupt to more enlightened modern minds, such *quids pro quo* were hardly uncommon in pre-modern society. So-called merchant bankers, for example, who specialized in both trade and finance, dominated the European commercial and financial landscape from the late middle ages to the early modern era. They were the principal lenders to sovereigns. As Kohn notes,

> The profitability of sovereign lending did not, however, derive solely or even principally from the economies of intermediation. The main reward, especially for large loans, generally came in the form of favors the government granted the merchant bank in return for its lending–commercial concessions, monopolies, and tax farms.<sup>[21](#footnote21)</sup>  

The restriction on incorporated banks and the six-partner rule were both lifted in 1826 for banks operating outside of a 65 mile radius around London.<sup>[22](#footnote22)</sup> The number of incorporated country banks then, unsurprisingly, increased quickly. There were already thirty-three by 1833.<sup>[23](#footnote23)</sup> As Temin and Voth conclude, “The very speed with which many of these country banks switched their business to the joint-stock model as soon as they were allowed to is eloquent testimony to the limitations of the earlier corporate form”.<sup>[24](#footnote24)</sup>

A key aspect to modern banking is that most commercial banks are actually not independent companies. Instead, they are usually a member of a **banking group**: a collection of banks and, often, other financial companies that are connected in their ownership. 

The legal organization behind a banking group typically consist of both operational and holding companies. An **operational company** is just what we would standardly understand as a company, one that actively engages in the production, distribution, and sale of goods and services. We might describe a **holding company** as follows:

* It does not have any real business operations of its own. Instead it holds stakes in other companies. These are frequently majority stakes, but they can also be significant minority stakes. 
* The holding company is not usually involved in the day-to-day operations of the companies they own. That is the responsibility of the company’s executives and employees. However, the holding company will typically exert a lot of influence through the company’s board of directors and shareholder meetings. The influence here is mainly on a strategic level. 
* The holding company’s main goal is to profit from their holdings of companies through dividends, and companies are usually owned for a very long time. The holding company will, of course, also hold assets other than stocks in companies, such as bonds, cash, and so on. 

When an operating or holding company has a majority stake in a particular company, the latter is usually called a **subsidiary**. The owner is then often called the **parent company**. A company that is owned entirely by another operating or holding company is sometimes also called a **full subsidiary**. 

Most commercial banks are legally, thus, but a node in a network of operating and holding companies. At the top of the network standardly sits a single holding company. The existence of banking groups necessitates additional laws and regulations. These govern, for instance, the types of activities that can take place between a commercial bank and other banks or financial companies within the same banking group.<sup>[25](#footnote25)</sup>

While banking groups are typically centered around a main brand, they often contain multiple brands. For example, HSBC holdings—the top level holding company for the HSBC group—also owns the Hang Seng Bank in Hong Kong (which, in turn, owns Hang Seng Bank in China) as well as the Saudi Awwal Bank. For another example, Citigroup—the top level holding company which also owns Citibank in the United States—also owns the Banco Nacional de México, one of the largest banks in Mexico.  

While the choices of the legal organizational structure of a banking group can be motivated by various concerns—including the separation of business activities and the management of separate brands—it is often motivated by geographical and jurisdictional considerations. In this manner, for instance, HSBC holdings ultimately owns a variety of companies involved in banking and financial services, including HSBC Bank Mexico, HSBC Bank USA, HSBC Securities USA, HSBC Bank Egypt, and so on. Citigroup, in turn, owns, amongst many other subsidiaries, Citibank Canada, Citibank Japan, and Citibank, N.A., the national bank headquartered in New York City. 

Banking groups organized via a top-level holding company are not exactly a modern invention. As has been well-documented by the Belgian-American historian Raymond de Roover, the Medici banking house can be credited as the earliest precursor of managing their operations through a network of subsidiaries with a “holding company” in Florence sitting at the top.<sup>[26](#footnote26)</sup>

The Medicis were **merchant bankers**: international traders who also specialized in financial services. Merchant bankers were key financial intermediaries in the Europe of the **late Middle Ages** (1300–1500) and the **early modern period** (ca. 1500 – 1800). They also marked the society and economy of Colonial America. We will return to the fascinating history of the merchant bankers, including the Medici, in the next chapter. For now, we will wrap up this chapter by exploring the other deposit banks that mark the American banking landscape.  


## 3. Deposit banking 

In *Section 1*, we introduced the notion of a deposit bank. We stated that it is any institution, which accepts deposits from the public into credit money accounts; facilitates payments with that credit money, including in the form of credit money transfers; makes loans to consumers and businesses; and affects the money supply, primarily through lending. 

Commercial banks are by far the most important, but not the only, modern-day institution to practice **deposit banking** or **banking**. In the United States, the two main other types of deposit banks are **savings associations** (also known as **savings banks**, **thrifts**, or **savings and loans**) and **credit unions**.

Savings associations are very similar to prototypical commercial banks, but at least traditionally focus much more actively on products and services for savings, and not as much on checking and payment products. Historically, they have also focused mostly on extending retail mortgages, rather than business loans. Many other countries have financial institutions that are similarly focused, at least historically, on offering products and services for savings, such as **building societies** in the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand; and **sparkassen** and **spaarbanken** in Germany and the Netherlands, respectively.  

Many savings associations in the United States are just standard for-profit corporations owned by shareholders. Sometimes, however, savings associations are for-profit corporations that are **mutually owned**, meaning that shares can only be owned by those involved with the savings association (e.g., as account holders, borrowers). Savings associations can also have a hybrid model of ownership. While savings associations do receive their own charters—either nationally by the OCC or from a state agency—they are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks.<sup>[27](#footnote27)</sup>

Next, **credit unions** offer similar services to commercial banks and savings associations, but are formed as non-profit corporations. That is, profits are not distributed to shareholders that own the bank, but instead are utilized to improve or decrease the costs of the products and services offered. Typically these institutions are mutually owned by account holders. Rather than owning shares, however, account holders are members of the non-profit corporation with voting and other governance rights. These rights are typically independent of the financial stakes involved. Credit unions are normally focused on retail and small business clients. 

While savings associations are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks on the national level, credit unions are actually regulated and chartered by the **National Credit Union Administration** (**NCUA**). At the state level, credit unions are sometimes chartered and regulated by the same agency as banks and savings associations. Sometimes, however, they also have their own regulatory agency. Credit unions cannot be FDIC-insured. Instead, the NCUA manages a special insurance fund for credit unions called the **National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund**. 

As with savings associations, we can find institutions that are similar to the American credit unions in other countries. The building societies of the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand that were mentioned earlier are actually very much like credit unions as they are also non-profit. In Ireland, the same types of institutions are standardly called **mutual banks**. So-called **kredietbanken** in the Netherlands tend to be non-profit institutions, mutually owned by entrepreneurs which offer bank services and loans to other entrepreneurs. 

Many common distinctions further clarify the nature of commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions. With a **community bank**, for example, we typically mean a commercial bank that has a local focus, and we similarly often speak of a **community credit union** or a **community savings association**. For another example, any commercial bank that only services other commercial banks and savings associations as well as their officers, directors, and employees is commonly known as a **banker’s bank**. A **corporate credit union** (also a **central credit union**) is, in turn, one that only similarly services other credit unions. 

Besides commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions, there are some more exotic entities in the United States that meet, or nearly meet, the characteristics of deposit banks. 

An **industrial loan company** (**ILC**), for example, can offer really all the same services as a commercial bank, including checking and savings accounts, though they cannot guarantee that deposits will be made available within seven days (even if in practice funds are often available sooner). They can only be chartered by certain states—and not at the federal level—and are subject to different regulatory regimes than commercial banks and savings associations. For example, ILCs can also be subsidiaries of nonfinancial companies. In this way, for example, both Toyota and BMW operate banks in the United States called the Toyota Financial Savings Bank and the BMW Bank of North America.<sup>[28](#footnote28)</sup>

In sum, the main institutions in the United States that qualify as deposit banks are commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions. However, there are various other, more exotic types of institutions that meet, or nearly meet, the standard characteristics of a deposit bank. Importantly, while we loosely refer to these institutions as deposit banks, banks, or banks and credit unions,<sup>[29](#footnote29)</sup> various of those institutions, like savings associations and ILCs, are, in a legal sense, technically not always defined as “banks”. 

Many institutions in the United States might carry the label “bank” in the name, but are not really deposit banks as just described. In fact, they sometimes are fairly far removed from that notion. 

Consider, for instance, **investment banks**. These are institutions which have among their core activities helping companies and governments with raising capital (through stock and bond issuances); providing advice on mergers, acquisitions, and corporate restructurings; and helping manage the investment portfolios of clients such as pension funds, hedge funds, family offices, and wealthy individuals. Typically, these institutions would not provide checking and savings accounts or payment products. They also do not impact the money supply in the way of banks and credit unions. So, investment banks are not really “banks” in our sense above, even though they may be part of a larger brand that also includes one or more entities occupied with commercial banking. 

In some cases, making sense of the label “bank” will be a fairly complicated exercise. A common distinction that can bring such complications is that of a **private bank**. 

Sometimes an entity called a private bank is just as commercial bank that is privately owned (i.e., the stock is not traded on a public exchange). Often, however, the term also refers to for-profit companies that are specialized in offering wealth management services to high net-worth individuals and families, a service known as **private banking**. 

The possible services that are provided by private banks in the latter sense can vary widely and may include checking and savings account services, advice on investments and taxes, estate planning, custody services, high-value mortgages, and so on. In some cases, such private banks are just specialized commercial banks. But in other cases, these “banks” are really somewhat different types of institutions. 

Many other countries and regions will have deposit banks that show similarities to those in the United States, particularly in the developed economies. We have already shown that most countries have their own version of commercial banks, and that many countries have institutions similar to savings associations and credit unions. It should be re-iterated, however, that all countries and regions will have their own idiosyncrasies and features. And in some cases, the landscape can look substantially different. 

At least one notable difference with the United States and many other countries is the role of public banks. A **public bank** is one where the government owns the majority of the shares. The United States certainly has such banks, such as the Bank of North Dakota and various banks owned by native American tribal governments. But they are rare. By contrast, in European countries, public banks are more common. The sparkassen in Germany and the cantonal banks in Switzerland, for example, play a substantial role in their banking sectors. Even more extreme is the landscape in China. The four large banks that dominate its financial sector—the Bank of China, the China Construction Bank, the Agricultural Bank of China, and the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China—are predominantly state-owned.
