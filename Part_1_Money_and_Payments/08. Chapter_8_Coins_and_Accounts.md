# 8. Coins and accounts

Coinage and accounting are inescapable subject matters in a history of
money, payments, and banking. They are also required background
specifically to better understand the activities of the merchant and
deposit banks that arose in the High Middle Ages.

In this chapter we first introduce some key notions and a limited
history regarding accounting. *Section 1* discusses the origins of
accounting. In *Section 2*, we cover the most significant aspect of
modern accounting and its history: the technique of double entry.
*Section 3* explores pre-modern moneys of account. In the last two
sections of this chapter, we pull together all the information on
coinage and accounting. *Section 4* discusses the legal, market, and
intrinsic value of coins. In *Section 4*, we offer some reflections on
the bimetallic and trimetallic monetary systems that marked Europe until
the 1800s.

## 1. The origins of accounting

The world's earliest known accounting system originates from the land
surrounding the Tigris and the Euphrates rivers, an area commonly known
as Mesopotamia.[^1] The system consisted of clay tokens of various
shapes and sizes, including spheres, discs, rectangles, cones, and so
on. They seem to have been used for counting, recording, and
communicating about commodities. Each token represented either a certain
type of commodity such as jar of oil, a sheep, or a trussed duck, or a
commodity in a certain quantity such as a bushel of grain. The earliest
of these accounting tokens date to around 8,000 B.C., that is, about
7,500 years before the first coins were ever minted in Ionia and Lydia.
For thousands of years these accounting tokens were employed all
throughout the Near East within the context of village and agricultural
life.

From the mid-fourth millenium BC onwards, city states led by
priest-rulers developed in Sumer, a region in southern Mesopotomia that
bordered the Persian Gulf. They included cities such as Uruk, Ur, and
Nippur. The quantity and complexity of the primitive accounting tokens
increased substantially during this period. Around 3,250 B.C., the
Sumerians began placing the tokens into certain clay receptibles, known
as *bullae*, making impressions of the tokens—either directly or with
a stick or stylus—on the outside to indicate their contents.[^2]
Shortly after, the Sumerians transitioned to recording economic
transactions and other administrative data on clay tablets. Many of the
key markings in that early clay tablet system clearly resemble the
shapes of the accounting tokens.

The initial pictographic and ideographic markings on the clay tablets
then developed over hundreds of years, and eventually the Sumerians
could capture their entire spoken language with a system of markings.
This end-product was, in other words, the world's first writing system
and it originated from the need for economic and administrative records.
The arrival of this writing system is generally dated within the first
centuries of the Early Dynastic Period—the historical period of
Mesopotamian civilization from 2,900 B.C. to 2,350 B.C. Notably, the
need for economic and administrative records also seems to have played a
key role in the development of the Egyptian hieroglyphic writing system
which developed independently at around the same time.[^3]

The Sumerian writing system is classified as cuneiform script due to its
wedge-shaped markings ("cuneus" is Latin for "wedge"). The Sumerian
language has no known relationship with any other languages, both
existent and non-existent. Yet, other civilizations in Mesopotamia and
the Near East were able to adopt the cuneiform script for their own
languages, including first the Akkadians and later the Babylonians and
Assyrians, all of whom spoke early Semitic languages.[^4] Hundreds of
thousands of tablets from these early Mesopotamian civilizations have
been uncovered and studied since the nineteenth century by archeologists
and Assyriologists.[^5] At least in terms of written records, we known
much more about the Ancient Mesopotamians than the Egyptians who used
papyrus—which is much more fragile and perishable than clay
tablets—for writing.

The early Sumerian city-states were led by religious authorities, and
both economic and political life centered around temples.[^6] Over time,
the role of temples in the political and economic order was in large
part displaced by the increasing importance of kings and palaces. These
temples and palaces rented out land and equipment; received gifts, fees,
taxes, and the spoils from war; supported craftsmen with rations and raw
materials; made loans; coordinated and financed foreign trade; and
settled commercial disputes. They were very much financial centers as
well as governmental centers that often involved hundreds or thousands
of people. Unlike modern governments, temples and palaces were
standardly creditors rather than debtors.

Entrepreneurs also came to have a substantial place in the Ancient
Mesopotamian societies, so that it is inaccurate to say that they were
entirely centrally planned.[^7] Nevertheless, they certainly appear more
centrally planned than modern Western societies. And certainly in the
early days of the Bronze Age, it, therefore, seems to have been the
temples and palaces that especially propelled forward economic
specialization and the division of labor in Mesopotamian civilizations.

All recovered early clay tablets involved economic and administrative
transactions. In many cases, the tablets recorded simple receipts,
bills, or transfers. In other cases, the tablets were more complex and
summarized many transactions.[^8] Clay tablets only started to be used
outside of the scope of economic and administrative transactions
hundreds of years later, but eventually extended to epitaphs, laws,
annals, religious rites, hymns, poems, and other literary works. The
oldest recorded literary works, for example, stem from around 2,600 BC.
The oldest recovered recordings of the well-known epic about the King of
Uruk, *The Epic of Gilgamish*, date from the beginning of the second
millenium, though only some of the tablets have been recovered.

It is in the Mesopotamian economic and political order dominated by
temples and palaces that particularly silver developed as a standard
money of account.[^9] A money of account is a measurement device for
expressing economic value in a recognizable and convenient manner with
regards to bookkeeping, contracts, taxes and fees, receipts, and prices.
So to say that silver was the primary money of account merely meant that
various weights of silver at a certain fineness were utilized to express
economic value.

The need for moneys of account in Ancient Sumer should not be
surprising. Temple and palace complexes were huge operations. To make
them administratively efficient and transparant, the Sumerians required
ways to compare the values involved in different economic transactions.
So, to address that need, temples and palaces published lists of
exchange rates for common commodities they would accept as payment,
including silver pieces, copper, barley, and wool. For instance, early
on, a bushel of barley, commonly known as a gur, was set equal to one
shekel, or about eight grams worth of silver. While values might be
expressed in one commodity such as for a loan or rent, the system of
exchange rates generally allowed payment in any common commodity.
Supposedly, these official rates were stable over long periods of time.

Silver became the most common way to express value over time, likely as
a result of its common use in jewelry, religious objects, and trade. To
assure the soundness of the system, the rulers of the Ancient
Mesopotamian cities enforced various standards. To weigh silver pieces,
they introduced a sexagesimal system consisting of shekels, minas, and
talents. Sixty shekels equalled one mina; sixty minas, in turn, equalled
one talent. The standard of fineness commonly became 87.5% in the third
millenium BC. Temples and palaces became specialized in refining silver
to oversee its purity and weight and exerted efforts into combatting
fraud with regards to weights and fineness. The introduction of coinage
in the seventh century BC was, in this way, a fairly minor step in the
evolution of money.

Temples notably maintained their role in refining metals and
metal-working until classical Greece and Rome. In Classical Athens, the
mint was located in a temple dedicated to Theseus. Rome's silver and
gold coins, in turn, were struck and stored during the Punic Wars in the
Temple Juno Moneta, dedicated to the goddess Juno. She received the
epithet "Moneta", or "warner," supposedly after cackling geese
surrounded the temple to alert of an invasion by the Gauls in 390 BC.
The later use of the Latin "moneta" to mean money derrives from the name
of this Roman temple. The English terms "money" and "mint", in turn,
derrive from this later meaning of the Latin *moneta*.[^10]

While silver was predominant as a money of account, it was not all that
frequently utilized in actual transactions. First, in these early
Mesopotamian civilizations most economic transactions were handled by
running debt balances that were only settled periodically. Ale houses,
for instance, were typically owned and supplied by temples and palaces.
Patrons apparantly just ran up their tabs, only settling them at harvest
time. Second, while debts, fees, taxes, and rents were often expressed
in silver, they could also be paid through other commodities, hence why
the official exchange rates maintained by temples and palaces were so
important.[^11] Finally, many contracts continued to be specified and
paid in other types of commodity moneys.[^12]

The temple and palace centered systems of exchange, therefore, propelled
forwards certain commodity moneys. Over time, these commodity moneys,
particularly silver, also became increasingly common and standardized in
private economic spheres as means of expressing value, making payments,
and storing value.

## 2. Double entry accounting

Many cultural and economic practices from the Near East eventually made
their way into pre-modern Europe. The core decision-making body of
Classical Athenian democracy was the Assembly (*ekklesia*). While
Athenian females, foreign residents, and slaves were excluded, it was
open to all male citizens from twenty years of age after their military
training.[^13] The Assembly was, however, hardly an Athenian invention,
and such institutions actually were common in the Near East much
earlier, probably introduced into Greece by the Phoenicians.[^14] The
idea of commercial loans with interest was probably also introduced to
Greece by the Phoenicians in the eighth century BC.[^15] While we will
not trace all the developments from Ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt
onwards, certainly the roots of pre-modern European accounting lie with
the advances made by these civilizations.

Perhaps the single biggest innovation in the history of accounting and
one that was almost certainly made by Europeans, specifically northern
Italians, is the technique of double entry.[^16] Let's first turn to
explaining the notion of double entry accounting and then briefly trace
it's history.

The basic building block of accounting is the **account**. Any
particular company might have, depending on the size of the company,
anywhere from a dozen to hundreds of acounts. Any type of transaction
the company makes—a purchase of raw materials or equipment, obtaining
a loan, making a tax payment, and so on—has to be reflected within
these accounts. The particular accounts companies utilize for organizing
their financial statements will depend on the nature of their business,
company size, regulations, and other factors.

Suppose, for example, that Random Bytes is a small company that advises
local businesses on software packages and helps them implement any
recommendations. Random Bytes might have some accounts to keep track of
their costs and revenues, including "Website costs", "travel costs",
"wages", "Revenues from consulting", and "Revenues from subscriptions".
They will also have accounts that reflect the assets they own such as
"Office building", "Bank deposits", as well as any outstanding
liabilities such as "Bank loans" and "VAT Payable".

The key record that contains all company accounts is known as the
**accounting ledger**. A key principle in modern **double-entry
accounting** is that changes in its accounts are tracked by splitting
them into two columns: **debits** and **credits**. These terms are here
just the names for the left and the right columns of accounts,
respectively, and should not be conflated with the general notions of
debt and credit. The central rule to double entry accounting is, then,
that any type of transaction made by the company or organization
minimally requires one entry on the debits column of an account and one
entry on a credits column of a different account. In some cases, more
than just two entries may be required. In any case, for any transaction,
the total value of the debit entries made should equal the total value
of the credit entries made.

Standardly, all transactions for a company or organization are first
recorded in an **accounting journal** as they occur, including all
relevant information such as the accounts to be credited and debited, a
date, a reference number, and so on. Only, then, are they posted to the
accounting ledger. Historically, companies generally updated the
accounting ledger only periodically. With modern advances in computing,
however, the accounting ledger is often continuously updated with
transactions from the journal.

Let's illustrate the main idea behind double entry accounting through an
example. Suppose that Random Bytes, the software company mentioned
above, applies for a \$10,000 loan from City Bank. They are approved for
the loan and receive the money in their bank account. Suppose further
that the loan is repaid over three years on a monthly basis out of the
same bank account, and that some principal and interest is contained in
each payment. Let's also assume that the company only deals with City
Bank, as it is fairly small. On the basis of that description of the
situation, they could reasonably decide to register the transactions in
the following manner:

- For the initial receipt of the loan, debit the "Bank deposits" account
  for \$10,000 (which in this case means an increase in the value of the
  account), and credit the "Bank loans" account by \$10,000 (which in
  this case means an increase in the company's total liabilities).

- Each month, the loan payment comes out of the "Bank deposits" account,
  so that account needs to be credited (that is, the value of it
  decreases). The principal part of the payment is, then, debited to the
  "Bank loan" account (meaning the overall liability decreases). The
  "Interest expenses" account is, then, debited for the interest part of
  the payment (meaning the overall account increases in value).

The language of debits and credits is often a bit confusing for
newcomers to accounting, but it all comes down to the accounting
equation introduced in *Chapter 2*. With any transaction, the total
debits and credits entered must be of equal value. Debit entries for
assets can be reflected by corresponding credit entries for other
assets. In this way, for example, adding a new computer to a company
might be compensated by a credit entry on "Bank deposits", if the
computer was paid for in that manner. In other cases, however, debit
entries for assets are reflected by corresponding credit entries among
liabilities or equity capital. A credit to a liability or equity capital
account, in other words, increases the value of total liabilities or
equity capital, respectively. Any decrease of liabilities or equity
capital subsequently has to be reflected through credit entries.

In *Chapter 2*, we also introduced the notion of a financial statement,
the balance sheet and the income statement being the most important
ones. These reports are really outputs that can be produced from a
properly maintained accounting journal and ledger. The income statement
is merely a statement of the profit or loss that has resulted from
combining all the cost and revenue accounts over a certain period. The
profit or loss is, then, added to the equity capital of the company (and
possibly distributed as dividends in a subsequent transaction). After
recording the profit or loss of a certain period in this way, a company
can draw up a balance sheet by incorporating all the debit and credit
entries from the period of the remaining accounts.

The accounting equation demands that the total value of assets balance
out with the total value of liabilities and equity capital. When people
in finance and accounting speak about "balancing the books", they mean
precisely the drawing up of a balance sheet at the end of the period
where the value of all assets is required to equal the value of the
liabilities plus equity capital.

While it is difficult to fully convey all the details of double entry
accounting and its importance in our limited discussion, the key insight
here is that modern double entry accounting much more robustly prevents
errors and subsequent (legal) conflicts than more primitive systems.

Double entry accounting received its first systematic written treatment
in 1494. This contribution came from from **Luca Pacioli** (1447–1517),
also known as Fra Luca Bartolomeo de Pacioli, an Italian mathematician.
Unusually for his time, Pacioli had expertise both in the more abstract
mathematics taught at universities—with Greek and Arabic origins—as
well as the practical mathematics used by and taught to merchants.
Pacioli was well-integrated into Italy's circle of intellectuals,
artists, and aristrocrats of the time, and notably had a close personal
and working relationship with Leonardo Da Vinci.

Pacioli's contribution to double entry accounting is found as a chapter
in his *Summa de Arithmetica, Geometria, Proportioni et
Proportionalità*, and was entitled *Particularis de Computis et
Scripturis*. Unlike the Latin title might suggest, the *Summa* was
notable for being written in Italian, as common practice throughout the
Middle Ages had been to write scholarly texts in Latin.

While Pacioli was the first to offer a systematic treatment of double
entry accounting, it is important to note that he was only describing
practices that had existed for much longer. Double entry accounting was
almost certainly invented by Italians in the High Middle Ages. The
evidence suggests that it probably arose at the regional fairs of
northern Italy during the twelfth century.[^17] Just as at the Fairs of
Champagne, these fairs had certain proprietors responsible for currency
exchange as well as deposit banking services. By extending credit
through overdraft facilities and assuming the risks associated with
credit transactions away from the merchants, they reduced their risks
through sound record keeping. These bankers probably also traded goods
at the fairs themselves using funds they had received from
merchants.[^18]

Double entry accounting would have also diffused among merchants, though
more slowly than among the deposit bankers. Among the major Italian
merchants from Genoa, Venice, Florence, and elsewhere, knowledge of
double entry accounting steadily emerged in the thirteenth century.[^19]
Suggestively, by the middle of the fourteenth century, double entry
accounting was utilized by local merchants, local banks, craftsmen, and
households in Florence.[^20] Double-entry accounting is sometimes also
known as the **Venetian method**, its development being particularly
influenced by Venetian merchants. Nevertheless, the historical record
suggests, though perhaps only weakly, that double entry accounting was
actually first practised by Florentines.[^21]

While double entry accounting diffused among the Italian deposit bankers
and merchants in the High and Late Middle Ages, we only have very
limited evidence that shows the use of double entry accounting by
non-Italians before 1500.[^22] De Roover strongly believed that
diffusion to other European countries only really began in the sixteenth
century and that it was greatly influenced by Pacioli's treatise.[^23]
That claim is certainly consistent with the clear Italian dominance in
international trade throughout the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries,
which would have been enabled through superior business and accounting
practices.

Pacioli's exposition of double entry accounting was well-received and
copies of it circulated throughout Europe in multiple languages. Many
subsequent publications on accounting all relied on Pacioli's treatise.
While the popularity of double entry accounting before 1800 has often
been downplayed by accounting historians, Sangster has recently argued
that the picture between 1500 and 1800 (as well as before 1500) is one
of steady diffusion.[^24] That said, adoption was certainly strongly
stimulated by nineteenth century advances in corporate law. The English
*Joint Stock Companies Act 1844*, for instance,[^25] required all
registered companies to keep proper books and release a balance sheet
annually for viewing by shareholders.[^26] The English *Companies Act
1900* introduced a general legal mandate for the independent auditing of
corporations.[^27]

## 3. Pre-modern moneys of account

All coins from the High Middle Ages to the modern period functioned as
media of exchange and stores of value (though some obviously did so
better than others). But only some coins became the basis for what are
known as moneys of account. As already mentioned, a money of account is
simply a measurement device for expressing economic value in a
recognizable and convenient manner with regards to bookkeeping,
contracts, taxes and fees, receipts, and prices. Any financial
obligation stated in a money of account in pre-modern times could, then,
be made by one or a combination of circulating coins.

While historians have been long puzzled over the nature of pre-modern
moneys of account, it seems that most commonly a money of account from
the High Middle Ages to the modern period was just anchored in an
actively circulating coin.[^28] To illustrate the operating principles
behind a money of account, let's briefly turn to the monetary system of
the Burgundian Netherlands in the fifteenth century as an example.[^29]

During the High Middle Ages, the Low Countries comprised a patchwork of
duchies, counties, and other semi-independent territorial entities.
Formally, though typically not in practice, the rulers of many of these
areas were vassals to the Holy Roman Emperor. The Count of Flanders,
notably, was a vassal to the King of France. Some territories, like
Friesland, were not a fief, but an "independent" territory.

In 1369, then, a large part of the Low Countries as well as some
adjacent territories came under the control of the House of
Valois–Burgundy—a cadet branch of the House of Valois that started in
1363 when King John II of France gave the Duchy of Burgundy to his
youngest son, Philip the Bold (1342–1404)—when Philip married
Margaret III of Flanders. Over time, the Burgundian State—the name for
the Duchy of Burgundy and the other territories under its control—was
able to expand its sphere of control over the Low Countries through
conquest, land purchases, diplomacy, and inheritance. The area
collectively became known as the Burgundian Netherlands. The patchwork
of territories varied in their customs, institutions, and laws, but with
time Burgundian control over the region did become more centralized.

A key step in that process was monetary harmonzation, starting in the
early fifteenth century, particularly from the efforts of Philip the
Good (1396–1467). Through official mints, the Burgundians introduced
more uniformity in the native coinage. While diverse smaller
coins—often with very low silver content—continued to be minted in
the individual territories, the Burgundians issued a few gold coins and
various larger "silver" coins that varied substantially in their
fineness. While probably a few handfuls of older native and foreign
coins continued to circulate, our limited evidence from hoards does seem
to indicate that the Burgundian coins became increasingly predominant.

The Flemish groat was a coin first minted in Flanders in the early
fourteenth century, being just the county's version of the larger silver
coins that had become popular in the thirteenth century. Due to the
economic influence of Flanders, this particular coin had already become
quite common in the Low Countries by the early fourteenth century. So
the Burgundians, naturally, made it the main official coin for the
Burgundian Netherlands. As a consequence, two moneys of account based on
the Flemish groat also increasingly became more common: the pound groat
and the pound of forty groats.

The pound groat was just equivalent to 240 groats. The pound of forty
groats was, instead, as implied by the name, equal to forty groats. So
an amount such as "3 li. 6 s. of 40 gr. fl." should be read as "three
pounds and six shillings of forty groats flemish". In total, this would
have been equal to 132 groats. The pound groat system was
straightforwardly based on the Flemish groat, as one pence was
equivalent to one groat coin. The pound of forty groats system was
somewhat less straightforward, as the pence unit was defined as
one-sixth the value of a Flemish groat coin. Both moneys of account,
however, were based on an actual circulating coin.

So, values in financial transactions and account books were increasingly
expressed in these two moneys of account in the Burgundian Netherlands,
particularly in the pound of forty groats. And any price could be
honored by the official gold and silver Burgundian coins; other native
coins, including those no longer being minted; and various foreign
coins. So while dozens of coins circulated in the Burgundian
Netherlands, only two moneys of account were common, certainly later in
the fifteenth century.

The discussion above gives a more precise impression of the notion of a
pre-modern money of account. It was typically just a measurement system
with one or more denominations based on a particular coin. Often the
denominations were expressed as pounds, shillings, and pence, but other
systems were also in use. One coin could form the basis for multiple
measurement systems, as was the case with the pound groat and the pound
of forty groats.

Just like in the Burgundian Netherlands, actual circulating silver coins
were frequently the basis for a money of account elsewhere. In the
Middle Ages, this was commonly the circulating penny. In this way,
common moneys of account were the livre tournois, the livre parisis, the
lira genoese, and the pound sterling. The livre parisis, for example,
had been, due to French influence, the predominant money of account in
Flanders during the High Middle Ages. It continued to be a money of
account into the fourteenth century, even as it was becoming
increasingly replaced by the pound groat and the pound of forty
groats.[^30] Similar transformations occurred throughout other parts of
Europe with the introduction of groats.[^31]

Though less common, gold coins were sometimes also the basis for a money
of account. This was the case in Florence from the late thirteenth
century onwards, where the gold florin coin became the basis for the
"florin", the money of account. Each florin consisted of 29 shillings
and 384 pennies. While the largest unit, the florin, corresponded to an
actual coin, the subunits did not correspond to any actual circulating
coins.[^32] This was, thus, the exact opposite of the pound groat
system. Notably, while moneys of account based on gold coins existed,
the value of circulating coins, whether gold or silver, were always
expressed in a silver-based money of account.[^33]

While most moneys of account had multiple denominations, in some cases
they seem to have had only one denomination. In the second half of
fourteenth century Deventer and Arnhem, for example, various gold coins,
even after they ceased to circulate, became the names of moneys of
account. They, according to city records, were utilized for certain
types of accounting entries, including loans to the city, military
expenditures, and particularly in relation to gifts. While not obvious
from their names, their values were actually defined as certain
multiples of the circulating Flemish groat. In this way, for example,
the *Brugse schild* or *schild* became the equivalent of 24 Flemish
groats. These moneys of account, however, were not expressed in
denominations such as pounds, shillings, and pence, and just seem to
have been simple counters.[^34]

In some cases, moneys of account were based on the silver or gold value
of a coin that was no longer in circulation. The gros tournois, a silver
coin first struck by Louis IX in 1266, had first been struck at 4.220
grams of weight with 4.044 grams of pure silver. While it ceased to be
minted in the early fourteenth century, the gros tournois was long
afterwards used as a money of account, even after it had gone out of
circulation. In places like Brabant and Flanders, it became a money of
account known as the "pound of old groats" ("pond oude groot" or "livre
de vieil gros"). Supposedly, the wages of Flemish officials were
expressed as gros tournois as late as the 1580s. It seems that, at least
for some time, the gros tournois was thus utilized as a money of account
without any relation to an existing coin.[^35]

The names of coins were sometimes just adopted within existing money of
account systems to expand on the available denominations. The "florin",
for example, became defined in fifteenth century Milan at a value of 384
times the circulating penny. The "florin" here was not like in Florence
a distinct money of account, instead it was just the name for a new
denomination for a money of account based on the circulating penny.
Similarly, in Genoa the "florin" was defined as 300 times the
circulating penny. The "ducat," in turn, was defined as 124 shillings of
the circulating penny in sixteenth century Venice.[^36]

The names of coins were sometimes also adopted to replace the names of
existing denominations. An example is the case of the *stuiver* and
*gulden* in the Burgundian Netherlands. The *stuiver* was a silver coin
that was for long periods equivalent to two Flemish groats. In the
1450s, the rhinegulden—a gold coin modelled after the florin—was
briefly valued at forty groats. As a consequence, the pound of forty
groats increasingly became called the rhinegulden or *gulden* and a
shilling in this system increasingly a *stuiver*. Hence, nothing really
changed about the pound of forty groats system other than its name and
the names ascribed to certain denominations.

The use of the names of coins in book keeping and contracts could become
very confusing, at least to the modern observer. In the example above,
for instance, the relative value ratios of the rhinegulden, stuiver, and
Flemish groat coins shifted. Hence, the ratios inherent to denominations
of the gulden money of account, still based on the Flemish groat, began
to diverge from the exchange rates between the gulden, stuiver, and
groat coins. During the regency of Maximilian I for the Philip the Fair
(1482–1494), for example, the stuiver coin came to have an exchange
rate of six groats; as a unit in the gulden money of account, however,
the stuiver was still defined as two groats.[^37] Similarly, by 1488,
the gulden coin was valued at ninety Flemish groats, while, as a money
of account, the gulden was still forty groats.[^38]

A last point that should be emphasized: while moneys of account were
intended to simplify book keeping and contracts, multiple moneys of
account were often still used in a particular city, town, and region.

According to Van Werveke,[^39] Brabant towards the end of the fourteenth
century had in place at least four moneys of account based on coins out
of circulation, including the pound of old groats; at least five moneys
of account based on circulating gold coins; and various moneys of
account based on circulating silver coins, notably the pound of Flemish
groats. Even after the monetary reforms of 1433, the accounts of the
Burgundian receiver general in the Low Countries—the primary taxation
and financial official—for the years from 1433 to 1444 still contained
nine other moneys of account besides the pound groat and the pound of
forty groats.[^40] As mentioned above, Deventer and Arnhem in the
fourteenth century had various moneys of account in gold coins, even if
these ultimately were also grounded in the Flemish groat.[^41] In their
study of account books from Utrecht, Ketner and Tenhaeff identify at
least seven local moneys of account that were used in the city from the
late fourteenth to the late fifteenth centuries.[^42] According to
Spufford,[^43] an accountant in the Middle Ages might even make up an ad
hoc money of account if he deemed it convenient.

In our discussion, we have clearly distinguished between moneys of
account and situations where denominations are added or changed with
names of circulating coins. This is not always the case in historical
discussions. So some of the data mentioned above might be somewhat
inflated according to our stricter definitions. Nevertheless, it does
indicate the general diversity in standards before the modern era.

Moneys of account have long puzzled historians. Many in the early
twentieth century adopted the position that moneys of account were a
purely abstract standard of measurement,[^44] notably the Italian
economist Luigi Einaudi.[^45] He argued that imaginary money was "not
money at all". Instead, it was "a mere instrument or technical device
used to perform some monetary functions".[^46] Most modern historians,
however, have argued, as we have here, that moneys of account were
always grounded in some real coin, even if it was no longer in
circulation and sometimes difficult to identify.[^47]

Moneys of account seem somewhat complicated. However, their purpose is
fairly straightforward. In an economic environment with many circulating
coins, account books, contracts, and prices require standard measurement
systems of value for efficiency and transparancy. Most straightforward
is to create such systems from circulating coins. Sometimes the
temptation might exist to use coins that are no longer in circulation
out of habit, to promote stability, or for other reasons. In some cases,
the old standards of a coin might even be used for a money of account
when a newer version of the coin is actually circulating.

Importantly, the money of accounts described above, largely based on
coins, marked pre-modern accounting systems from the thirteenth century
onwards. However, in certain ways, the system before the thirteenth
century was probably quite similar. As mentioned in *Chapter 4*,
obligations at the end of the Dark Ages and into the High Middle Ages
were commonly specified in commodities or both coin—the regional
silver penny—and certain commodities.[^48] Obligations were, thus,
also stated in certain moneys of account. These obligations could then
be paid with the coin or the commodity specified, but also with other
types of commodities such as horses, oxen, sheep, cloths, vases, or
grain.[^49] The whole system would have been quite similar to that
regulating economic exchanges in Ancient Mesoptomia. During the High
Middle Ages, Europe then transformed from a system where coins and
commodities served as moneys of account to one where predominantly coins
served as moneys of account.

## 4. Legal, market, and intrinsic value

As was common in Ancient Mesopotamian civilizations, pre-modern European
coins standardly had official rates expressed in a common money of
account. For both gold and silver coins, their official rates were
always listed in silver-based moneys of account.[^50] This official
value of a coin might also be called its face value—despite the
absence of value markings on pre-modern coins—or its par value—where
"par" is Latin for "standard". Whenever a coin circulates at an official
rate in the marketplace, just counting them can determine their overall
value in exchange. We can, therefore, say that such a coin circulates by
tale. Typically, coins with official rates were considered legal tender:
that is, they were required to be accepted as a valid means of debt
settlement in economic exchange.

Importantly, monetary realms normally only provided official exchange
rates for the coins they issued domestically and a selection of foreign
coins, mainly international gold coins such as florins and ducats. Other
foreign coins were typically considered legally to be bullion and were
exchanged at rates determined by the market. Old domestic coins would
also sometimes be demonetized.[^51]

Lets set aside for now the thorny question about the extent to which
actual exchange rates for coins in the marketplace followed the official
rates in pre-modern economies. Instead, lets first turn to the details
of pre-modern minting systems where official exchange rates were
certainly important.

The minting systems in England and elsewhere in Europe were usually
based upon the principle of free coinage.[^52] Anyone could bring in
silver or gold bullion and request it to be minted into new coins,
though typically the bullion was brought in by money changers and
merchants. They often were, or at least were supposed to be, agents of
the mint, a role which included helping assure that sufficient bullion
was brought to the mint. The mints, of course, were operating under the
*de jure* and *de facto* guidance of certain rulers. It is clear that
instructions were not always closely followed.[^53]

In return for bringing bullion to the mint, a customer would receive
most of the new coins, typically a few weeks later. The mint, in turn,
kept a small number of them to cover the brassage and seignioriage. From
the Late Middle Ages onwards, customers often received a collection of
different coins rather than a single type.[^54] In some cases, the
customer could choose the coins they wished to mint from their
bullion.[^55] Instead of bullion, customers could also bring in old and
foreign coins. These would, then, be molten down and minted in just the
same fashion. So, at the mint at least, even old legal-tender coins were
actually priced according to their bullion content when brought in for
re-coining.

Let's work through a somewhat artificial example of the minting process.
Suppose that a London money changer in the sixteenth century brought
silver bullion into the Tower of London, say several bars weighing five
pounds in total. The purity of the silver is at 74%. The mint price was
standardly expressed in the money of account for a certain weight and
fineness. Suppose that the mint price is 1£ 18s pound sterling for one
troy pound of silver at 92.5% fineness. Given that each troy pound
contains 5,760 grains, the mint price is then, effectively, £1 18s pound
sterling per 5,328 grains of pure silver (5,760 x 0.925). The customer's
bullion contains 21,312 grains of pure silver (5 x 5,760 x 0.74). So the
customer, effectively, has brought four troy pounds of silver at 92.5%
fineness to the mint (21,312 / 5,328). Hence, the customer should
receive coins with a total face value of £7 12d pound sterling (4 x £1
18s).

Say for simplicity that the Tower of London is currently only producing
groats. These are, on average, 3 grams each with a 92.5% degree of
fineness. The mint only produces 120 groats from each troy pound of
92.5% silver—or 360 grams of groats instead of roughly 373—as some
of the silver is lost in the production process. Each groat is worth 4d
pound sterling. So the total coined value from one troy pound of bullion
at 92.5% fineness in the production of groats is £2 pound sterling. This
is known as the traite (or sometimes as the mint equivalent). Hence,
from the customer's four troy pounds of silver at 92.5% fineness, the
mint produces 480 groats or £8 pound sterling in coined value. The
customer is entitled to 7£ 12d pound sterling, which is the same as 456
groats. The mint retains 24 groats, or 8d pound sterling, for the
brassage and seigniorage, a total mint fee of 5%.

The mints often returned multiple coins. Sometimes the published traite
might have been given on the basis of a mixture of coins. In any case,
the production of any individual coin would have been marked by a
distinct traite, implicitly or explicitly. We can define the
depreciation of a coin such as a penny or groat then as simply an
increase in its (implicit) traite.[^56] Essentially, depreciation just
means that a coin's legal value has increased relative to its average
precious metal content. Coins were depreciated by two methods, which
were sometimes combined.

First, the monetary authority could raise the legal value of the coin.
This was also called the "crying up" of a coin given that in pre-modern
societies public laws and regulations were often communicated by verbal
announcements in public places. Suppose in the example above that the
monetary authority raises the official rate of the groat to 5d pound
sterling without any other changes to the production process and mint
fee. While 120 groats are still produced for every troy pound of 92.5%
silver, the total coined value from each such pound then becomes £2 10d
pound sterling instead of £2. A commercial loan, a rent, or any other
contract payment due in current pound sterling now becomes less dear in
terms of bullion. A rent of 10d pound sterling, for instance, now
requires a payment of 24 groats, whereas before it required 30 groats.
Depreciation through raising the legal value was commonly applied to
larger silver coins and especially gold coins.

Second, the monetary authority could depreciate a coin by reducing the
precious metal content either through a decrease in its purity (almost
always by adding copper), a decrease in its weight, or a combination.
This form of depreciation is called debasement.[^57] In the example
above, the monetary authority can, for instance, debase the groat by
reducing its weight to 2 grams and leaving the fineness at 92.5%.
Assuming no extra loss of silver in the production process, the mint can
now produce 180 groats per troy pound of 92.5% fine silver instead of
120 groats. A commercial loan, a rent, or any other contract due in
current pound sterling now becomes less in terms of bullion. A rent of
10d pound sterling, for instance, still requires a payment of 30 groats.
However, the precious metal content due has decreased from 90 grams to
60 grams. Depreciation through debasement was commonly applied to silver
coins, particularly those coins of lower value.

The appreciation of a coin is then the opposite of depreciation, namely
the decrease of the legal value of a coin relative to its precious metal
content. Appreciation of a coin by a reducing its legal money of account
value was also known as "crying down" the coin. Appreciation through
increasing a coin's precious metal content can be called reinforcement.
While the depreciation of coins was a much more common phenomenon in the
pre-modern world, coins were indeed sometimes subject to appreciation,
typically to restore their integrity after a period of severe
depreciation.

Let's now turn our discussion to the extent to which legal tender coins
were accepted at official values in the marketplace of their monetary
realm instead of some independent market rate.

Gold and silver bullion had a certain value in the marketplace and at
the mints. We might define the intrinsic value of a coin merely as the
economic value of its bullion content. The general impression in the
literature seems to be that the establishment of any market rate not
equal to the official rate for a coin would have to be grounded in
judgments about its intrinsic value. It is certainly true that
pre-modern citizens believed that the economic value of any coin was
generally determined by its intrinsic value, even if that belief
somewhat began to wane during the early modern period. Assuming that
establishing an alternative market rate for a coin indeed required a
grounding in judgments about its intrinsic value, the case for
circulation by tale becomes quite obvious, particularly for silver
coins.[^58]

Lets first turn to the circulation of silver coins. Mints only published
official rates, not information about the average weight or precious
metal content of different coins. In fact, during periods of aggressive
depreciation by the mints, quite frequently keeping the extent of the
depreciation a secret was the official policy.[^59]

But constantly using scales and touchstones in commercial transactions
would have been extremely cumbersome for normal exchanges with silver
coins. Many tradesmen did not even possess touchstones. And in any case,
those silver coins would always have been shrouded in certain
irreducible uncertainties given the large imperfections in measuring
their precious metal content. Especially touchstones missed accuracy.
Within the medieval period, many numismatic historians believe they only
had somewhere between five to ten percent accuracy.[^60] Even by the
nineteenth century, the touchstone was supposedly still about 1% off the
mark on average, assuming a high purity sample.[^61] So, the observation
of minor deviations in silver coins used during a commercial exchange,
could, therefore, also generate conflict.

Besides the costs of establishing the intrinsic values of silver coins
as compared to their legal values, circulation on the basis of a coin's
intrinsic value also seems fundamentally less practical. Suppose, for
example, the four main silver coins that circulated in sixteenth century
England: the penny, the groat, testoon, and the crown. The pound
sterling was the main money of account. As the money of account was
indexed one-for-one on the circulating penny, the official rate of a
penny was simply 1d pound sterling. The groat, testoon, and the crown
were larger silver coins. These were legally set at the following rates:
1 groat at 4d pound sterling; 1 testoon at 1s pound sterling; and 1
crown at 5s pound sterling.

Say these rates were exactly reflected in the silver content, but that
now the mint slightly debases the penny. Whereas before the traite was
300 pennies from a troy pound of sterling silver, the traite is now
raised to 309 pennies. New pennies are, therefore, made with about 3%
less silver than the old pennies, even though the official rates for all
the larger silver coins stayed the same. It is unlikely that the general
public would have really noticed such changes. But even if they did,
what would the practical consequences have actually been? One can hardly
pay a price of 12d pound sterling with 2.85 groats or some other
convenient combination of groats and pennies.

The main argument for silver coins commonly circulating by tale is, in
sum, that it is a low-cost convention. Of course, given the strong
belief in pre-modern economies that the intrinsic value of coins largely
determined their economic value, one would expect the practice of
acceptation of domestic silver coins by tale to come with certain
limits.

Pre-modern economic actors were certainly aware of issues such as
debasement, natural wear and tear, and clipping, shaving, and culling.
Certainly, our pre-modern counterparts were capable of making some basic
judgments about the standards for coins. In some cases, then, one would
expect general value judgments to arise on certain types and issues of
coins which deviated from official values. In this way, for example, an
older issue of the groat might circulate at a premium over newer issues,
or a groat might circulate at a premium compared to the penny. General
knowledge about the coinage might have also led to negotiations on the
spot for discounting coins, if a set of certain coins looked of
particularly poor quality compared to the standard. It is unlikely,
however, that touchstones or scales were frequently utilized for
ordinary transactions with silver coins.

The impression in the literature is that the situation was quite
different for gold coins. And that makes sense. Large commercial
transactions, particularly within the context of interregional and
international trade, were commonly conducted with gold coins. Small
deviations in the precious metal content of gold coins could impact
their overall economic value more than in smaller-scale transactions
with silver coins. But more importantly, official values of gold coins
were commonly listed in silver-based moneys of account. As the silver
gold bullion ratios fluctuated in the market, discrepancies between the
intrinsic value of silver and gold coins and their official exchange
rates were more likely to arise. Given that international and
interregional trade was conducted by specialists, scales and touchstones
were probably often available and well-known devices to most holders of
gold coins.

So, the pre-modern actors involved in exchanges with gold coins would
have been less incentivized to just accept them by tale. This is not to
state that gold coins never circulated by tale. Just that circulation by
tale was much less common. While the incentives were less pronounced,
substantial transactions with larger silver coins would have also made
acceptation by tale less attractive relative to ordinary transactions.

Some empirical evidence suggests that circulation by tale was indeed
quite common. Circulation by tale explains why rulers were frequently
able to increase seigniorage revenues from aggressive debasements of
silver coins. The mint price generally increased in such episodes but
relatively less than the debasement. Rulers, then, pocketed the
difference. If circulation by tale had little pull on market
participants, then it is a puzzle why any merchant or money-changer
would have ever brought old coins to the mint in such
circumstances.[^62] Many pre-modern economies also experienced Gresham's
Law type of effects, where certain higher-quality coins disappeared from
circulation due to them being legally undervalued.[^63] The existence of
such patterns requires acceptance by tale for an explanation.[^64]

In some cases, the divergence between legal and intrinsic values seems
to have been quite substantial. According to De Roover,[^65] for
instance, mites and double mites circulated practically as token coins
in late medieval Bruges, as their face value was much higher than their
intrinsic value. Munro suggests this was generally true for the
Burgundian Netherlands in the late fourteenth and fifteenth
centuries.[^66] Perhaps the same was true of France, where billon coins
also were generally overvalued.[^67] Another interesting observation in
this regard is from Spufford.[^68] He contends that during the English
Great Debasement (1544 – 1551),[^69] exchange rates and prices clearly
followed the increases in mint prices for bullion, not the rate of
actual debasement which was substantially greater. He also suggests that
similar trends have been observed in France during the fifteenth
century. Such observations can make sense if the public lacks knowledge
of the extent of coin depreciation and uses mint prices as a heuristic
to their bullion content.[^70]

There is also substantial evidence that coins, at least sometimes,
circulated at market rates that differed from their legal rates,
primarily due to deviations between legal and intrinsic values. In the
late eighteenth and early nineteenth century, the Spanish milled dollar
circulated at a premium to the U.S. silver dollar on the basis of a
fairly small difference, namely 373.5 to 371.25 grains of pure
silver.[^71] In the seventeenth century, the English authorities
undervalued gold guineas and these circulated at a premium against its
official silver-based money of account value almost immediately, one
that grew from around 10% to 50% from 1663 to 1690.[^72] In late
medieval and early modern France, silver and gold coins were frequently
depreciated by increasing their legal value. Many monetary edicts were
accompanied by complaints about the market prices of gold and
silver.[^73] When the Burgundian rulers strengthened the currency in
1389–90, they specified that deposit bankers could settle their
existing deposits using old groats (at the rate of 102 groats to the
noble) rather than new groats (at a rate of 72 groats to the
noble).[^74]

Certainly in other cases, various coins also circulated at a premium, or
perhaps a discount, instead of at their face value.[^75] That
particularly gold coins circulated at non-official rates would explain
why the quality of gold coins was generally better than silver coins:
there was less incentive to deteriorate them intentionally.[^76]

Rulers commonly understood that market exchange rates could differ from
legal exchange rates on the basis of disproportionate intrinsic values.
They often, therefore, strove to keep the value of the precious metal
content sufficiently proportional to the legal value of different coins.
This is why debasements in lower value silver coins were commonly
accompanied by the crying up of larger silver and gold coins, or
sometimes by the introduction of new coins with a lower precious metal
content.[^77] Sometimes rulers probably needed to be reminded about such
facts and myopically followed developments in the marketplace.

A famous case was, for instance, the debasements by France's Philip IV
who ruled from the end of the thireenth to the early fourteenth century.
From 1295 to 1303, he substantially debased the denier tournois without
making any changes to the legal value of the gros tournois (measured in
those deniers). Eventually, the gros tournois began circulating at a
substantial premium. As the rulers were not accepting the coin at a
premium but at official rates, there was a disincentive to use the gros
tournois for official payments. Due to the inconvenience, Philip was
forced to raise its money of account value to 15d tournois from 12d
tournois. In 1303, he even had to increase the value of the gros
tournois to 26.25 tournois, while reducing the coin's fineness by
25%.[^78] During the late medieval and early modern period, the rulers
in France often also seem to have increased the traite of gold coins
without the necessary adjustments in silver coins. Those adjustments
would abruptly follow at later points, undoubtedly due to the pressures
of undervaluing the silver currency.[^79]

Maintaining official exchange rates was fairly straightforward within
the coins of a particular metal: the precious metal content just needed
to be sufficiently proportional to discourage premiums or discounts. It
was, of course, much more difficult to manage between coins made from
different metals due to fluctuating bullion rates.

An interesting practice in this regard is observed in Florence during
the Late Middle Ages and early modern period. Unlike in most other
monetary realms, the florentine authorities did not attempt to set legal
exchange rates between silver coins and the florin. Instead, the florin
and silver coins were measured in separate moneys of account and no
official exchange rates existed between them. Instead, these exchange
rates were just determined by market dynamics.[^80] Venice seems to have
operated with a similar system by the early fifteenth century.[^81] It
is tempting to describe these systems as rational responses to the
complications of maintaining official exchange rates when gold coins
tended to circulate on the basis of their intrinsic value.[^82] Systems
with floating exchange rates between gold and silver coins have also
been called systems with a parallel standard, rather than a bimetallic
standard.[^83]

In the discussion so far, we have made little mention about the legal
enforcement regimes that encouraged circulation by tale. Most of Europe
had restrictions backed by punishments and fines that encouraged the use
of coins at their official rates, including restrictions on the use of
foreign coins. While these rules undoubtedly played some role in
encouraging circulation by tale, certainly in particular times and
places, they seem to have often been fairly toothless. In fourteenth
century Bruges, for instance, De Roover notes that money-changers
typically did not bring bullion to the mint, often willingly circulated
counterfeit coins, and normally bought gold coins at market rates
instead of official rates. He also suggests that non-official coins
often were re-circulated by moneychangers, rather than brought to the
mint, that laws on the export of bullion were broken.[^84] Hence, the
low-cost convention of accepting coins by tale would have generally
exerted more pressure on commercial exchange than legal threats.

The discussion on circulation by tale or the intrinsic value of the
coins has been the subject of some controversy among economists and
economic historians.[^85] It also a discussion that can probably not be
settled easily just by empirical evidence. But circulation by tale must
have had substantial influence on commercial exchange, particularly for
silver coins. The reasons are obvious. It is a low-cost and practical
convention in the face of substantial costs surrounding the
determination of the intrinsic value of coins. To some extent, it was
also backed up by legal threats.

So, at least in normal times, then, legal tender silver coins generally
circulated at their tale value in the ordinary exchanges of a monetary
realm. That value included some small premium with respect to their
intrinsic value.[^86] Coins had utility over bullion, as they were in a
more convenient form for standard commercial exchanges. They also
offered immediate recognizability to anyone in the marketplace and
credibility with regards to their intrinsic value. The public would have
also understood that mints incurred costs and believed it legitimate
that rulers were entitled to a small profit from their operation. To a
lesser extent, gold coins would have sometimes circulated in this
manner. The existence of a premium on domestic coins was important, as
will be further discussed in *Chapter 6*, to assuring the smooth
operation of international bill of exchange markets.[^87]

There were only a few pre-modern exceptions to this generality where
domestic coins generally circulated at a value substantially higher than
their precious metal content, notably in England until the time of Henry
II and in Late Republican Rome.[^88] In these cases, a credible legal
apparatus with fines and punishments would certainly have been more of a
contributing factor.
