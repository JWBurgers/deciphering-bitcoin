# Chapter 6. The Commercial Revolution

The more stable conditions at the end of the Early Middle Ages led to an
economic expansion that lasted into the early modern era,
notwithstanding of course the interruptions caused by the epidemics,
famines, and wars that plagued the Late Middle Ages. The key actor
within this economic boom was the merchant who helped transform Europe
from a petty capitalist to a commercial capitalist system.

By the end of the Middle Ages, local and regional trade was carried on
by merchants everywhere in Europe. While long-distance trade was also
carried on by the Dutch, English, Spanish, and particularly the Germans
through the Hanseatic League, the majority of long-distance trade was
controlled by northern Italians from cities such as Genoa, Venice, Pisa,
Florence, and Milan. They introduced new business techniques such as
double-entry accounting and the bill of exchange, and more than any
other group expanded European markets during the Middle Ages. It is,
therefore, specifically the Italian merchants that should be credited
with Europe's transformation into a commercial capitalist system.

In this chapter, we first describe the role of the merchant in the
medieval economy. In *Sections 2* and *3*, we, then, discuss two key
innovations introduced by merchants in the High Middle Ages, namely
exchange contracts and the medieval trade fairs. We describe the
transition of Europe from a petty capitalist to a commercial capitalist
system in *Section 4*. Finally, in *Section 5*, we briefly offer some
reflections on the Hanseatic League.

## 1. The Medieval Merchant

Throughout the High and Late Middle Ages, agricultural production,
artisanry, and basic manufacturing was typically centered around
household enterprises that were based on the nuclear or extended family.
Other than at a local level, these producers would rarely engage in
trade directly. Instead, particularly trade at the regional level and
beyond was the domain of professional **merchants**. They bought goods
wholesale and sold them wholesale to other merchants and retailers, and
often also to end-consumers. What separated merchants from mere
retailers is that the nature of their business was grounded in wholesale
trade. Merchants in the Middle Ages traded a great diversity of goods,
and took advantage of whatever opportunities were present.

Throughout the High and Late Middle Ages, merchants were not really
involved in production and mostly limited themselves to trade.[^1] This
separation between production and trade was, of course, not universal.
The Medici, for example, owned silk and woollen textile manufacturing
operations in the fifteenth century.[^2] While silk textiles were a
luxury product, woollen textiles were the primary product for making
clothes and other textile products for the masses. Linnen and cotton
were much less commonly used for textiles. The Medici textile
manufacturing operations were organized as a **putting out system**,
where they essentially circulated the raw materials and intermediate
products to specialized artisans for processing in stages. The different
steps in the process of production were not unified within one
manufacturing facility.[^3] The steps involving different artisans were
mechanized to varying degrees. Innovations such as the fulling mill, the
horizontal treadle loom, the draw loom, and the spinning wheel had been
introduced in the previous centuries.

The Medici operations were not unique. From the thirteenth century
onwards, the putting out system had become increasingly common in
European textile manufacturing. The manufacturing process could include
more than thirty different steps. The entrepreneurs managing it were
typically called drapers (or *drapiers* in French or *lanaioli* in
Italian). Commonly they also acted as merchants on local, regional, and
international markets for the textiles produced by these manufacturing
operations.[^4] Overall, while trade remained their primary business,
merchants increasingly seem to have been involved in production during
the Late Middle Ages of which textile manufacturing is the foremost
example.[^5]

Trade until the early modern era was primarily in foodstuffs, textiles,
and textile raw materials, especially wool. But it also extended to
timber, tar, metals, peat, coal, jewelry, arms, and many other
products.[^6] Output at that time lacked modern standardization and
quality guarantees, so goods were rarely shipped to order on the basis
of descriptions or samples; instead, merchants transported goods from
place to place, so that inspections and purchases could be made on the
spot.[^7] This venturing nature to trade was situated in an environment
of slow transport times and risks from pirates, bandits, government
authorities, and not always reliable transport services. Merchants were
largely operating as venturers well into the early modern period.[^8]
This venturing nature to trade explains why many early English regulated
and joint-stock companies have the terms "adventurers" or "merchant
adventurers" in their names.

From the start of the High Middle Ages into the early modern era, Europe
generally consisted of two main economic zones: northern Italy and
northwestern Europe. During the Middle Ages, the core area of economic
activity within northwestern Europe was in the Low Countries,
particularly the southern Low Countries. As seen in *Chapter 3*,
economic growth, including per capita growth, was driven by Italy and
the Low Countries throughout the period.

Different regions within these large zones—understood broadly as the
areas concentrated around towns of a few thousand inhabitants and
upwards—traded with each other. The two zones also traded with each
other as well as with Asia and Africa, and, from the late fifteenth
century onwards, with the Americas. While long-distance trade,
particularly between Europe and the rest of the world, has often been
the subject of historical discussion, the bulk of trade in the Late and
High Middle Ages was actually local and regional. Long-distance trade
and to a lesser extent interregional trade was quantitatively much less
important.[^9]

Long-distance trade was only carried out and financed by the wealthiest
merchants. But while it was quantitatively less important than local and
regional trade, it was more crucial to transforming Europe during the
Middle Ages than its moderate quantitative scale might suggest. It led
to the introduction of new types of goods, technologies, and ideas.
Trade with Asia introduced Europe during the Middle Ages, for example,
to new industries such as paper-making, gunpowder, cotton and silk
textiles, glass, soap, ceramics and porcelain, sugar, and rice.
Long-distance trade also introduced Hindu-Arabic numerals to the son of
a Pisan customs official in Algeria, Leonardo da Pisa (ca. 1170–1250),
better known as **Fibonacci**. His books, most famously his *Liber
Abaci* (1202), were instrumental in spreading awareness throughout
Europe about the Hindu-Arabic counting system, though it took several
hundred years for the system to be really embraced.[^10] Interregional
trade, in turn, seems to have had the most extensive impact on the
specialization of production, the division of labor, and technological
progress in manufacturing and agriculture.[^11]

While everyone engaged in local and regional trade, long-distance trade
was dominated in the High and Late Middle Ages by Italian merchants from
cities such as Venice, Genoa, Pisa, Milan, Siena, and Florence.

By the end of the Early Middle Ages, the majority of the trade in the
Mediterranean was controlled by the Maghribi Jews, so-called because
they stem from the Maghreb region in North Africa. Their trading empire
also extended to the Red Sea, Arabian Sea, and the Indian Ocean. To a
smaller degree, Mediterranean trade was also conducted by Muslim Arabs,
Byzantines, and others.[^12] As northern Italy developed, Venetian,
Genoese, Pisan, and Amalfitan merchants increasingly traversed the
Mediterranean waters, often with naval military support. While Italian
influence had already been increasing in the region, the position of the
Italian merchants as the primary Mediterranean trading power was further
cemented by the Crusades, which began in 1096. Antisemitism increased
drastically in this period and the Maghribi Jews did not have the naval
military forces to protect their trading activities. By the early
twelfth century, the Italians dominated the Mediterranean.

From here on out, Italian influence over international trade and finance
grew strongly. During the Late Middle Ages, the Italians became key
players in international trade and finance in cities such as Barcelona,
Valencia, Montpellier, Lyon, Paris, Bruges, and London. Anywhere across
southern and western Europe that had significant markets for
international trade and finance typically had substantial Italian
enclaves.

As discussed in *Chapter 3*, Europe seems to have experienced moderate
economic growth, including per capita growth, in the High and Late
Middle Ages. According to Maddisson, Western Europe had an average
growth rate of 0.29 percent in the period. Eastern Europe had a somewhat
lower growth rate of 0.19 percent annually on average.[^13] Maddisson
also estimates the average per capita growth rate annually for the
period as 0.13 percent for Western Europe and 0.04 percent for Eastern
Europe.[^14] Many historians broadly place the expansion of trade and
the activities of the medieval merchants—particularly the Italian
merchants—made possible by the more stable conditions at the end of
the Early Middle Ages, at the forefront of explanations on the economic
expansion that lasted into the early modern period.[^15]

A Smithian explanation for much of the economic growth experienced in
this period seems fitting. Kohn, for example, offers such an
explanation.[^16] He argues that the key factor behind the expansion of
trade was the decreased costs to trading, including in transaction
costs, transportation costs, and financing costs.[^17] These cost
reductions were driven by merchants. The resultant growth in trade was,
then, accompanied by population growth, urbanization, advances in
finance and business practices, and technological progress. There were
certainly substantial feedback loops at play. Expansion in trade,
notably, generated investments in institutional and physical
infrastructure, increased specialization in production, and further
divisions of labor. These developments fostered technological progress.
All of these factors, in turn, further expanded trade.

Economic progress was not completely limited trade, of course. The High
and Late Middle Ages also saw growth and advancements in agricultural
production and manufacturing.

Agricultural output increased substantially from the tenth to the
fourteenth century.[^18] It outpaced population growth and, thus, per
capita agricultural output also increased. In the Late Middle Ages, per
capita agricultural output seems to have been at least twice that of the
Carolingian era. Crop failures had easily led to famines and
malnutrition in the Early Middle Ages, but famines were relatively rare
by the early thirteenth century. By the end of the Middle Ages, the vast
majority of the population in Europe was still employed within the
agricultural sector. Flanders, Holland, and northern Italy, however, did
begin to somewhat resemble regions with modern economies.

A large driver behind the agricultural expansion seems to have been the
development of new agricultural land as well as the reclamation of
abandoned agricultural land. The later Early Middle Ages and the High
Middle Ages also saw the broad introduction of various technologies and
innovative techniques, including the watermill, the windmill, the heavy
plough, and the three-field system. The use of horses instead of oxen
also became more in common in part through the widespread introduction
of technical advancements such as the horse collar and the nailed iron
horseshoe. Overall, the medieval agricultural sector made substantial
strides in using labor-saving devices compared to the Romans. Some of
the medieval technologies, like the watermill, even stem from Antiquity,
but never became well-utilized before the Middle Ages. The growth in
agricultural surplus until the fourteenth century supported the
expansion of towns, cities, manufacturing, and commerce. But agriculture
was in turn also influenced by these developments. The rise of markets
and communication networks meant farmers could increasingly manage the
risks of specialization; it also supposed the spread of technologies and
techniques.

The rise of the merchant class also boosted artisans and
manufacturing.[^19] The woollen textile industry generally saw the most
mechanization and efficiency gains.[^20] Textiles made from wool were
the most important product of the Middle Ages other than foodstuffs.
They were produced for a mass market. From the eleventh to the
fourteenth century, the heart of production was in the southern Low
Countries, particularly in the Flemish cities of Ghent, Bruges, and
Ypres. Florence was another main center of textile production that arose
in the thirteenth century as did several other northern Italian towns
and cities. By the end of the Middle Ages, woollen textile production
was more fragmented and, besides the southern Low Countries and
Florence, also included England and Holland as important international
centers. Various labor-saving innovations were introduced during this
period, including the fulling mill, the horizontal treadle loom, the
draw loom, and the spinning wheel.

The woollen textile industry was advanced and massive by the standards
of the High and Late Middle Ages. Around 1300, for example, more than
half the population of Ghent and Ypres were probably involved in the
wool industry. Many peasants were lured to the towns and cities to
participate in it. However, while those working in the textile industry
were hardly pure artisans, we cannot yet describe them as factory
workers by the end of the Middle Ages. They were typically organized at
that time according to the putting out system, a decentralized system
spread across various workshops. Additionally, the level of
mechanization and automation was simply too primitive. So the woollen
textile industry at the time is probably best described as
pre-industrial. Other types of crafts in the Middle Ages remained less
organized and automated, typically confined to family workshops.

So, while economic progress in the High and Late Middle Ages was also
seen in the agricultural and manufacturing sectors, it was the expansion
of trade and the merchants conducting that trade which really drove
Europe forward. Medieval merchants introduced many innovations such as
double-entry accounting, Arabic numerals, and bills of exchange. All
this innovation generally came from the bottom up. In the words of the
economic historian David Landes,[^21]

> The economic expansion of medieval Europe was thus promoted by a
> succession of organization innovations and adaptations, most of them
> initiated from below and diffused by example. The rulers, even local
> seigneurs, scrambled to keep pace, to show themselves hospitable, to
> make labor available, to attract enterprise and the revenues it
> generated. At the same time, the business community invented new forms
> of association, contract, and exchange designed to secure investment
> and facilitate payment. In these centuries a whole new array of
> commercial instruments came into use; commercial codes were elaborated
> and enforced; and partnership arrangements were devised to encourage
> alliances between lenders and doers, between the men who supplied the
> funds and merchandise and those who went to distant lands to sell and
> to buy. Almost all of this "commercial revolution" came from the
> mercantile community, bypassing where necessary the rules of this or
> that city or state, inventing and improvising new venues for encounter
> and exchange (ports and outports, *fauburgs*, local markets,
> international fairs), creating in short a world of its own like an
> overlay on the convoluted, inconvenient mosaic of political units.

Merchants and the innovations they brought were, of course, hardly
always welcomed by landed nobility and ecclesiastical rulers. Since
Antiquity, merchants had often been looked at with suspicion and trade
was seen as a devious activity. This sentiment partially explains why
the Maghribi Jews were the main long-distance merchants for Europe at
the start of the High Middle Ages. The High and Late Middle Ages is,
therefore, also a period in which merchants fought for social and
political standing.

The **guild** was a key instrument. While the origins of guilds surely
precede the High and Late Middle Ages, they really came to the fore in
that period for both merchants and artisans as economic development
progressed. By 1294, for example, more than 36,000 out the nearly 50,000
citizens of Bologna were a member of a guild or a family-member of a
member of a guild. These Bolognese guilds not only covered artisans,
merchants, and shopkeepers, but also professionals such as notaries and
physicians.[^22] While guilds came in various flavors, they typically
standardized products, regulated competition and entry into the
profession, set labor standards, and often acted as a united front
politically and socially. The regulated companies which preceded the
joint stock companies in England, as mentioned in *Chapter 4*, as well
as the hansa, which we discuss later in this chapter, were somewhat
similar types of associations for merchants.

Particularly the most powerful merchants began to obtain political power
and influence during the High and Late Middle Ages. This is most visible
in the northern Italian communes and city states. From the early
fourteenth to the early sixteenth century, for instance, the Genoese
commune as well as the House of Saint George was dominated by landed
elites and powerful merchant bankers. Similar scenes played out
elsewhere. In some cases, more modest merchants and artisans also gained
political influence. The foremost example here is Florence, where
politics was dominated by the *grandi*—wealthy merchants, bankers, and
landowners—and the *popolo*— more economically modest local
merchants, manufacturers, shopkeepers, artisans, and notaries—from the
early fourteenth to the early sixteenth century. When Florence became
ruled by a prince rather than a republican political system in the early
sixteenth century, it was naturally a powerful merchant banking family,
the Medici, who acquired the ducal title. The rise of parliaments to
represent the new and revived towns and cities from the late twelfth
century onwards should also, of course, also largely be seen in the
light of furthering the interests of merchants.

This quest for influence by merchants continued into the Early Modern
Period. Most notably, the Dutch Revolt (1568–1648), the English Civil
Wars (1642–1651), and the Glorious Revolution (1688) were driven by
merchants that wanted to increase the power of parliaments, to their
benefit, at the expense of the monarch.[^23] The success of these
political movements made the Dutch Republic and England the economic
motor of Europe in the age of absolutism.

## 2. Exchange contracts

With regards to long-distance payments, commercial credit, and exchange
rate arbitrage and speculation, the single most important financial
innovation that emerged among Italian merchants during the High Middle
Ages was that of the exchange contract. It was a very popular financial
instrument from around 1200 well into the early modern period. We
discuss the history and mechanics of exchange contracts here, and then
turn to their functions more elaborately in *Chapter 9* when discussing
merchant banks.

According to Baumgartl,[^24] merchants at the Fairs of Champagne—key
international trade and financial events that were particularly
important from the end of the twelfth century until about 1300—could
already make purchases cashless and on credit through two types of
financial instruments at the end of the twelfth century: the *lettre de
foire* and the *instrumentum ex causa cambii* or *cambium* contract.
Both documents contained essentially promises to pay a debt at a later
time. The *lettre de foire* was a certificate only issued by the court
of the fairs, and confirmed the promise of a buyer to pay a seller at a
later fair. The **cambium contract** was, instead, written up by a
merchant and officially notarized. It contained a promise to pay at a
later date and place, but was not bound to the fairs in this regard.
Often repayment was in a different currency. These contracts served
buyers who needed to make purchases on credit; but they could also be
created just for the purpose of a direct loan.

Others maintain roughly similar dates as Baumgartl for the emergence of
the *cambium* contract. According to Kohn,[^25] for example, the
earliest record of one dates to 1220, though it probably indeed already
existed in the twelfth century. And according to De Roover, the
*cambium* contract was certainly popular in the thirteenth and early
fourteenth centuries. Many examples exist from Genoese notarial records
of the time, where money would be borrowed in Genoa through a *cambium*
contract and repaid at one of the fairs in Champagne.[^26] These
contracts were often the product of purchases on credit, but were also
created purely for lending activities outside the scope of offering
credit to buyers.

The bill of exchange, initially called the *lettera di pagamento* and
later the *lettera di cambio*, was the successor to the *cambium*
contract. We cannot say exactly when it emerged. Our oldest surviving
examples come from the early fourteenth century.[^27] Indirectly,
however, the records certainly indicate that it was already used in the
thirteenth century.[^28] At Avignon, for instance, statutes referencing
bills of exchange were already passed in 1253.[^29] The Venetians, in
turn, already had statutes pertaining to bills of exchange by 1272.[^30]
Keiley interestingly argues that the bill of exchange originated with
the expulsion of the Jews from France in 1181 by Phillip II. As it was
forbidden to take or send money out of the kingdom, Jews sold their
personal property to trusted parties in France. They, then, settled in
Lombardy and sold early versions of bills of exchange to Italian
merchants who, then, had a claim on the proceeds kept in France.[^31]

So what exactly is the bill of exchange?

The **bill of exchange**, unlike the *cambium* contract, is not a
promise to pay, but a payment order. Roughly speaking, it is an order
written up by a party A with instructions to a party B to pay a party C
at some time in the future at a different location. Unlike the earlier
*cambium* contract, the bill of exchange was not officially notarized.
Instead, bills of exchange were written up by the merchants themselves.
Consequently, they fell under the jurisdiction of merchant
courts—specialized courts that enforced merchant customs[^32]—rather
than civil courts.

The lack of notarization represented a substantial break with standard
Italian business practices. During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries,
notaries were utilized for practically every kind of business contract
in Italy, unless the transaction proceeded through a cash transfer. This
included contracts for partnership agreements, capital investments from
non-partners, loans, credit sales, banking operations, leases, labor and
apprenticeship contracts, and so on.[^33] The practice of writing up
bills of exchange among Italian merchants naturally would have been
supported with the increasing literacy and general education of
merchants during the High Middle Ages, including through the *abbaco*
schools, trade schools which first emerged in thirteenth century
Italy.[^34] It would have also been incentivized by the growing volume
of trade and the time investment and hassle required for notarization.

Given that the bill of exchange was an order by a party A that, at a
different location, party B pay party C, the recognition of handwriting
on bills of exchange was extremely important. In the fifteenth century,
for example, the Medici Company in Florence maintained lists of people
authorized to write out bills of exchange for their foreign branches as
well as for any companies with which they regularly executed exchange
transactions. They also kept verified handwriting samples for these
people. If handwriting on a bill of exchange was not recognized from
among those in the Medici "database", it could lead to the rejection of
the bill.[^35]

Lets now turn to the detailed mechanics of the bill of exchange. The
standard version of the instrument actually involves four logically
distinct parties in two locations. They are standardly known as the
deliverer, the taker (or drawer), the payer (or drawee), and the payee.
Essentially, in location A, the taker receives a sum of money from the
deliverer; then, at a later agreed upon time, the payer gives a sum of
money to the payee in location B. Sometimes the payer and payee agreed
on the collection of the money at an earlier time against a small
discount. Typically the two legs of the transaction involved two
different currencies, usually those local to locations A and B. The bill
was drawn up by the taker, hence why the taker is also called the
drawer.

The deliverer and the taker in a bill of exchange were in some type of
relationship with, respectively, the payee and payer in location B.
Suppose the deliverer was a merchant. The payee might, then, have been
an agent for that merchant looking to purchase commodities in location
B. The taker in location A could, then, have been a merchant banker who
also had a branch or an agent in location B that acted as the payee. The
nature of the relationships involved differed. Most generally, we can
describe the payee as a correspondent of the deliverer, and the payer as
a correspondent of the taker. The term **correspondent** here obviously
originates from the fact that these long-distance relationships were
managed by correspondence.

In some cases a bill of exchange might be due upon sight. Commonly
repayment was also set to the date of an international fair.[^36] In
many cases, however, repayment was just set at a number of days from the
issue of the bill, often at what was known as usance.[^37]

Much of the traffic in bills of exchange occurred between large towns
and cities that functioned as financial centers. In the Late Middle
Ages, these included, to varying degrees, Bologna, Florence, Genoa,
Milan, Naples, Palermo, Pisa, Rome, and Venice in Italy. Outside of
Italy, they included, to varying degrees, Barcelona, Palma de Majorca,
Valencia, Avignon, Montpellier, Bruges, Paris, and London.[^38] A bill
of exchange between any two such towns and cities had a standardized
period after which they were due from the moment of issuance. That
period was known as **usance**. The usance between Florence and London,
for instance, was 90 days in the Late Middle Ages, while it was 60 days
between Florence and Bruges.[^39] From London to Antwerp, Amsterdam,
Middleburg, or Hamburg in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was 30
days.[^40] Bills between towns and cities that functioned as financial
centers were commonly due at usance, though recent evidence suggests
that it may have been less frequently used than originally thought by
historians.[^41]

The key aspect about the bill of exchange, as well as the earlier
*cambium* contract, is that money "transfers" from the deliverer in
location A to the payee in location B *without requiring any shipment of
specie, bullion, or other types of valuables*. By **specie** here, we
mean gold and silver coins issued by a government or some private
institution. By **bullion**, we mean gold and silver in standardized
shapes such as bars. Other types of suitable valuables might have been
commodities such as grain or salt. So, no value, at least not in the
physical sense, moved between locations A and B with a bill of exchange
or a *cambium* contract.

To really understand the mechanics of bills of exchange, it is best to
explore an actual example. Consider the following bill of exchange
discussed by Bolton and Guidi-Bruscoli. It is from 1447 and translated
from Italian.[^42]

> \[In the name of\] Jesus on the 7th of March 1447, in Venice. Pay by
> this first letter at usance to ser Allessandro Borromeo, son of messer
> Antonio, 400 ducats at the rate of exchange of 43.5 sterlings per
> ducat and this sum was received \[in Venice by Michele Zon\] from ser
> Giovanni Borromei & Partners. \[Addressed to\] Jacopo Salviati &
> Partners in London.

The religious opening here was extra encouragement for the taker and
their correspondent to honor the bill. While the writing is brief and
somewhat archaic, the details are fairly easy to comprehend.

To start, the bill of exchange was written in Venice on March 7, 1447 by
Michele Zon. He acknowledges to have received a payment from Giovanni
Borromei & Partners of 400 Venetian ducats. In the fifteenth century,
the Borromei family had three family lines that managed banking
operations: a Milanese, a Florentine, and a Venetian line.[^43] Giovanni
Borromei & Partners is the name of the bank owned and operated by the
Venetian line of the family. It also had branches in Bruges and London.
So, here, Michele Zon is the taker (or drawer) and the Venetian Borromei
bank the deliverer.

The bill, then, instructs the Salviati company in London—who here,
thus, have the role of the payer and are a correspondent of Michele
Zon—to pay a sum of 400 Venetian ducats at the rate of 43.5 sterlings
per ducat. At the time, London, as elsewhere in Europe, often employed a
system of counting for coins where the pound was equivalent to twenty
shillings, and each shilling was equivalent to twelve coins. The units
were denoted, respectively, by £, s, and d. The sterling coin mentioned
in the bill refers to the English penny. So, the bill actually instructs
the Salviati company to pay 17,400 pennies, the product of 400 Venetian
ducats multiplied by 43.5 sterlings per ducat. This is equivalent to £72
10s English pennies.

The payee for the sum of money, £72 10s English pennies, is a
correspondent of the Venetian Borromei bank in London, Allessandro
Borromeo. The latter was actually running the London branch of the bank.
The payment was to be made at usance, which at the time was 90 days
between Venice and London. So the payment was to be collected on June 5,
1447.

The Salviati company and Allessandro Borromei would have been brought
into the bill of exchange through correspondence. First, Michele Zon
would have probably drawn up the bill in multiple copies. Then, in order
to guarantee receipt, the Venetian Borromei bank probably sent two or
more copies by different channels to Allessandro Borromeo. Michele Zon,
in turn, may have sent several copies of a letter of advice (or *aviso*)
to the Salviati company, instructing them to honor the bill of exchange
when it was presented by Allessandro Borromeo. Alternatively, Zon may
have included the matter of the bill in a more general business letter
to the Salviati company.

Once Allessandro Borromeo received a copy of the bill of exchange from
Venice, he would have presented it to the Salviati company in London.
The latter would have "accepted" the bill under normal circumstances by
writing *acetta* across it. On June 5, Allessandro Borromeo would, then,
have returned with the accepted bill to collect his money from the
Salviati company. Sometimes a payee might, instead, have collected the
sum of money earlier at a small discount.

Importantly, unlike the description of this situation might suggest, it
is unlikely that any specie, bullion, or other form of physical value
ever changed hands in either Venice or London. Instead, in Venice,
Michele Zon likely just received a credit of 400 ducats with the
Venetian Borromei bank. He probably also held an account with the
Salviati company that would have been debited by £72 10s to facilitate
the payment. In any case, both legs of the payment likely proceeded
through credit transfers.

While physical money or value did not move between locations A and B,
the essence of a bill of exchange, it was thus also uncommon for any
physical money or value to be involved *at* locations A and B. Bolton
and Guidi-Bruscoli, for instance, studied around 23,000 transactions
from the bank branches in Bruges and London owned by the Milanese, and
not the Venetian, line of the Borromei family. They conclude that "cash
payments amounted to a very limited share of all transactions, even at a
local level, with credits and debits transferred from one account holder
to the other or to and from the bank".[^44] For another example, Kohn
mentions that between 1456 and 1459 a bank in Genoa received 160,000
lire in payments from bills of exchange, of which 92.5% was settled in
bank credit. Only the remaining 7.5% was in the form of cash
settlement.[^45]

The example above has shown the mechanics of the bill of exchange. While
the form of the earlier *cambium* contract was different, the mechanics
were very similar. One party would give a sum of money to another party
at location A. The contract, then, specified that the recipient or their
correspondent would pay a sum of money to the party that gave the sum or
their correspondent at a later date in location B. A *cambium* contract
typically also involved two different currencies.

Due to the similarities, the "bill of exchange" in the literature
sometimes refers to both the *lettera di pagamento* and the *lettera di
cambio*, as well as the earlier *cambium* contract. For clarity, it is
convenient to generally call them and similar instruments **exchange
contracts**.

A key aspect to exchange contracts is that they contained an inherent
credit aspect. A sum of money was given in one place to be paid back at
a later date in another place. However, interest was never explicitly
charged within such contracts. Instead, interest rates were incorporated
indirectly into the currency exchange rates.

The Middle Ages and the early modern period were marked by highly
restrictive **usury laws**—that is, laws which impose restrictions on
the charging of interest rates for loans. While usury laws still exist
today, they are generally much less strict. Sometimes usury laws banned
charging interest outright, as in England until 1545.[^46] More
commonly, however, they imposed a maximum interest rate. Usury laws
received much support from biblical and theological arguments and the
church was often a key driving force behind them, though arguments from
economists and other scholars also provided an intellectual foundation.
Perhaps the key intellectual argument against usury laws that
precipitated their loosening in recent history is Jeremy Bentham's
*Defense of Usury*, originally published in 1787.[^47]

Due to the exchange of different currencies, exchange contracts
typically fell outside of the scope of usury laws.[^48] De Roover has
often emphasized the evasion of usury restrictions for the popularity of
exchange contracts. Others have also emphasized other factors, noting,
for example, that the bill of exchange remained popular even after usury
restrictions decreased.[^49]

## 3. The Medieval Fairs

The expansion of trade during the High and Late Middle Ages was
accompanied by many institutional and technical advancements. One key
institutional advancement was the increasing importance of the medieval
fair.

By a medieval **fair**, historians typically mean a certain type of
periodic gathering for the purposes trade and finance. Every fair was
integrated into a larger network of such events for supporting
particularly interregional and interzonal trade. Fairs were mostly
attended by professional merchants and marked by wholesale trade. They
were also important financial markets, and sometimes they were really
more financial than commercial markets. The **Fairs of
Besançon-Piacenza**,[^50] most prominent towards the end of the Middle
Ages and at the beginning of the early modern period, were set up by
Genoese merchant bankers purely for the purposes of serving as financial
markets. By contrast, when historians of the period speak of a
**market**, they more commonly refer to a periodic gathering that
focused on serving local and regional trade. While not devoid of
wholesale trade, markets were generally more oriented towards
end-consumers.[^51]

In the Middle Ages, fairs and markets usually took place outside or in a
courtyard.[^52] They operated on the basis of privileges granted by
ruling authorities who specified their rights and regulations, including
specifications on dates, times, and location. Ruling authorities
benefitted from fairs and markets through taxes, fees, and, certainly
with regards to fairs, the broader financial and commercial
opportunities these brought to their realm.[^53] Fairs typically took
place once or a few times per year, while markets could be weekly,
monthly, or even annual.

The famous medieval fair that took place at St. Denis, near Paris, had a
history that can be traced back to the seventh century. But into the
ninth century, there is scarcely any further information about similar
events, though local markets were certainly common. A sharp growth in
the size and the number of fairs is, then, seen in the eleventh and
twelfth centuries.[^54] The most important fairs to emerge out of this
period are certainly the **Fairs of Champagne**.

In their heyday, the Fairs of Champagne consisted of six gatherings per
year in four different towns, including Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and
Bar-sur-Aube. There is some variation in the literature with regards to
the supposed duration of these fairs. But indicatively, Sangster
concludes each of them seems to have lasted between 46 and 52 days.[^55]
Merchants from France, Flanders, Brabant, Spain, Germany, England, and
elsewhere came to buy and sell their goods, including textiles, raw wool
and silk, leather, furs, linens, spices, sugar, wax, grain, wines, and
more. The key participants in these fairs were above all else the
Italian merchants who used them foremost to purchase northern textiles
in exchange for spices, silk, and other luxury items. Those northern
textiles were subsequently distributed throughout the Mediterranean
world.[^56]

The four towns involved in the Fairs of Champagne already had trade
events by the early twelfth century.[^57] But, at that time, these
events were just local or regional markets. During the twelfth century,
however, they transformed into hubs for international trade and finance.
While the fairs had already grown substantially in size by the middle of
the century, a key development would have been the arrival of the
Italian merchants in the last quarter of the century.[^58] From the late
twelfth century to around 1300, the Fairs of Champagne really served as
the center of international trade and finance, after which they went
into a quick decline in importance.[^59]

The Fairs of Champagne were, at least by Medieval standards, colossal
and intricately organized events. As illustratively described by
Verlinden,[^60]

> The lords of the fairs were at first the counts of Champagne, and
> later the kings of France. They derived considerable revenues from the
> fairs: taxes on the residences and stalls of the merchants, entry and
> exit tolls, levies on sales and purchases, dues upon weights and
> measures, justice and safe-conduct charges upon the Italians and Jews.
> The chief officers attached to the fairs were the wardens of the fair
> who were responsible for order and justice and controlled the fair
> seal, at least until 1318 when a chancellor or keeper of the fair seal
> was appointed. Under them were the wardens' lieutenants or clerks,
> together with sergeants-at-arms to maintain order. It should not be
> forgotten that, conjointly with the special jurisdiction set up for
> the fairs, the town magistrature and the local ecclesiastical
> dignitaries also exercised a certain authority over the merchants and
> their transactions. The court of the fair wardens was, however, a
> tribunal of capital importance as can be seen especially by the number
> of sergeants it employed. In 1317 there were no less than 140,
> although the Châtelet of Paris, about this time, had no more than 150.
> There were sixty notaries at the Châtelet and forty at the fairs.

There is widespread conviction among historians that the Fairs of
Champagne, in their heydays, operated with fairly sophisticated payment
systems and also served as international financial markets.[^61] With
regards to both these aspects of the fairs, the *cambium* contracts and
the *lettres de foire*, as mentioned in the previous section, would have
been important financial instruments. Later in the thirteenth century
and into the early fourteenth century, bills of exchange would have also
played some role.

Let's first turn to the payment systems that operated at the fairs.

At the end of the twelfth century, Europe had a silver economy, one
spurred on by its ample silver deposits, particularly in central Europe.
Any payments by coins and bullion would have, thus, specifically been in
silver coins and silver bullion. While Africa had substantial gold
deposits throughout the High Middle Ages and this gold circulated
throughout North Africa and the Middle East, it did not extensively
overflow into Christian Europea for much of the High Middle Ages. It was
not until 1252 that sufficient gold flowed into Genoa and Florence that
we see the minting of major gold coins in Europe, the genovino and the
fiorino d'oro (also gold florin or florentine florin). While we, indeed,
see some evidence of gold, including the Italian coins, penetrating the
Fairs of Champagne in the second half of the thirteenth century, the
predominant physical payment method would still have consisted of silver
coins and bullion.[^62]

The general impression is, however, that little silver actually changed
hands during commercial transactions at the fairs. Many purchases of
goods, instead, were made through credit in some form or another.[^63]
It is also quite clear that each fair functioned as a **clearing house**
after all the commercial transactions had been completed, that is, as an
institution which netted credit transactions to make a more simplified
set of final transactions. After determining a more simplified set of
transactions, the remaining obligations would be settled.[^64] This
**clearing and settlement** system was regulated by the fair
authorities.[^65] While the operational details of the system are
largely absent from the literature, we can broadly piece together how it
might have operated.

To start, perhaps not every credit-based commercial transaction at a
fair would have been processed by the clearing and settlement system. In
some cases, sellers probably just agreed to extend credit to buyers
beyond the period of the fair, perhaps to be settled at a next fair or
at some other date and location. These transactions would have mostly
proceeded through paper financial instruments (i.e., the *cambium*
contract, the *lettre de foire*, or the bill of exchange). In some
cases, merchants might have also directly extended credit beyond the
period of a fair merely through book entries. In these credit-based
commercial transactions, the merchants would have generally used
witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical
authorities.[^66]

So, while some credit-based commercial transactions during a particular
fair perhaps fell outside the scope of its clearing and settlement
system, the majority of such transactions likely fell within its scope.
It seems that merchants, in fact, kept track of the liabilities incurred
by the relevant credit transactions, at least at some point, through
documents called *carte*.[^67] The credit-based commercial transactions
at a particular fair processed by its clearance and settlement system
would have been of two main types: those where the merchants extended
credit directly, and those were an intermediary deposit banker was
involved.

When merchants arranged commercial exchanges on credit directly, in many
cases they probably just made book entries for the transaction to be
settled at the end of the fair.[^68] They would have, then, created a
*carte* as evidence for the debt under the presence of witnesses. In
some cases perhaps only drawing up a *carte* was sufficient without
making corresponding book entries. It is unlikely that the merchants
used any more formal paper financial instruments for direct credit
transactions that fell under the scope of the clearing and settlement
system.

Many credit-based commercial transactions instead probably utilized an
intermediary deposit banker.[^69] The Fairs at Champagne were marked by
the presence of specialized proprietors, mostly Italian, that focused
precisely on (physical) currency exchange and deposit banking. Rather
than the merchants arranging a credit relationship directly, the deposit
banker could assume responsibility for the obligation by extending
credit to the creditor and accepting the indebtedness of the debtor.
Evidence of the transaction was probably also recorded with a *carte*.
In this arrangement, the deposit banker would have assumed
responsibility for honoring the money due to the creditor at a fee.

Trade at the Fairs of Champagne proceeded in structured segments.
Notably, the northern merchants, mostly from Flanders and France, first
sold their wool and cloths to the Italian merchants, before the latter
sold their spices, silks, and other luxury goods to them. As the Italian
merchants were the first to make purchases, their initial credit
transactions via deposit bankers would have only been as debtors.
Contrary to what one might expect, it does not appear that the Italian
merchants deposited any specie at the beginning of a fair with the
bankers to reduce the risks for the latter. The deposit bankers, in
other words, were extending the Italian merchants significant credit in
the early stages. Later in each fair, any Italian merchant could reduce
their debt burden, and potentially build themselves up to a credit
position, through the sale of their own goods.

The appearance of deposit bankers at these fairs should not surprise us
that much. The merchants probably also made credit arrangements with
each other directly. But the bankers, as argued by Sangster,[^70]
generally would have enjoyed more trust within an environment with
strangers. They would have reduced the financial risks for the
merchants. Credit transactions were probably also conducted more
efficiently via deposit bankers. Merchants directly engaging in a credit
relationship would have probably placed a heavier burden on involving
witnesses such as notaries, fair wardens, or ecclesiastical authorities.

At the end of each fair, all these commercial transactions that involved
credit merely for the duration of the fair were, then, cleared and
settled. While we do not have any detailed description of the procedures
involved, it is not difficult to imagine how this clearing and
settlement period might have roughly progressed.

When deposit bankers were involved in a credit-based commercial
transaction, they would have just recorded it in their accounting
journal. In modern accounting, we keep running overviews of accounts
using the accounting ledger. But such overviews were not yet used in the
late thirteenth century.[^71] So the deposit bankers probably just
tallied all the transactions for their clients only at the end of the
fair. With final positions for all clients in hand, the bankers would
have compared each other's books. As the bankers surely also had
accounts with each other, they could have further simplified the
obligations existing between them at the end of the fair. The bankers
and the merchants probably would have also included credit-based
transactions made directly between the merchants as part of the overall
simplification process.

Much time was probably spent not just on simplifying resultant
transactions, but on verifying and confirming the records held by
merchants and deposit bankers. Any debts outstanding at the end of the
clearing period between the merchants and bankers could, then, be
settled by various methods. According to Kohn,[^72] if a merchant had a
debt to a banker, he might settle it in cash, but more likely would
carry it over to the next fair as an overdraft or borrow deposits from
another merchant with a credit balance. For merchants with a credit
balance, they could have been paid in specie or allowed to loan their
credit to another merchant in debt to the deposit banker. Surely, as
suggested by both Face and Sangster,[^73] many of these outstanding
debts between merchants and bankers as well as between merchants
themselves would have also been settled with paper financial instruments
(i.e., *cambium* contracts, *lettres de foire*, and bills of exchange),
typically due at the next fair.

Let's now turn to subject of the Fairs of Champagne as international
financial markets.

First, obligations from paper financial instruments were often settled
at the fairs (and probably from mere book credit as well). In some
cases, these obligations may have arisen just out of a need for a
general loan on the part of the borrower. But particularly with
merchants, many of these debts were incurred during the regular course
of business. A Genoese merchant in the thirteenth century, for example,
was much more likely to buy silk, spices, and other wares through being
the taker on a *cambium* contract than with cash. The merchants would
have agreed on a total price and just specified on the contract that the
merchant accepted goods for the value of the price. Given the focal
point of the Fairs of Champagne for trade, the merchants often agreed to
settle such debts at one of the upcoming fairs, not in the least because
that is typically when the wares just bought on credit would be sold.

Probably, the clearing and settlement system at a fair did not just
process the majority of the credit-based commercial transactions
incurred specifically during the course of the fair. More than likely,
other outstanding debts due at the fairs would have been processed as
well. Given all these various debts and obligations, and the potential
for conflicts in the records to arise, it is hardly surprising that the
clearing and settlement period of each fair has been estimated to take
several weeks.[^74]

The Fairs of Champagne were also an attractive venue for obtaining
straightforward loans outside of the context of purchases on credit.
Sometimes these loans might have been extended to merchants that
required capital, either for business elsewhere at the fair or outside
the scope of the fair. It is also clear, however, that loans were
acquired at the fairs by outsiders. These borrowers would have normally
been aristocrats, clergy, or other high-status individuals. The counts
in Champagne frequently guaranteed such loans, as the merchants and
bankers were hesitant to extend loans without such guarantees.[^75] On
the flip side, people that had excess funds came to the fairs to loan
funds to the bankers and merchants who, in turn, would have loaned those
funds out to others, of course, pocketing an interest rate differential.
Loans incurred in this manner, as has already been suggested above, were
probably often settled during the clearing and settlement phase of some
subsequent fair.

An interesting and educational question is how the loans above were
granted exactly. Currency exchange was a substantial part of the
business of the deposit bankers, and loans would certainly also have
been extended by them through specie. Illustratively, Bautier notes that
in the second half of the thirteenth century and into the fourteenth
century, merchants sometimes would use the fairs to obtain loans in
specie from the bankers and then travel to Flanders to purchase textiles
in cash from producers. Those textiles would, then, be transported to
the next fair or, particularly later on, to Paris, from where they would
be transported to Italy.[^76] The debts corresponding to these specie
loans could have been managed through the books of the banker or via
formal paper instruments. Merchants at the fairs, not just the bankers,
would have probably also extended specie loans either through book debts
or, more likely, paper instruments.

But surely not all loans would have been granted in specie. Merchants
and bankers could have also created a corresponding credit in their
books for the borrower, instead of handing over specie. The utility of
such credits to a borrower would have depended on the financial and
commercial network of the merchant or banker, and the purposes of the
loan. In any case, we know that in later fairs, loans were increasingly
extended in the form of book credit, rather than specie. The same is
true for deposits. Looking at the books of the Salviati firm at the Lyon
Fairs between 1544 and 1549, for example, Matringe finds that deposits
and loans by the bankers only involved specie 7.3 percent and 7 percent
of the time, respectively.[^77] And Parker,[^78] for instance, notes
that between 1580 and 1620, about fifty million scudi were exchanged
every year at the fairs of Besançon-Piacenza. Yet, illustratively, only
an estimated 100,000 to 400,000 scudi were physically exchanged during
each fair, which was held four times a year.

After the decline of the Fairs of Champagne, large fairs that
facilitated interzonal and interregional trade and that served as
financial markets continued elsewhere. These include, notably, the
Geneva fairs that were prominent in the fourteenth and fifteenth
centuries, the Lyon fairs that were prominent in the late fifteenth and
sixteenth centuries, the Besançon-Piacenza fairs that were prominent in
the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and the Frankfurt fairs that were
prominent in the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries.

These large international fairs seem to have increasingly served a
financial function. This included the extension of loans, primarily
through bills of exchange, and the clearance and settlement of bills of
exchange and other types of debts. Even though the institutional details
differed,[^79] the broad lines of these events had already been set out
at the Fairs of Champagne. As noted by Denzel,[^80] in the sixteenth
century, the timing of international fairs was coordinated in such a way
so that they provided a "permanent 'pan-European' money, credit, and
exchange market in which merchant-bankers could conduct their business
and financial transactions almost all year round". Baumgartl, in turn,
concludes that "\[a\]t the height of their importance from around 1580
to 1620, the Bisenzone fairs were probably the largest capital market of
the time and also the clearing center for European payment
transactions".[^81]

At the end of the fourteenth and into the fifteenth and sixteenth
centuries, there was also an explosion of regional fairs throughout the
continent, despite the impact on the population from the epidemics,
famines, and wars of the Late Medieval Period. Epstein attributes this
growth in regional fairs to increasing economic specialization and the
provision of the right institutional support by increasingly powerful
late medieval states.[^82] Around the year 1500, an estimated 5,000
periodic markets and fairs were taking place in roughly 1,500 towns and
cities in the Holy Roman Empire.[^83]

At the end of the Early Middle Ages, perhaps at best a few of the larger
cities such as Rome were developed enough to sustain a lively commercial
area. In any case, no cities or towns could have supported the large,
wholesale trading activities of merchants. So, as trade expanded during
the High Middle Ages, fairs became increasingly important concentrations
of goods and capital. By the end of the High Middle Ages, various towns
and cities began to show vibrant commercial areas. By the late
thirteenth century, cities such as Pisa and Genoa were open for business
practically year-round.[^84] Wholesale trade and financing was
increasingly been attracted to these commercial areas and loosely
organized bourse trading developed during the Late Middle Ages.
Institutionalized exchange trading, then, first developed in Antwerp in
the course of the sixteenth century. As trade and finance increasingly
established in certain commercial nerve centers, it increasingly put
pressure on the relative importance of the medieval trade fair.

## 4. The Commercial Revolution

At the end of the Dark Ages, much of the practical activity of the
merchants consisted of travelling with their goods. Especially over
longer distances, travel typically occurred within larger groups, often
armed, due to the hazards and risks involved. Merchants may have had one
or two assistants, and formed partnerships with other merchants
typically for the length of a single expedition. We can obtain a view of
the nature of trading from Kohn's description of that activity in early
twelfth-century Genoa:[^85]

> Genoa traded by sea with the eastern Mediterranean and by land with
> the Low Countries and the Fairs of Champagne. In both cases, the trade
> was conducted by traveling merchants. The Mediterranean trade was in
> the hands of the Genoese themselves. Ships for Syria, for example,
> left Genoa in the fall and returned in the Spring: one recorded fleet
> of three ships carried some one hundred merchants. The overland trade
> was largely in the hand of merchants from Arras, who traveled south,
> and of merchants from Asti, who traveled north. Both groups bought
> cloth in the North and carried it south in caravans to Genoa. There
> they sold it to Genoese merchants who specialized in the export of
> cloth to various Mediterranean destinations. The caravan merchants
> then purchased Mediterranean goods, mainly spices and silks, and
> returned with them to the North.

This is not to say that, at the end of the Early Middle Ages, every
merchant involved in a trade expedition actually travelled with the
caravans. Retired merchants formed partnerships with younger merchants
merely to contribute capital, connections, and expertise. Similarly,
even working merchants sometimes acted only as investors through such
partnerships. We certainly see such relationships already by the late
eleventh century,[^86] and they more than likely existed earlier. The
main point is that, towards the end of the Early Middle Ages, goods were
transported largely by merchants in caravans, and any active merchant
would have spent a substantial amount of their time traveling in such
caravans.

Certainly by the end of the thirteenth century, however, our view of
commercial activities would be far too simple with only the image of the
traditional **caravan merchant** in our toolbox. Richard Face offers an
illuminating discussion in this regard.[^87] According to him, certainly
by the end of the thirteenth century and probably much earlier, the
traditional notion of the caravan merchant is inadequate to describe the
organization of trade at the Fairs of Champagne, particularly with
regards to the northern Italian merchants.

Specifically, by the end of the thirteenth century, the Italian
merchants frequently employed resident partners (often family),
employees, and agents to manage trade activities at the fairs on their
behalf. The ability to conduct business over distance through partners
and agents was supported by improved communication networks. By the
second half of the thirteenth century, various courier
services—operated in-house, by guilds, or towns—were running between
the Italian business centers and the fairs. These transported market
news, credit instruments, and other information in about half the time
required for a caravan of goods to travel the distance. The
transportation of goods was also increasingly facilitated by specialized
service providers, rather than personally managed by the merchants. That
transport occurred predominantly overland by crossing the Alps. Finally,
trade through credit at the fairs, as we have already seen, was
pervasive. Face notes the following on the record of *cambium contracts*
due at the Fairs of Champagne:[^88]

> In the contracts which record loans in the form of exchanges to be
> repaid at the fairs of Champagne, there appears a freak once in a
> while which specifies that repayment should be made in gold or silver
> coin and not in credits at a banker's table. But the vast majority of
> these documents took for granted payment by paper.

All these factors ensured much less intense travel to the Fairs of
Champagne by the merchants themselves than we would expect given just
the picture of the traditional caravan merchant.

The developments at the Fairs of Champagne as illustrated by Face were
transforming trade throughout Europe. By the end of the thirteenth
century, trade in Europe was increasingly making way for the **sedentary
merchant**. Rather than travelling from place to place with their own
wares, these merchants operated from their headquarters in a particular
city or town and used partners (often family members), employees, and
agents located elsewhere to manage interregional and long-distance
trade.[^89]

The first sedentary merchants were mostly Italians. As Venetians,
Genoese, Pisans, and others increasingly came to dominate the
Mediterranean trade in the High Middle Ages, they began to establish
trading enclaves. This process started as early as the eleventh century.
The trading enclaves were established on other parts of the Italian
Peninsula, but also in far-away places like Acre, Alexandria, and
Constantinople. Such enclaves included living quarters and storage
facilities. Arrangements were made with rulers regarding rules, taxes,
and duties.

In the thirteenth century, the Italian branching practices spread
through other parts of Europe. Piacentene and Sienese trading companies
already began to maintain representatives in Genoa, Marseilles, Bruges,
Paris, and elsewhere from the early thirteenth century onwards.[^90]
According to Herlihy, Pistoia's merchant bankers began branching in the
second half of the thirteenth century, both in Italy and elsewhere in
Europe.[^91] Supposedly, the first Italian representatives were
operating in London by 1220.[^92] And by the end of the thirteenth
century, many of the major Italian companies had branch offices in
Paris, so that representatives could easily travel to the nearby Fairs
of Champagne.[^93]

By contrast, at the end of the thirteenth century, the merchants from
particular towns and cities in Flanders still commonly congregated in
the traditional caravans to travel together to the fairs. These caravans
were accompanied by armed men, a commander, and a standard-bearer,
though the nature of the procession may have become more ceremonial than
a necessity, as the roads had become much safer to travel by that
time.[^94]

The establishment of representation in Flanders by the Italian companies
apparently came somewhat late, namely in the early fourteenth
century.[^95] That said, the foremost European colony of the Italians
was undoubtedly Bruges and it became the economic heart of northwestern
Europe during the Late Middle Ages. Various factors probably contributed
to the decline of the Fairs of Champagne. It seems, for example, that
policies by the public authorities were the major factor both in the
rise of the fairs and their initial decline around 1300.[^96] One common
argument, however, is that the establishment of permanent trade networks
between northern Italy and Flanders also reduced their importance.[^97]

It is difficult to overstate the influence of Italians in international
trade and finance during this period. During fourteenth and fifteenth
century Bruges, for instance, the native upper class "was not made up of
merchants, save for a few exceptions, but of brokers, inn-keepers,
*drapiers*, and commission agents".[^98] Native merchants were largely
absent from the upper class because international trade through the city
was facilitated by companies from Venice, Genoa, Florence, Lucca,
Sienna, and elsewhere in Italy. The same was true for the other
commercial and financial centers of southern and northwestern Europe.
Lyon, for instance, in 1557 apparently had 183 foreign merchants
permanently residing there. Over eighty percent of them were Italians,
over a quarter of the Italian merchants coming from Florence.[^99]

According to Norman Gras, Europe transformed from a **petty capitalist
system** to that of a **commercial capitalist system** (or **mercantile
capitalist system**) during the High and Late Middle Ages. The key
figure in this new capitalist system was the (Italian) sedentary
merchant. He describes the transition and the role of the sedentary
merchant as follows:[^100]

> \[Petty capitalism\] was a system of small shopkeepers, storekeepers,
> hucksters, pedlars, and traveling merchants\.... The new mercantile
> capitalism grew upon the backs of petty capitalists. It was a system
> of wider trade, larger amounts of capital, and unprecedented growth of
> control, of policy-formulation, and of management, all centering in
> one office—a counting house. The central figure was the sedentary
> merchant, a merchant too wise, too occupied, too economical to travel.
> His distant connections were maintained by agents, traveling or
> resident. This sedentary merchant grew up in the ancient period but
> apparently had a short life in that era. In the later period he
> flourished from about 1300 to 1800. The outstanding sedentary
> merchants were called merchant princes. Some mercantile families
> actually attained political power and princely status. Their tastes
> and culture were the object of public admiration and their friendship
> sought by princes and emperors far and wide. Holding the
> purse-strings, they were often the arbiters of considerable
> international contests.[^101]

Gras argues further that it was really the sedentary merchant's knack
for business administration that supported the advancements and
civilization of the Late Middle Ages and the early modern period.[^102]
The period of commercial capitalism only came to an end with the
Industrial Revolution. The merchant's leading role in the European
economy, therefore, only ended with the rise of the industrialist.

The transformation from the traveling to the sedentary merchant was so
profound that it has been dubbed a **commercial revolution** by Raymond
De Roover: "a complete or drastic change in the methods of doing
business or in the organization of business enterprise just as an
industrial revolution means a complete change in the methods of
production, for example, the introduction of power-driven
machinery".[^103] According to De Roover, various factors enabled the
rise of the sedentary merchant, including the formation of long-term
partnerships, the invention of the bill of exchange, greater security
for both land and sea travel, the introduction of maritime insurance,
and the development of bookkeeping techniques, particularly double entry
accounting.[^104]

While the establishment of permanent trade networks between Flanders and
northern Italy may have partially contributed to the decline of the
Fairs of Champagne, as explained above, the medieval fairs certainly did
not disappear entirely from the European landscape for trade and
finance. Quite the contrary. While permanent trade and financial
networks became increasingly important, we continue to see the medieval
fairs thrive: a testament to the expansion of trade and finance in the
period.

## 5. The Hanseatic League

The Italians came to dominate trade and finance in the Mediterranean
region and Western Europe during the High and Late Middle Ages. The
Dutch, English, Spanish, and others were, of course, also involved in
trade. The closest rival to the Italians in terms of scale and
organization were the merchants of the Hanseatic League. These were
mostly Germans, but the league also included Dutch, Danish, and
merchants of other origins.[^105]

The German term **hansa** was the name for certain associations of
travelling merchants doing business in foreign cities. After the Viking
Age, the Scandinavians had continued to dominate the waters of the
Baltic Sea militarily and commercially. But during the High Middle Ages,
the Germans became increasingly influential in the Baltic Sea. The
German merchant associations were often supported by Teutonic Knights in
establishing trading posts. They aggressively founded numerous trading
settlements, including in Latvia, Estonia, and Lithuania. In other
places such as Poland, the German merchants settled more peacefully.
Trading posts were even founded in Scandinavian towns and cities such as
Bergen. By the late thirteenth century, Scandinavian naval power had
essentially collapsed and the Germans ruled the Baltic Sea both
militarily and commercially.

Increasing cooperation between the hanse eventually grew into a
commercial and defensive federation of linked towns known as the
**Hanseatic League**. The League lasted into the early modern period,
but its peak occurred during the Late Middle Ages. There were various
degrees of membership and the pool of member towns obviously changed
throughout the league's existence. In general, however, members were in
northern Germany and the rest of the Baltic region, as well as in
Western Germany and the northeastern Low Countries. Core members were
towns such as Lübeck, Rostock, Hamburg, and Cologne. Most of the
merchants and associates that operated within the league were,
naturally, German.

The Hanseatic league also had trading outposts in many cities and towns
that were not direct members. These were called *kontors*, and included
such places as London, Bruges, Bergen, and Novgorod. In London, the
*kontor* was famously known as the steelyard. Often the Hanseatic
merchants operated within particular areas of the city, and they would
have formed enclaves similar to the Italians. In some of the *kontors*,
trading activities were conducted under fairly risky circumstances.
These trading posts were much smaller than their Italian counterparts.
Even the major *kontors* of London, Bruges, and Bergen were probably
only comparable the earliest Italian enclaves in their size.

Overall, the merchants of the Hanseatic League were in many regards not
quite as developed as their Italian counterparts. While trade indeed
became more sedentary over time, that transformation was less developed
than among the Italian merchants. Large trading companies did not arise.
In general, the business techniques of the Hanseatic merchants also
remained behind those of the Italians, at least until the sixteenth
century. Notably, banking and money markets in the Baltic region were
much less developed than in the Mediterranean and much of Western
Europe. As a consequence, moving funds within and outside of the
Hanseatic region often occurred through shipments of specie and bullion.
