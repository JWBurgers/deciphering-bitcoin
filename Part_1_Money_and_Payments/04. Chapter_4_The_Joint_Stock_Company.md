# Chapter 4: The Joint Stock Company

The modern history of money, payments, and banking is closely
intertwined with the evolution of the joint stock company into the
modern corporation. After having broadly sketched the economic,
political, and social changes that marked Europe from the High Middle
Ages to the modern age, we will now take a closer look at the birth of
the modern corporation. In this chapter, we address the nature, origins,
and importance of the joint stock company. In *Chapter 5*, we will
examine the development of several key features of the modern
corporation over time, including exchange trading, registration regimes,
and limited liability.

We introduce some financial jargon in *Section 1*. The discussion is
organized around three main concepts: equity capital, securities, and
derivatives. In *Section 2*, we describe how joint commercial ventures
were organized in pre-early-modern Europe. We, then, in *Section 3*,
characterize the nature of the joint stock company and its appearance in
England and the Dutch Republic in the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries. We draw a distinction between incorporated joint stock
companies and unincorporated joint stock companies. Finally, in *Section
4*, we examine whether Genoa's House of Saint George—Europe's first
significant public bank—was a medieval joint stock company.

# 1. Equity Capital, Securities, and Derivatives

We begin this chapter by introducing some financial jargon. We structure
the discussion around three main concepts: equity capital, securities,
and derivatives. They are treated in order.

At the core of describing the performance of modern commercial ventures
are so-called **financial statements**. These are standardized financial
overviews that give government agencies, investors, and others insight
into the performance of a company. While legal reporting requirements
and customs vary across jurisdictions, companies in most of the
world—from proprietorships to publicly traded corporations—are
typically required to produce, at a minimum, periodic balance sheets and
income statements. These are the core financial statements in modern
accounting.

The **income statement** indicates the **net income** of an
organization. This is the difference between all the income generated
and the costs incurred over a certain accounting period, typically a
quarter or a year. The net income is also called net earnings, net
profit, or the bottom line (as it's typically the last line on an income
statement). It is sometimes also just simply called profit, a term that
usually denotes net profit and not what is known as gross profit, which
captures operating income minus the cost of goods sold.[^1]

The income statement normally only gives an indirect impression of a
company's money flows in the form of credit transfers and cash payments.
First, the costs in a certain period are not necessarily equivalent to
all expenses. Some expenses are namely treated as capital investments
rather than costs. Those investment expenses are then distributed as
costs over multiple quarters and years. Second, the expenses incurred in
a particular accounting period are also not necessarily paid in the
period of their occurrence. And finally, income generated in a period is
not necessarily equivalent to money received in that period. Invoices
already sent out, for example, may not have been paid yet. A different
financial statement, namely the **cash flow statement**, is used
especially by larger companies to capture the money flows over a quarter
or year.

The **balance sheet** is the statement of all the assets of a company at
a particular point in time, typically at the end of a quarter or year,
with an indication of how those assets were acquired, either through
debt or capital investments. Balance sheets are governed by what is
known as the **accounting equation**, which states the following:[^2]

Assets = Liabilities + Equity Capital

Essentially, the accounting equation means that all company assets were
acquired either by "borrowing" in a very loose sense (e.g., formal
loans, bank overdrafts, unpaid invoices, unpaid taxes) or by capital
investments. The latter is broadly determined by the investment of
external funds from the owners into the business as well as any profits
that have not been distributed via dividends. The term **equity
capital** denotes the sum of these capital investments.

Next, we turn to the concept of securities. We might broadly define a
**financial asset** as any type of asset that obtains its value from a
monetary contractual agreement. Financial assets can be distinguished
from **non-financial assets** such as land, buildings, equipment,
goodwill, patents, and trademarks. A **security** commonly refers to any
tradeable financial asset which represents an ownership position or a
creditor relationship, or derivatives based on such financial assets.
These are typically distinguished as **equity securities**, **debt
securities**, **hybrid securities**, and **derivative securities**.

The equity security category includes common and preferred stock, but
can also include more exotic financial assets such as interests in real
estate investment trusts. The category of debt securities includes
financial assets such as government and corporate bonds, debentures,
commercial paper, and promissory notes. Some products do not fit neatly
into either category. An example is the convertible loan, a loan to a
company of which the debt outstanding can be converted into equity
according to certain rules. These types of securities are sometimes
called hybrid securities.

Shares in companies or trusts that pool various securities can also be
called securities. Technically, these are equity securities though they
commonly invest mostly or exclusively in debt securities. For example,
one of the most common investment funds in the United States is the
**mutual fund**. It is the primary type of open-end fund,[^3] one of the
three main types of investment funds identified by the SEC (closed-end
funds and unit investment trusts are the other two types). Mutual funds
have a long-term time horizon and tend to invest mostly in public stocks
and bonds, though they could also invest in commodities, futures,
options, and so on. That mutual funds are open-ended means that shares
in the fund are not fixed and can be created and redeemed throughout the
fund's life. Shares in mutual funds are not bought and sold on an
exchange, but instead can only be bought and sold once per day.
Regardless of the underlying investments, a share in a mutual fund is
technically an equity security.

Mortgage- and other asset-backed securities as well as collateralized
debt obligations are, in turn, typically classified as debt securities.

Loans such as mortgages are not easily transferrable. The concept of
**securitization** refers to the process of turning the income from
loans into tradeable financial assets. So-called **mortgage-backed
securities** are types of bonds. Companies that issue these bonds use
the proceeds to purchase a pool of mortgages. The owners of these bonds
are then entitled to cash flows from mortgage payments and any proceeds
from foreclosures. In the United States, companies that issue
mortgage-back securities are typically owned by investment banks or
certain government-sponsored enterprises like **Fannie Mae** (Federal
National Mortgage Association), **Freddie Mac** (Federal Home Loan
Mortgage Corporation), and **Ginnie Mae** (Government National Mortgage
Association). These institutions normally do not deal with mortgage
clients directly, but purchase the underlying mortgages from banks and
other financial institutions.

Many securities are created by a similar principle as mortgage-backed
securities, but on the basis of car and student loans, equipment leases,
credit card receivables, and so on. The general term for all such
financial instruments is **asset-backed securities**. Similar bond-like
securities that are based on multiple types of debt assets are
standardly called **collateralized debt obligations**. These bonds can
be backed by various types of loan obligations or even asset-backed
securities directly. But they can also be backed by other types of debt
such as government and corporate bonds. Both asset-backed securities and
collateralized debt obligations are typically structured into
**tranches**, or classes that come with different risk and return
profiles. As asset-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations
are like bonds, we can also categorize them as debt securities.

Finally, we turn to the subject of derivatives. We call a financial
asset a **derivative** when its value *derives* from the exchange price
of an underlying financial asset or commodity, or from some other
financial or non-financial indexes or indicators. Common derivatives
include forwards, futures, and options.

A contract between two parties which obliges one party to purchase a
specific commodity or financial asset at a predetermined price from the
other party at some point in the future is either a **forward contract**
or a **futures contract**.[^4] The former in case the contract is
customizable and privately negotiated; the latter in case the contract
is standardized and tradeable on an organized exchange.

Futures and forward contracts are usually settled in cash, rather than
in the underlying commodity or financial asset. For instance, suppose
that party A agrees to buy from party B one barrel of oil for \$100 on
December 31, but the contract stipulates that it should be settled in
cash. Suppose that a barrel on December 31 is \$120. Rather than sell a
barrel of oil for \$100 to party A, party B just pays party A \$20. The
monetary benefit of the contract is essentially the same for party A, as
if she had been given a barrel of oil for \$100 and sold it for \$120
(at least if we assume good liquidity of the barrel of oil).

Futures are not exactly traded like stocks. In the example above, party
A creates an offer to buy a barrel of oil for \$100 on December 31,
which party B accepts. That matching process creates the futures
contract at that point. Suppose that party A at some later point thinks
the price of a barrel of oil is, contrary to her initial expectations,
likely to be lower than \$100 on December 31 and that party C thinks the
opposite. Suppose that party C offers to purchase party A's position for
\$98 and the latter accepts. In that case, party A's position in the
contract is transferred to party C.

Futures and forwards always require execution. An **option**, on the
other hand, grants one party the right, but not the obligation, to buy
or sell some financial asset or commodity at a particular price (called
the **strike price**) within a certain period of time or on some
particular date. The other party has an obligation to follow the
decision of the option holder. They accept that obligation on the basis
of a sum of money, or **premium**, which is paid by the buyer when the
contract is created. With forwards and futures, by contrast, no money
exchanges hands between the two parties when the contract is created. As
with futures, both parties can opt to sell their position to another
party on an exchange. Sale of a forward contract is typically more
complicated.

According to our concepts of a security and a derivative, it follows
that some, though not all, futures and options are indeed also
securities. Given that forwards are usually difficult, and sometimes
impossible to transfer depending on the terms, they are not really
securities. In addition, many options and futures are not based on the
prices of debt or equity securities. Most obviously, futures and options
also exist for commodities such as in the example of a barrel of oil
above. A commodity like oil is not a financial asset; hence, derivatives
based on commodities are not securities. In this light, it is fairly
common for practitioners to also speak of **derivative securities**, or
those derivatives based on debt and equity securities.

It should be noted that particularly the language on derivatives and
securities is not universal in the financial sector. Those terms are
used in a varied manner by practitioners and also vary across, and
sometimes within, legal regimes. Our characterization of a security
above, for example, is surely somewhat narrower than that maintained by
SEC.[^5]

# 2. Pre-modern commercial enterprises

In *Chapter 1*, we described the modern corporation as any commercial
enterprise with legal personhood, limited liability, entity shielding, a
distinction between owners and management, and the transferability of
shares. It is a common legal form for commercial enterprises requiring
the pooling of equity capital and the sharing of risks and profits.
Although isolated precursors have been identified in medieval
Europe—among them the Mills of Toulouse and the House of Saint
George—recognizable early forms of the modern business corporation
only began to emerge more prominently in the early modern era. These
early business corporations are often called joint stock companies.

Without the business corporation as an organizational form, how were
commercial enterprises that required the pooling of equity capital and
the sharing of risks and profits organized?

In pre-modern Europe, longer-term commercial enterprises that required
equity pooling were frequently structured around family relationships.
Commonly they were run by a few key family members. Sometimes these
family companies also included outsiders. The Medici firm of the
fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, for example, also included outsiders
in prominent positions, even though the family members were the core
decision-makers within the company.[^6] The importance of family ties in
structuring longer-term joint commercial enterprises was hardly unique
to Europe. In the pre-modern world, the institution of the family firm
emerged endogenously in various places throughout Eurasia.[^7]

Shorter-term joint commercial enterprises—such as a single trade
expedition—would often also involve partners with family ties. But
they were more likely to involve pooling capital beyond family members,
and often were been arranged between entrepreneurs without any familial
ties at all. Even so, such partnerships were still typically marked by
strong personal bonds through origin, ethnicity, religion, or membership
in some other form of shared community. Pre-modern commercial
enterprises that required equity pooling were based overwhelmingly on
personal ties, rather than on impersonal cooperation underpinned by
sound legal and institutional frameworks.[^8]

Customs and sanctions played a substantial role in enabling pre-modern
joint commercial enterprises as well as trade and commerce more
generally. To varying degrees, operations with pooled capital would have
also been shaped by laws and institutions. In those contexts where
really distinctive legal institutions developed, joint commercial
enterprises typically resembled, speaking somewhat loosely, some form of
partnership.

The Romans are renowned for their well-developed legal frameworks. The
main legal device for engaging in joint commercial ventures was the
**societas**. Contributions from partners came in the form of money,
skills and labor, or in-kind capital such as goods, rights, or claims;
the contributions did not have to be equal. A *societas* typically only
had a few members, and any partner could generally dissolve the
partnership at will, incurring damages if stipulations on the duration
had been included within the partnership's contract. The *societas*
differed from modern partnerships in many respects. All partners had to
sign contracts to be bound by them. Obligations for debts incurred by
the *societas* were also pro-rata, not joint and several. While Rome had
well-developed laws on the *societas*, it is important to realize that
these laws codified practices that often went back many centuries. The
origins of the laws and customs on partnerships really predate their
recording.[^9]

From the High Middle Ages onwards, merchant companies were often
organized as some form of general partnership. The main model for such
partnerships was the *compagnia*, already introduced in *Chapter 1*. It
first emerged for the merchant houses of Genoa and Tuscany during the
thirteenth century. Unlike with the Roman *societas*, the *compagnia*
was marked by joint and several liability; over time, the legal form
also adopted **mutual agency**, the ability of one partner to enter into
an obligation on behalf of all the partners. The shares of the
*compagnia* were illiquid and there were few protections against
partners or their creditors dissolving the partnership. As with the
Roman *societas*, the *compagnia* always had a definite ending date.
Typically they lasted anywhere from a few years to well over a decade.
After closure of a *compagnia*, a new one was usually started with the
same or a similar name and an identical or nearly identical group of
partners. As the shares of a *compagnia* were illiquid, their limited
duration gave the opportunity for (partial) exits from the
enterprise.[^10]

The *compagnia* was the main model for companies focused on trade and
finance into the early modern period. The Fuggers, for instance, were a
powerful South German family of merchant bankers in the Late Middle Ages
and the early modern era. While the family firm was renewed and revised
numerous times throughout the centuries, the **Fugger Company** was
generally structured along the lines of a *compagnia* for most of its
life.[^11] For another example, when deposit banking became popular in
England during the seventeenth century, most deposit banks were formed
as small general partnerships.[^12] As many of these small deposit banks
were originally started by goldsmiths, they are known as **goldsmith
banks**.

While the nomenclature varied across jurisdictions and time periods,
historians commonly use the Italian term **commenda** for another
important type of partnership contract in medieval and early modern
Europe. The oldest known references to the *commenda* are from Arab
Muslim writers in the seventh century.[^13] The first known reference to
the *commenda* in Europe is from tenth century Venice.[^14] We have
notarial acts for such a contract in Europe from as early as the
eleventh century.[^15] The oldest known European legal text pertaining
to the *commenda* is from Pisa in 1156, the *constitutum usus*.[^16] By
the end of the twelfth century, the *commenda* was very popular in
northern Italy and other areas of Mediterranean Europe. Commenda
contracts are, for instance, abundant in Genoese notarial records from
the late twelfth century.[^17] Originally the *commenda* was only
utilized for single maritime trade expeditions. Over time, however, a
longer duration of a *commenda* became more common and the contract also
saw uses beyond maritime trade.[^18]

Historians generally believe that the Arabian *commenda* originated
before the Islamic period. Some believe it originated on the Arabian
Peninsula in pre-Islamic times; others argue that it was based on
earlier practices elsewhere in the Eastern Mediterranean. The opinions
of historians also vary with regards to whether the European *commenda*
was imported from the Arabian Peninsula or developed independently in
Italy. In any case, variations on the institution eventually migrated to
most of Eurasia from the Arabian Peninsula and Italy. During the early
modern period, particularly with the rise of English and Dutch joint
stock companies, the *commenda* began to lose relevance.[^19]

The archetypal European *commenda* was a bilateral contract between an
active, traveling partner and a sedentary partner that lasted for a
single maritime trade expedition. The contract organized capital for
purchasing goods to trade as well as to arrange and outfit a ship (often
owned by third parties). Usually, the traveling partner was
predominantly in charge of the trade expedition; sometimes he also
provided capital. The sedentary partner's main role was typically as an
investor of capital; sometimes he also offered expertise and support for
the expedition. Profits were shared according to a formula that depended
on the respective capital contributions of the two partners. By the end
of the High Middle Ages, it was common to see merchants lock-in multiple
bilateral *commenda* for particular expeditions. A key feature of the
*commenda* was that the sedentary partner only risked their capital. The
traveling partner mostly risked only their own capital, if any was
invested, and their labor, at least as long as he stayed within the
mandates of the contract.

The capital investment into a *commenda* contract was generally
illiquid. It still, however, became a popular investment vehicle.
Extensive markets for the *commenda* developed in northern Italy during
the High Middle Ages, particularly in Venice. As these markets
developed, the sedentary partner increasingly became a non-merchant
passive investor.[^20] The sedentary partners faced substantial
challenges in monitoring their investments, and laws and regulations
were passed to prevent abusive practices. Of course, the participants in
these contracts typically had some personal connection such as through
ethnicity or origin. Even though the contracts were normally of short
duration, repeated contracts with particular partners were common.

While the *commenda* has some similarities to the modern limited
partnership, to which it is often compared, it is important to note that
the *commenda* was more a bundle of contracts than a specific legal
entity. The sedentary partner\'s limited risk reflected both their
typical anonymity and physical distance from the venture, and the rule
that the active partner could not borrow beyond the assets contained
within the *commenda*. The limitation in liability was, in other words,
not an inherent feature of a legal entity.[^21]

The *societas*, the *compagnia*, and the *commenda* were generally
illiquid investments. Their temporary nature allowed for (partial)
exits. An interesting more liquid form of investment was a system of
ownership by shares in ships.[^22] As with the *commenda*, the
*societas*, and many other institutions, the origins of this system are
obscure. It seems to have existed already in Venice in the eleventh
century.[^23] Our first documented case of its usage in the northern
trade is from the turn of the twelfth century.[^24] Our most well-known,
early instance of the usage of ship shares, however, comes from Genoa,
where the system first became popular during the second half of the
twelfth century.

In the Genoese version of the system, the shares of ships were called
*luoghi* (Italian) or *loca* (Latin). As many as seventy ownership
shares existed per ship. Apparently, these shares were also bought by
groups, so that individuals sometimes also owned fractions of shares.
Unlike investments in the *societas*, *compagnia*, and *commenda*, the
*luoghi* were highly liquid. They were traded in secondary markets and
used as collateral for loans. They were also invested as capital into
*commenda*, probably to obtain financing or to gain rights for transport
on particular ships. They were even used to pay the wages of mariners
and to purchase goods for export. The ship shares were common
investments for Genoese merchants. Ownership of ship shares was
sometimes necessary to enable the transport of goods on a particular
ship. But the shares also had an investment value. Aristocrats,
artisans, and others also invested in them. Probably from its use in
maritime trade, the Genoese share system of ownership was extended to
other types of joint enterprises. Notable are the use of such shares in
*maone*, private associations that were formed typically for commercial
undertakings, and in *compere*, associations that managed public debts.

Ship ownership was managed by various models from the High Middle Ages
onwards. In some cases, the ship was owned by a rich merchant or a
family company. In other cases, the ship was owned by a
partnership.[^25] Loan syndicates to finance the purchase of a ship were
common. The system of share ownership in ships had the advantage over
other approaches that shareholders could invest small amounts of money
into multiple ships. This reduced exposure to shipwrecks, piracy, and
other seafaring risks and made such investments much more viable. For
Genoese merchants the share system was also a good way to obtain
exposure to the profits from shipping and to gain access to ships for
the transportation of their goods. By the nature of a fixed asset, the
capital was locked in. This capital lock-in was precisely what allowed
extensive secondary markets to develop for ship shares. The liquidity of
these shares would have been an attractive feature for many investors.
While the shareholders had personal liability exposure in principle,
this was limited in practice by specific arrangements just as with the
*commenda*.

The system of share ownership was also used in other Italian cities
besides Genoa and Venice. We know such a system was also used in
northern Europe by the twelfth century.[^26] During the Late Middle
Ages, the share system was a fairly common method for financing the
construction and outfitting of ships in the Hanseatic League areas along
the North Sea and the Baltic Sea. Notably, ship shares were extremely
popular in the Low Countries during the fifteenth, sixteenth, and
seventeenth centuries, particularly in Holland.[^27] In Dutch, this
legal form of organization was called a *partenrederij* and the
shareholders were called *reders*. As the market for maritime insurance
only really developed in the Low Countries during the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries, the division of ships into shares was an important
insurance mechanism.[^28]

In Holland, ships were split into as many as eighty parts, or even
more.[^29] One of the shareholders typically took on the role of
skipper, which also implied he had a representative role for the company
in much of its affairs. Next to the profits he could reap as an owner,
the skipper was also paid a fee for leading expeditions. Sufficiently
many shareholder votes could remove the skipper from his function. One
or more shareholders were also appointed to represent the entire group
in handling the accounting and distribution of profits after each
journey. It seems that all shareholders in these *partenrederijen* had
limited liability. If losses exceed initial investment, maritime law
allowed investors to abandon their shares; the participants also could
not lose more than their initial investment according to customary
law.[^30]

Typically, the *partenrederij* was hired by one or more merchants for
the transportation of their goods. These merchants were sometimes
partial owners of the ship as well. Standardly, capital was collected
from the ship's shareholders to outfit the ship for each journey,
including for provisions, the hiring of a crew, and the payment of the
skipper. Sometimes the client of the company might outfit the ship
partially or entirely on their own. The skipper and crew were normally
allowed some room for trading on their own accounts, and the transport
of their own merchandise was usually free. This was apparently an
important pecuniary motivation for the participation of skippers and
crews in trade expeditions. The *partenrederij* as a whole also
frequently traded on its own account, and sometimes expeditions were
devoted to trading entirely on their own account (notwithstanding the
small provision for the captain and crew members to trade on personal
accounts). While the *partenrederij* was an enduring entity, each
expedition was typically treated as its own discrete commercial venture.
Generally, capital was raised for each expedition anew and profits were
shared at its conclusion.

As a high number of shares for each ship was common in Holland, even
people of modest means could make investments into the shipping
industry. The investors were often people involved with outfitting the
ship, such as lumber dealers, shipbuilders, sailmakers, rope producers,
brewers, and compass makers. Wealthy and smaller merchants also commonly
invested in the market. Family members or personal relationships of the
captain or others involved with the ship were other frequent investors.
There was a very active secondary market in ship shares. Given the
physical depreciation on ships, share prices for any particular ship
typically decreased over time. Any share was strictly tied to a part
ownership of the ship and did not extend to other capital invested by
the share holders for any specific trade expedition. Nevertheless, when
in transit, an appropriate share of the working capital or merchandise
traded on the company's account were sometimes included as part of a
sale.

The share system allowed for building larger ships and reducing overhead
costs. This reduced transportation costs for merchants. Hence, the
system made the Dutch Republic, and Holland in particular, increasingly
competitive with the shipping industry of places like Brugge and
Antwerpen in the Southern Low Countries, where the system of fractional
ownership remained much less developed. Supposedly, the system of ship
ownership was of substantial importance to the growth of trade and
shipping in the northern Low Countries. As in Genoa and elsewhere in
Italy, the *partenrederij* model was also applied elsewhere in the Low
Countries, including for windmills, rope-making facilities, tile works,
overland transfer systems for boats, and breweries.

Importantly, while the *partenrederij* had similarities to the modern
business corporation, it did not have independent legal personhood.
Furthermore, ships had dozens of investors, not thousands. And many of
these investors typically had some direct or indirect relationship with
regards to the activities on the ship. The legal form of the share
system for ships persists to this day in the Netherlands, now simply as
the *rederij*.[^31] Instead of dozens of shareholders, *rederijen* now
typically consist of two to five members. Liability is *pro*-*rata*.
While liability extends to personal assets outside those of the
*rederij*, several mechanisms are utilized to limit liability. Similar
legal forms also persist elsewhere in Europe, such as the *reederei* in
Germany and the *partrederi* in Sweden.

## 3. Joint Stock Companies

A legal entity more closely resembling the modern corporation, the joint
stock company, makes its breakthrough appearance for commercial ventures
in England and the Dutch Republic during the sixteenth and seventeenth
centuries.

There is extensive discussion in the literature on the characteristics,
origins, and importance of joint stock companies. For our discussion, we
can understand the **incorporated joint stock company** as any
*commercial association* that has the following basic
characteristics:[^32]

- Ownership over the association's equity capital (or the value of all
  assets minus liabilities) is distributed among owners through shares.
  Initial contributions to the equity capital are made via the issuance
  of shares. Additions to the equity capital can come through the
  issuance of further shares, capital calls on existing shareholders, or
  through retained profits. Existing shares can be bought and sold in a
  secondary market. The equity capital is locked-in, meaning substantial
  protections prevent shareholders or their creditors from liquidating
  the company and withdrawing their funds.

- The association has obtained the status of a "corporation" through a
  royal charter or a legislative act. We mean corporate status here in a
  very thin sense, namely that the association has a legally separate
  identity from its owners, employees, and other associates. It means
  that the association is marked by an independent existence that
  continues regardless of the participation of any particular owners,
  employees, and other associates.

- The association draws a basic distinction between the association's
  management and its owners. There are characteristically many owners
  and most of them are not involved in the management of the company.
  Owners are usually represented by a small group in order to exercise
  certain controls over management. The association has formal rules and
  procedures for making high-level commercial and strategic decisions.

This characterization closely follows the canonical discussion by
William Scott in *The Constitution and Finance of English, Scottish and
Irish Joint stock Companies to 1720*.[^33] On this basis, Scott contends
that the earliest incorporated joint stock companies were really first
seen in sixteenth-century England. He suggests that,[^34] in the absence
of better evidence, the earliest such companies were the Africa and
Muscovy Companies—both formed in 1553 and chartered somewhat
later—even though they did not fully have all the features as
mentioned above.[^35] It should be noted, in particular, that, while
these companies indeed show the union of the corporate form with
commercial capital, locked-in equity capital was only first introduced
within the **Dutch East India Company** or **VOC** (*Vereenigde
Oostindische Compagnie*) in the 1620s.[^36]

The Dutch East India Company was concerned with trade in Asia. In the
seventeenth century, the terms "East India" and "East Indies" often
denoted a broad and fuzzy geographic area, including the Indian
Subcontinent, South East Asia, the Malay Archipelago, and even China and
Japan.[^37] The terms "West India" and "West Indies" instead referred
to, and still refer to, the group of islands broadly separating the
Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf of Mexico, and the Caribbean Sea. The
distinction arose because early explorers to the Americas believed they
had reached Asia via the Atlantic. The Dutch East India Company,
therefore, was intended for directly conducting trade with the lands
beyond Persia. The same is true for other East India companies of the
period.

The English counterpart to the VOC, and actually a forerunner by two
years, was the English **East India Company** or **EIC** (1600).[^38] It
was legally entitled to conduct all English trade with Asia and intended
as a tool to compete with the Dutch, Portuguese, and Spanish. It
eventually had thousands of shareholders. Following the VOC by a few
decades, it organized locked-in equity capital in 1657.[^39]

The VOC and EIC became enormously powerful companies, responsible for
carrying out the vast majority of trade between Asia and Europe in the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. They managed substantial armed
forces and established forts and trading posts throughout Asia and
Africa. While their ambitions to colonize territories—that is, to
truly rule over them via political and economic institutions—were
limited initially, their role as territorial and colonial powers did
expand over time. The EIC notably gained a foothold as a colonial power
over India after the Battle of Plassey (1757). After the Indian
Rebellion of 1857, the British government disbanded the EIC and took
over control of India directly. After the VOC's bankruptcy and
dissolution in 1799, the Dutch Republic used the company's
infrastructure to roll out colonial control over a territory that became
known as the Dutch East Indies, mostly compromising modern-day
Indonesia.

The key early incorporated joint stock companies were all involved in
trade. The Dutch companion to the VOC, the **West India Company** or
**WIC** (*West-Indische Compagnie*), was chartered in 1621. The English
**Royal African Company** was granted a charter to conduct trade along
the coast of West Africa in 1660. Other notable early joint stock
companies in England were those utilized to settle and commercially
develop the North American territories, including the Virginia Company
(1606), the Plymouth Company (1606), the Massachusetts Bay Company
(1628), and the **Hudson's Bay Company** (1670). The latter was the
oldest company in North America until its liquidation in 2025. Early
English joint stock companies outside of the confines of trade seem to
have been primarily in mining, including the Mines Royal (1568) and the
Mineral and Battery Company (1568).[^40] Particularly in England, the
joint stock company legal form was increasingly utilized outside of
trade during the early modern period and the era of industrialization,
such as for deposit banks and insurers as well as companies involved in
the building and management of roads, canals, waterworks, and railroads.

The VOC and WIC were incorporated through legislative acts by the States
General. All the English joint stock companies mentioned so far were
instead incorporated through royal charters.[^41] In *Chapter 1*, we
stated that US banks are chartered by the authority of a state or the
federal government. This chartering occurs via a bureaucratic process
administered by a government agency. In the English context, it should
be noted that "chartering" typically refers only to the extension of a
**royal charter**, a formal legal document issued by the monarchy which
instantiates a corporation and grants it certain rights and privileges.
These royal charters were valid only for some period—typically
anywhere from a few years to several decades—though they could usually
be renewed.

Corporations of various kinds—of course in the thin sense of the
word—were common by the late sixteenth century in England. The
practice of incorporation was first introduced by the Roman Catholic
Church in the High Middle Ages for the Church as a whole as well as
constituent parts such as bishopdoms, abbots, convents, and other
ecclesiastical bodies. Corporate practices were formalized through canon
law. According to Harris,[^42] the Roman Catholic Church built a
well-developed legal and conceptual framework around incorporation for
two reasons. First, it allowed the hierarchical and centralized Roman
Catholic Church to have a distinct standing from the Holy Roman Emperor
and other secular rulers. Second, the Church was influenced by the
rigorous tradition of Roman law. By the late sixteenth century, the
corporate form had been adopted by many secular bodies in England such
as cities and boroughs, universities, colleges, charitable institutions,
and guilds. Typically, the members of these corporations selected
management bodies to represent their interests.

Until the sixteenth century, various paths to incorporation other than
the royal charter existed in England. Many associations, for example,
lacked formal approval of any sort and claimed corporate status merely
on the basis of custom or prescription.[^43] By the late sixteenth
century, however, the Crown had monopolized the authority over
incorporation. According to Harris, "an explicit, ex ante and direct
authorization by the King became the only mode of incorporation. This
authorization was normally given in the form of charter (or letters
patent), and, occasionally, by way of Act of Parliament bearing the
King's explicit consent, or a combination of an act and a charter".[^44]

As the general powers of the English Parliament increased relative to
those of the Crown, particularly after the Glorious Revolution (1688),
it also increasingly gained independent authority for incorporation by
private legislative acts. This practice, naturally, also became common
with regards to joint stock companies more specifically. Notably, the
**Bank of England** (1694) was incorporated by a private act of
Parliament, and the initial issuance of shares saw 1,520 subscribers
with stakes ranging from £25 to £10,000.[^45] By the second half of the
eighteenth century, it seems that the incorporation of joint stock
companies proceeded predominantly via private legislative acts. That era
was marked particularly by the incorporation of canal companies—over
100 by 1800—and the vast majority indeed were incorporated by
Parliament.[^46]

Incorporation granted certain specific rights and privileges to joint
stock companies, such as the monopoly rights over the development of
trade between England and a certain geographic area. But the corporate
status of these companies also offered general legal privileges.
Notably, by the sixteenth century, the notion and custom of a legally
separate personhood for incorporated associations seems to have been
well-established.[^47] So incorporated joint stock companies had the
right to sue and be sued as an entity. It also meant that they had the
right to own assets which were seen as distinct from the personal assets
of their shareholders, a concept known as **asset partitioning**.

Scott contends that the incorporated joint stock company concept was
probably not imported from the Continent into England. Instead, it
appears to have been a fusion of two organizational English traditions:
that of the temporary, capital-intensive partnerships which marked
medieval commerce and that of the tradition of incorporation for
institutions with a public or semi-public function. The most direct
pre-cursor to the incorporated joint stock company was, in Scott's
opinion, surely the **regulated company**, an incorporated association
which organized standards, rules, and commercial opportunities for
merchants, without pooling the capital of their members. Some regulated
companies eventually began pooling capital, and so transformed into
joint stock companies. Schmitthoff also endorses the native evolution of
the joint stock company in England.[^48]

The joint stock company concept may have been transplanted to the Dutch
Republic from England. But more plausibly, it developed there in
parallel.[^49] In the Dutch Republic, the tradition of
*partenrederijen*—which was not really popular in England—surely had
a significant impact on the development of the *VOC*. It was, however,
much less influenced by the type of corporate tradition that shaped the
formation of the English joint stock companies, even though the *VOC*
was clearly a corporation.[^50] In any case, regardless of the origins,
it is particularly the model of the VOC that permeated throughout the
Continent in the seventeenth century. The French King Louis XIV, for
example, tried to get know-how from Merchants involved in the VOC in the
foundation of the French **Compagnie des Indes Orientales** (1604). And
many East India companies in other parts of Europe were actually started
by Dutch merchants.[^51]

There were certainly commercial associations on the Continent in earlier
times that could be classified as incorporated joint stock companies or
at least had significant similarities to them. In 1953, Germain Sicard
published a seminal study in which he claimed that companies with
features of the modern business corporation were already existent in
Toulouse in the fourteenth century. The Garonne River runs straight
through the city and extensive milling operations developed along
various fords during the High Middle Ages. In 1372, seemingly as the
result of a court case against one of them, the mill owners around one
of the fords, Bazacle, formed the Bazacle Milling Company, which adopted
many of the characteristics of the joint stock company. A year later,
the Castel Milling Company formed with similar characteristics. The
unique structure of the **Mills of Toulouse**, however, seems not to
have inspired commercial enterprises elsewhere. They also long escaped
the attention of financial and economic historians. While the Mills of
Toulouse had similarities with the later joint stock companies, at least
one point of the difference was the number of shareholders. In both
companies, the number of shareholders seems to have generally been
substantially less than 100, and they generally came from Toulouse. So,
they did not quite have the size and impersonal nature of the later
joint stock companies.[^52]

A better known potential example of an early or
proto-joint-stock-company—one long known to financial and economic
historians—is Genoa's famous **House of St. George** (*Casa di San
Giorgio*, hereafter also the "Casa" for short) established in 1407,
about a 150 years before the rise of English joint stock companies.[^53]
While Scott and Schmitthoff both acknowledge the
joint-stock-company-like features of the Casa, they were skeptical that
it had any real influence on the formation of the English and Dutch
joint stock companies.[^54] Recent scholarship from Carlo Taviani shows,
however, that their conclusion is somewhat overstated. He illustrates
that, indeed, the House of Saint George served as a model for the
formation of incorporated joint stock companies such as the VOC, the
Bank of England, and the **Mississippi Company** (1717).[^55] As we will
discuss in *Section 4*, the Casa might very well deserve the label of
incorporated joint stock company. Nevertheless, it is true that the
breakthrough appearance of the joint stock company as an organizational
form really begins with England and the Dutch Republic in the sixteenth
and seventeenth centuries, the Dutch VOC model particularly being
influential on the rest of the Continent.

In the Dutch Republic, the growth of joint stock companies seems to have
been somewhat limited after the formation of the VOC. In terms of
incorporated joint stock companies, the two main ones of the seventeenth
and eighteenth centuries were the WIC and an insurance company chartered
in 1720. The latter eventually came to be known as Stad Rotterdam
Verzekeringen, and it was the oldest Continental insurance company until
it merged with De Amersfoortse into ASR Nederland in 1997. Next to these
incorporated joint stock companies, unincorporated joint stock companies
were also formed from the seventeenth century onwards.

England, instead, seems to have experienced more substantial growth in
joint stock companies, particularly after the Glorious Revolution.[^56]
Importantly, as in the Dutch Republic, the English landscape was not
limited to incorporated joint stock companies. Instead, in the second
half of the seventeenth century, England also increasingly saw the
appearance of unincorporated joint stock companies.[^57] According to
Butler,[^58] their popularity grew particularly due to the influence of
the Dutch traders who migrated to England after the Glorious Revolution.

What exactly are unincorporated joint stock companies?

Obtaining royal charters or a private act from Parliament was subject to
extensive, costly procedures and political lobbying. So, many
entrepreneurs attempted to create companies that emulated the main
features of incorporated joint stock companies without formally
obtaining corporate status. **These are called unincorporated joint
stock companies**. At least in the eighteenth century, a key feature of
these arrangements in England was typically to entrust the company's
assets to the executive directors via a deed of settlement. Some
unincorporated joint stock companies also sought to obtain the right to
sue and be sued through private acts from Parliament in the early
nineteenth century.[^59]

The joint stock company of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was,
thus, perhaps not so much a specific type of legal entity, but rather an
economic arrangement. As suggested by Ireland,[^60]

> A joint stock company was an enterprise based on an accumulated
> capital fund; a firm involving many people, not usually closely
> related, whose membership took the highly impersonal form of a freely
> transferrable share and whose participation in management was minimal.
> The terms 'joint stock company' and 'company' conveyed nothing about a
> firm's legal status\.... Critically, the legal status of an enterprise
> made no difference to contemporary perceptions of its economic form: a
> joint stock company was a joint stock company whether it was
> incorporated or not.

From here on, we can follow that understanding of the **joint stock
company**, namely, as any commercial association that is characterized
by joint ownership over locked-in equity capital via transferable
shares; formal rules and procedures for making commercial and strategic
decisions; and a basic distinction between management and owners, there
typically being many owners, most of whom do not actively participate in
the company's affairs. Only those that were formed by a royal charter or
act of parliament were formal corporations. Unincorporated joint stock
companies sometimes might have had certain corporate characteristics.

Historically, the incorporated joint stock companies were far more
important. Unincorporated joint stock companies were actually
statutorily banned in England by the *Bubble Act 1719*.[^61] The ban
went into effect in 1720 and specifically mandated that joint stock
companies could only be created by royal charters or parliamentary acts.
Various explanations for the passing of the *Bubble Act 1719* have been
put forward, but it seems that that primary reason was lobbying by the
notorious **South Sea Company** (1711) in an attempt to shield itself
from competition.[^62] In fact, the act mostly pertains to the
incorporation of two marine insurance companies, and certain protections
for the South Sea Company, the East India Company, and the two new
insurance companies. Protection against bubbles formed only a small
portion of the law, and the name "Bubble Act" only became common in the
nineteenth century.

Eurasian trade has a long history. Trade between Mediterranean merchants
and India already existed in Ancient Greek and Roman times.[^63] In the
fifteenth century, Eurasian trade was organized along the **Silk
Road**—a network of overland routes—and various maritime routes.
Goods would pass through the hands of numerous traders from Asia to
Europe that handled different legs of the journey. In the sixteenth and
seventeenth centuries, however, Eurasian trade came to be dominated by
European traders in ships that handled most of the trade journey,
thereby eliminating numerous middlemen. They used the Cape Route to
Asia, which had been first made fully by the Portuguese explorer Vasco
da Gama (c. 1460s–1524) at the end of the fifteenth century.

In the sixteenth century, the ships conducting direct trade with Asia
were primarily Portuguese. The Spanish also conducted long-distance
trade, but it was largely confined to the Americas—except in Brazil
which was Portuguese—and the Pacific region, extending as far as the
Philippines. This division reflected extraordinarily brazen agreements
made by the Spanish and Portuguese Crowns in the Treaty of Tordesillas
(1494) and the Treaty of Zaragoza (1529) in which they essentially
divided the entire world into two legitimate spheres of influence.

The Portuguese Crown sought to monopolize and organize the Asian trade
directly. But it was hampered by a small tax base and the extensive
organization required. So foreign merchant bankers quickly became
involved in financing and both domestic and foreign merchants were
granted participation rights in expeditions. The Iberian Union of 1580,
in which Philip II ascended to the Portuguese Crown, left the Portuguese
legal and administrative system intact. It had no impact on the overall
state-drive approach to the Asian trade. During the sixteenth century,
this Portuguese state-driven trade with Asia was never able to achieve
anything near the scale of the VOC and WIC.[^64] In the seventeenth and
eighteenth centuries, instead, the vast majority of ships which took the
Cape route were from these two companies. The Dutch and English largely
dismantled the Portuguese Empire in Asia—known as the Estado da
India—in the seventeenth century, though Portugal held on to a few
possessions until modern times: Goa (1961), East Timor (1975), and Macao
(1999).

The development of the incorporated joint stock company in England and
the Dutch Republic is often judged by historians precisely as a response
to the challenges of international trade, particularly with Asia.[^65]
The new direct maritime trade with Asia came with very high risks,
including with regards to shipwrecks, piracy, and conflicts with foreign
powers. In the last years of sixteenth century, for example, more than
twenty percent of the Dutch ships conducting trade with Asia were
lost.[^66] Trade with Asia required extensive investments into
fortifications, trading posts, military convoys, agents, and diplomacy.
An infrastructure that supported the flow of information about routes,
markets, and competitors was particularly important.[^67] The journeys
were notably much longer than to other parts of Europe, West Africa, or
even the West Indies. Round trips required about two years to complete.
This was not only due to the distance of the journeys, but also due to
climate patterns, particularly the South Asian Monsoon. The Asia trade,
therefore, required an unprecedented amount of capital that could only
be acquired from a wide investor base, one that spanned to more
anonymous and less affluent investors. It also required long-term
planning and commitment. These challenges could not be well met by
partnerships, family firms, and state-run enterprises. So, joint stock
companies, in particular the VOC and EIC, evolved as a response.

While joint stock companies were started elsewhere in Europe, the most
successful ones in the early modern period are undoubtedly the English
and Dutch ones. Most notably, the VOC and EIC dominated the highly
profitable Eurasian trade in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
We will not develop an elaborate discussion on why this was the case
here, but do want to highlight one important factor: political
institutions.

As explained in *Chapter 3*, England (and Great Britain after 1707) and
the Dutch Republic were the two main exceptions to the absolutist rule
that marked early modern Europe. Unlike elsewhere in Europe, the VOC and
EIC—as well as other English and Dutch joint stock companies—were
credibly protected against the arbitrary interference from a sovereign
such as through the revocation of their charters or arbitrary
confiscation of their assets. In England, this was largely the product
of an increasingly influential parliament and independent judiciary. In
the Dutch Republic, this was partially the product of the federal
structure which created a weaker and more reserved central government.
The VOC was also supported by the fact that the Republic was ruled by
merchants and, hence, benefited the country and its political leaders.
The guarantees from arbitrary interference helped, for example, gain the
trust of smaller investors who, unlike wealthy merchants and bankers,
could not resort as easily to reputational sanctions.[^68]

## 4. The House of St. George

The House of St. George was the first public bank of significance in
Europe. Given its importance to the history of finance and banking, we
introduce it here more elaborately.

During the High Middle Ages, the northern Italians came to dominate
trade and shipping in the Mediterranean and the Black Sea regions,
predominantly at the expense of Byzantium and Islamic states. They
established numerous trading enclaves, including at Alexandria, Acre,
Constantinople, and Caffa in Crimea. Like many other northern and
central Italian cities, Genoa formally became a commune in the early
High Middle Ages. After its defeat of Pisa at the Battle of Meloria
(1284), Genoa became the foremost maritime power next to Venice. It was,
as Venice, heavily involved in the Mediterranean and Black Sea slave
trade, and a hub for importing goods from the Middle East and North
Africa. The two maritime powers fought major wars for maritime supremacy
in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, culminating with Genoa's
defeat in the War of Chioggia (1378–1381).

Genoa's position as an early maritime power helped propel financial
innovation within the city. Even though its maritime power declined from
the fourteenth century onwards, Genoa remained important to maritime
trade and continued as a hub of financial innovation until the early
modern period.[^69] One key financial institution that had apparently
developed by the middle of the twelfth century in Genoa was called the
*compera*.[^70]

The *compera* was probably inspired by the practice of share ownership
in ships that existed in the eleventh and twelfth centuries in Genoa,
Venice, and elsewhere. The **compera** was a legal entity that bought
the rights to certain indirect taxes (*gabelle*) for a period of time
from the Commune. The capital for the purchase would come from wealthy
merchants and other investors who in exchange were allocated shares
(*luoghi* or *loca*) in the *compera*. The initiative to organize a
*compera* typically came from the Commune when it required funds for
specific purposes, and wealthy Genoans were often forced to participate
in these capital raises. Each *compera* was typically named after the
specific purpose for the collected funds. Examples include the *Compere
Capituli* (funds for the Cathedral), the *Compera Venetorum* (a fund for
the war against Venice), and the *Compera Corsicae* (a fund for the war
against Pisa for Corsica). The shares of a *compera* paid out interest
and repaid the principal using the associated tax payments. These taxes
were typically collected indirectly through **tax farmers**, agents
specialized in collecting taxes. These were known as *gabelloti* in
Genoa.

The shares of the *compere* were actively traded in secondary markets by
the fifteenth century, such a market having existed at least since the
middle of the thirteenth century.[^71] They were quite possibly the
first debt securities in Europe.[^72] The shares of the *compere*, at
least initially, resembled modern-day asset backed securities. An entity
holding certain receivables—in this case the Commune with the power to
levy certain taxes—sells them to a special purpose vehicle—in this
case a *compera*. The latter entity pays for these receivables by
raising capital through the issuance of bond-like securities to
investors. The interest and principal on these securities is, then,
returned through payments on the underlying receivables.[^73]

The Genoese Commune occasionally made efforts at **debt consolidation**,
that is, various existing *compere* shares would be valued and exchanged
for the shares in a single new *compera*. The initial purpose of the
House of St. George was precisely a grand debt consolidation of the
outstanding *compere*, primarily in order to lower the interest burden
on the Commune and to reduce the administrative complexity of the
outstanding debt. The Casa was an office of the Commune though one with
a corporate and highly autonomous status. Over a few decades, it managed
to consolidate most of the outstanding *compere* into a single
*compera*. Initially, the Casa's shares offered a fixed interest rate of
7%, which was a few points lower than the average interest rate over all
the *compere* it had consolidated. The shift to centralized management,
thus, reduced the overall debt burden for the Commune.

In 1420, the interest rate on the Casa's shares became variable. In
addition, interest payments were typically no longer paid out according
to a strict schedule; instead, they would only be credited to
shareholders and payout was delayed for unknown periods. The Casa's
shares became very popular with local and foreign investors in part
because of the strong property rights by which they were accompanied:
even criminals and exiles were offered strong guarantees against the
confiscation of their shares.[^74] The House of Saint George facilitated
the secondary market in its shares through a meticulous registration
system. Companies and individuals existed that speculated on the value
of the Casa's shares. It seems that some of them also acted as brokers
and agents for other investors.[^75] The shares of the Casa were also
frequently used as collateral with pawnbrokers, and they were loaned
between private citizens (essentially a loan in exchange for the right
to interest payments).

The interest deposits on the shares of the Casa—that is, the
acknowledgments of interest not yet paid out—were also bought and sold
in the secondary market and the Casa again facilitated that market
through a registration system. The interest deposits were sold at a
discount to compensate for the uncertainty in the moment of payout and
the fact that the Casa probably did not pay interest on these interest
deposits. While it is unclear how exactly the secondary market on
interest deposits worked, it seems to have been popular with merchants
and artisans. Particularly, silk merchants appear to have speculated on
the discount rate.[^76] The discounting practice, of course, had a
somewhat uncertain status with regards to its legitimacy given the
theological principles and laws on usury.[^77] Supposedly the rights to
interest deposits could also be transferred through endorsed receipts,
which eventually functioned like bank notes.[^78]

The financial activities of the Casa were not just limited to debt
consolidation and the facilitation of secondary markets for its shares
and interest deposits. A key role was as a direct financier to the
Commune. Loans were extended via various mechanisms. For example, the
Commune levied the owners of the Casa's shares every year, a tax the
Casa collected on its behalf. Often the Commune was given advancements
on the tax at a discount. The Casa also loaned money to the Commune
against the right to collect certain other taxes. For most of its
history, the Casa also offered deposit banking activities, namely from
1408 to 1444 and from 1530 to 1805. In those periods, the Commune would
typically just receive the loan as credit money in an account on which
they could freely draw. In times of distress, the Casa was often quite
forgiving with regards to the Commune's obligations.[^79]

Loans were also extended to rulers, nobles, and ecclesiastical
authorities, commonly in exchange for the right to certain levies and
taxes. The Casa also raised capital by issuing new shares or even by
issuing new interest deposits. As already mentioned, for much of its
history the Casa functioned as a deposit bank, a part of the institution
commonly referred to as the **Bank of Saint George**. It facilitated
interbank payments by managing accounts for many private bankers. While
the Casa formally ceased its deposit banking role for about a hundred
years—namely from 1444 to 1530—it appears that the Casa continued
*de facto* to provide payment services to the public through the
transfers of interest deposits.

Between 1446 and 1562, the Casa also controlled and governed numerous
territories around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea. Many of these
territories were acquired from the Commune. Its first territory,
Pietrasanta, was acquired as collateral for a loan in 1446 before it
came under formal control in 1448. While land was commonly acquired in
relation to debts, the Commune also frequently handed territory to the
Casa for its ability to organize defense and protect trade interests.
Territories were also acquired from foreign political entities. In 1528,
Genoa underwent a substantial political transformation under the
leadership of Andrea Doria (1466–1560). The Casa handed all its
territories to the new Republic in 1562.

By the fifteenth century, the Genoese ruling elites were divided into
two classes: the nobles (*nobili*) and the *populares*. The latter class
consisted mostly of wealthy merchants and artisans. The former class
consisted more of traditional land-owning elites, though the distinction
between the two groups was fairly blurry. The nobles and *populares*
ruled the offices of the Commune as well as those of the House of Saint
George. The latter's main ruling body for most of its lifespan
constituted of eight officers. As was common in northern and central
Italy, selection for the offices of the Commune and the Casa proceeded
by a system of quota, elections, and lotteries. Those who held these
offices, regardless of their classification as nobles or *populares*,
were overwhelmingly involved in trade and finance. From 1490 to 1527,
for example, the Spinolas, Lomellinos, and the Grimaldis—all powerful
merchant bankers—were three of the main families represented in both
the Senate and the House of Saint George.[^80] Participation in the
selection of the Casa's officers was reserved for those who held a
certain number of the *compera*'s shares.

The Casa's financial power and territorial possessions made it really an
independent seat of power next to the Commune for much of the fifteenth
and sixteenth centuries. It seems that there was indeed a powerful group
within the Casa that wanted to take over control from the Commune
entirely, or at least many people perceived this to be the case.[^81]
Observing the Casa in the early sixteenth century, the famous Florentine
diplomat, philosopher, and historian Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527)
acknowledged the two competing powers within the Genoese Republic in his
posthumously published *Florentine Histories* (1532). He actually
appeared to view the Casa as a source of stability given the
factionalism that was afflicting Communal politics, most notably the
competition for the office of the Doge, or Genoa's head of state.
Throughout the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, the position
frequently shifted between the Fregosos and Adornos, two families from
the *populares*, often leading to a temporary exile of the losing
family. The House of St George, however, was a source of stability, even
during foreign occupations by the Milanese and French. Machiavelli,
therefore, thought it would eventually come to rule the Republic. In his
words,

> An example truly rare, never found by the philosophers in all the
> republics they have imagined and seen: to see within the same circle,
> among the same citizens, liberty and tyranny, civil life and corrupt
> life, justice and license, because that order along keeps the city
> full of its ancient and venerable customs. And if it should
> happen—which in time it surely will—that San Giorgio should take
> over the whole city, that would be a republic more memorable than the
> Venetian.[^82]

The Dorias were old, landed nobility, influential in Genoese politics
and society from the twelfth to sixteenth centuries. While also active
in trade and finance, the family is perhaps best remembered for
producing military leaders and statesmen. Andrea Doria was one of
Europe's foremost naval commanders at the time. And in 1528, he allied
with the Habsburg Emperor, Charles V, to the drive out the French from
Genoa and establish a new political order. The reforms notably sidelined
the Fregosos and Adornos and overall seem to have created a more
oligarchic and stable polity. While *de facto* already a republic, Genoa
formally adopted the title of a republic from 1528 onwards. The Genoese
became the bankers to the Spanish Crown which brought great opulence to
the city in the sixteenth century. The strengthening of Genoa's
political institutions meant that the House of Saint George was no
longer seen as a competing power by the end of the sixteenth century.
The Republic of Genoa persisted until 1797 when Napoleon invaded and
established the Ligurian Republic. In 1805, Napoleon annexed Genoa
directly and forced the House of St. George to close its doors.

Was the Casa a joint stock company?

Certainly, it did not start out as a joint stock company. However, the
debt securities issued by the Casa arguably morphed into equity shares
over time. The variable interest rate and irregular payouts were more
like dividend payments than interest payments. The "equity capital" was
also locked in. At least sufficiently large owners of the Casa's shares
had a role in selecting its officers. The shares (and interest deposits)
were easily and actively traded on secondary markets. They seem to have
been widely held beyond mere personal relationships. Unlike other
systems of public debt in Italy, the Casa indeed seems to have enjoyed a
corporate status with powers independent of the Commune.

While the matter need not be settled here, it is obvious that the Casa,
if not a joint stock company, at least had substantial similarities to
the later English and Dutch joint stock companies. And in any case, it
seems to have functioned as a model of some importance in the formation
of at least some of these companies.[^83]
