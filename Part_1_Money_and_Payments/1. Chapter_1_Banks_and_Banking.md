# Chapter 1: Banks and banking

Banks are at the core of money and payments. Like it or not, they are some of the most fundamental institutions that make our society run (and sometimes come to a screeching halt). To understand money and payment practices in the United States, we, therefore, require starting with some basic background on banks, particularly on the services they offer and the role they play in the monetary system. 

We start by offering a characterization of specifically commercial banks, the most important type of bank. We examine the legal foundations of commercial banks in *Section 2*. In *Section 3*, we will briefly discuss various other types of banks than the commercial bank and explore some closely related financial institutions. We finish in *Section 4* with a brief discussion on the development of banking in the United States until the early days of the Republic. 


## 1. Commercial banks

We might distinguish between various types of banks. But by the far the most important type of bank is what is generally called a commercial bank in the United States. 

Commercial banks extend credit to consumers and businesses. They offer many products and services for payments, savings, and investments. They employ a great number of people. And a somewhat less well-known fact: commercial banks play an important role in determining the money supply. Thus, their activities and financial performance unsurprisingly has a massive impact on the overall state of the economy. 

Countries and regions across the world are typically dominated by a handful of commercial banks that wield the majority of economic influence and market share. In the United States, these are household names such as Citibank, JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, and the Bank of America. In France, we might think of BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, and Société Générale, or in Spain of Banco Santander, BBVA, and CaixaBank. The United Kingdom is dominated by a handful of **high-street banks**, including Lloyd’s, Barclays, HSBC, and NatWest. Japan’s banking landscape is dominated by several megabanks, called **city banks**, including MUFG Bank, SMBC, and Mizuho Bank.    

Besides these well-known giants, there are thousands of smaller commercial banks. Most commercial banks in the United States are legally required to be members of the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** (**FDIC**). This is a government-owned corporation that insures the money which consumers and businesses keep in banks, at least up to a certain amount. Each quarter, the FDIC publishes a report on the financial performance of its members. According to the report from September 2024,<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> the United States counted a whopping 3,966 commercial banks with over $23 Trillion in combined assets. Most of these are smaller community and regional banks, often in rural areas. 

A similar picture emerges in Europe. While the number of credit institutions—mostly consisting of commercial banks—has been consolidating in recent years, the 27 members of the European Union still counted more than 4,614 credit institutions that were natively incorporated in 2020. Some states show much more variety than others. Germany, for example, has a much greater number of credit institutions than France and Spain, even accounting for differences in population size.<sup>[2](#footnote2)</sup>

So what exactly is a commercial bank? And what distinguishes it from other types of banks and financial institutions? 

While commercial banks offer a wide variety of services and, particularly the larger ones, are often complex organizations, we can offer a basic characterization that suffices for our purposes. Specifically, **commercial banks** are *for-profit* financial institutions that are marked by the following four properties and activities. 

First, commercial banks accept and manage deposits on behalf of consumers and businesses. A **deposit** here is just a sum of money that is placed into a bank account. The owners of these accounts can, in turn, use these deposits in various ways.    

Commercial banks notably offer bank accounts to consumers and businesses from which money can be drawn without notice. The main type of account here is what is typically called a **checking account** in the United States and a **current account** in the United Kingdom. Checking accounts, as we will call them, are those that consumers and businesses use on a day-to-day basis for receiving and making payments, typically through a mobile or web-based software application, or via physical or virtual payment cards. Money in these accounts can normally also be withdrawn in cash via a bank teller or more commonly from an **automated teller machine** (**ATM**). 

Besides checking accounts, commercial banks commonly advertise various types of other accounts. Certain very restrictive accounts will accept deposits that cannot be withdrawn for several months or even years (except perhaps at a penalty), but they pay relatively high interest rates. In the English language, we see a variety of terms that pertain to such accounts, including **time deposits**, **term deposits**, **savings deposits**, and **certificate of deposits**. The latter is a really common term particularly in the United States. 

Other types of very restrictive accounts offered by commercial banks are those specifically geared towards retirement. These accounts typically offer a means to save, invest, or a combination of both. In some cases, these **retirement accounts** are organized by employers or unions. Retirement accounts also exist that are organized by an individual, typically called **individual retirement accounts** (**IRAs**) in the United States and **personal pension accounts** in the United Kingdom. Retirement accounts come with various degrees of influence for their beneficiary with regards to the savings and investment decisions. 
 
Both time deposits and savings-oriented retirement accounts are generally considered to be special, very restrictive types of **savings accounts**. There are many different account products that travel by this name, but a more standard savings account might look as follows:

* Money has to be withdrawn to a checking account, either at the same bank or a different bank, before it can be used. Sometimes this process is quick and without real limitations. In other cases, the process is somewhat more restricted. 
* The account normally comes without payment cards or check books. 
* The account usually enjoys a positive interest rate, at least in fairly normal economic times. Checking accounts, because money can be drawn without notice, will often have no interest, or at least a lower rate of interest. 

Another common type of account offered by commercial banks is the **investment account**, where deposited funds can be used to buy stocks, bonds, and other types of investment products. These accounts sometimes also offer interest rates, and the funds have to be withdrawn to a checking account before they can be utilized more generally. Investment-oriented retirement accounts are really just very restrictive investment accounts. 

Second, commercial banks are key institutions for lending to businesses and consumers. With respect to consumers, commercial banks mostly lend on a running basis through overdrafts on checking accounts and through credit cards. Commercial banks also offer loans for, amongst other things, education, cars, and, most importantly, property purchases and renovations. With respect to businesses, commercial banks offer a variety of loans that can be used for large investments and working capital. Many businesses often have an extensive line of credit with their banks. For large commercial projects, commercial banks and other financial institutions will often join forces in extending loans. The latter types of loans are typically called **syndicated loans**. 

You will commonly hear the practice of lending referred to as “the extension of credit.” The term **credit** here just means a loaned sum of money. In Latin, “credit” means “one believes.” So the expression “the extension of credit” emphasizes the faith the lender has in the borrower to pay back the loan. Other than denoting a loaned sum of money, the term “credit” also takes on various other interrelated meanings in finance. A person or institution’s credit, credit rating, or credit score, for instance, is an indication of their general trustworthiness with regards to paying back loans, as in the expression “That person has good credit.”

Third, commercial banks are standardly incorporated entities and require special approval from the government to operate as a bank. Most certainly in developed economies, operating as a commercial bank normally comes with strict regulatory and legal requirements that are much more extensive than those for other types of companies. So-called **compliance departments** ensure that a commercial bank meets all these requirements. The costs of compliance on modern commercial banks are typically very high. Many would argue that these costs are too high and that they hamper product development, customer service, and real innovation. 

A particularly salient target of criticism here are the laws and regulations which target **money laundering**: that is, the process of taking the money obtained from criminal activities and introducing it into the commercial and financial system in an effort to conceal the criminal origins of that money. Financial crime expert Ronald Pol, for example, recently suggested the global anti-money laundering regime might be the “world’s least effective policy experiment.”<sup>[3](#footnote3)</sup>

Fourth, commercial banks do not just accept deposits to bank accounts, they can also create new money within them. Similarly, commercial banks can destroy existing money within bank accounts. To put it simply, commercial banks can affect the money supply through the creation and destruction of the money that sits in our bank accounts. They do this primarily through their lending activities. Mostly this process occurs via checking accounts specifically. 

Central banks attempt to steer this whole process through monetary policy. Government bodies and central banks also seek to steer the bank money supply through the regulatory regime, particularly through capital and liquidity requirements. 

We will delve into the details of the role of banks with regards to the money supply in *Chapter 4*. For now, however, we can briefly offer some intuition on how commercial banks create and destroy money using the history of the goldsmith bankers in England. 

Before the 17th century, banking services such as the extension of credit had first been provided by the Jewish community until they were expelled by Edward I (1290), and then by the Lombards from Italy. Within the 17th century, however, many of the goldsmiths in England (along with some other types of professionals) also started acting as bankers.<sup>[4](#footnote4)</sup>

How did this happen?

Goldsmiths were not just competent in forging jewelry and other objects out of gold and silver. Many people also used them to store, in addition to other valuables, their gold and silver in the form of jewelry, specie, and bullion. By **specie** here we mean gold or silver coins issued by a government or some private institution. By **bullion** we mean gold and silver in the shape of bars and ingots. The demand for the custodial services from goldsmiths seems to have grown particularly in the 17th century after the seizing of the mint by King Charles I in 1640 and the outbreak of the English Civil War. 

In the early 17th century, the goldsmiths started to issue notes against the gold and silver deposited with them, which entitled their bearer to a certain amount of gold or silver. These notes were, in other words, **negotiable**: they could be transferred to others. In this way, they became as a means of payment. These notes were known as **goldsmiths’ notes** and, in function and appearance, were essentially banknotes. While banking was hardly new in the 17th century, the goldsmiths do seem to have been among the first bankers to issue banknotes. 

The goldsmiths did not just hold onto gold and silver deposits for safekeeping. They also loaned out these deposits against interest rates. These rates would have been higher than any interest they might have paid to those who deposited the gold and silver. At some point, the goldsmiths would have realized they could extend their loans in goldsmiths’ notes, rather than in the actual silver and gold deposits. So, in a final leap of financial creativity, they would have realized somewhere in the 17th century that they could issue goldsmiths’ notes that represented *more value* in gold and silver than they actually had on deposit. Customers, after all, would not come and collect all their gold and silver at the same time, so they (mistakenly) assumed.

The goldsmiths’ notes functioned as currency. By issuing more notes than the specie and bullion in their vaults warranted, the goldsmiths were, in fact, creating money. While the process is somewhat more abstract today, the essential principle behind it is the same: money is generally created whenever commercial banks grant loans (and destroyed when those loans are paid off). 

So, in sum, characteristically, commercial banks are incorporated for-profit financial institutions which (1) manage deposits from consumers and businesses, including within accounts that offer withdrawals without notice and various payment products; (2) extend credit extensively to consumers and businesses; (3) require special approval from the government to operate and are subject to restrictive laws and regulations; and (4) affect the money supply primarily through their lending activities.  

Importantly, the characterization of commercial banks offered here pertains to what we commonly call “commercial banks” in everyday language. It is not intended to have prescriptive implications. Some, for example, might balk at the suggestion that commercial banks can affect the money supply, or that they need special approval from the government to operate and are heavily regulated. The purpose here is not to express a strong opinion on those matters, but to merely to offer a baseline conceptualization of a commercial bank that allows for further discussion.


## 2. The legal foundations of commercial banks

To develop a good understanding of commercial banks, as well as banks and banking more broadly, we require some basic background on their legal foundations. We will start our discussion with the claim in the previous section that commercial banks are typically “incorporated.” What exactly does that mean?  

To start, in practically every country, there are various types of **legal structures**, which businesses and organizations can adopt upon their instantiation. These are very broad templates that describe the basic nature of the organization and have certain legal and regulatory implications. As legal structures are only broad templates, two businesses or organizations with the same legal structure can still vary greatly with regards to their business or organizational model, governance and management arrangements, operational details, and so on.   

In the United States, the creation of businesses and organization is largely the prerogative of the state governments in which they will come to reside. For businesses, the main legal structures are proprietorships, general partnerships, limited liability partnerships, limited partnerships, limited liability companies, S corporations, and C corporations. Nonprofit corporations and unincorporated associations are common legal structures for non-profit organizations. Sometimes collective activities are also managed via trust deeds, in which case no specific legal entity is created. Instead, the trust deed is just a contract that governs the relationship between existing legal entities. Businesses and organizations typically default to certain types of legal structures if no paperwork is filed (such as proprietorships, general partnerships, and unincorporated associations). 

We must carefully distinguish between the foundational legal structure of a business or organization and other legal distinctions that might be applied to it in the context of laws and regulations. For instance, a **venture capital fund**—broadly speaking, a company which invests money pooled from shareholders into startup companies—is a distinct type of investment company within the laws and regulations that are relevant to the work of the US **Securities and Exchange Commission** (**SEC**).<sup>[5](#footnote5)</sup> However, it is not a foundational legal structure in our sense discussed here. Rather, the legal structure of a a venture capital fund is typically a limited partnership. 

The US state governments typically offer three main corporate structures: two for-profit corporate structures—the **S corporation** and the **C corporation**—and the **nonprofit corporation**. All three structures have a key document to help in its legal establishment, which provides its main aims and rights. The document is known by various names, but we will refer to it generally as the **articles of association** (also **articles of incorporation** or the **corporate charter**). You might view the articles of association as the constitution of a corporation. A key principle of any corporation is that it is a separate legal person, distinct from the owners, executives, and employees. 
 
In both C and S corporations, ownership is determined by the relative ownership of **shares**, and the holders of these shares are called **shareholders**. The C Corporation is available in all 50 states, though Delaware is the most popular choice due to well-developed and business-friendly corporate laws. The S Corporation is also available in most states. S Corporations are a less common choice than C Corporations by for-profit corporations because of various restrictions on S Corporations, including a limit of 100 shareholders. Shareholders of corporations only risk the capital they place within the corporation, not their other personal assets (except in extreme cases such as fraud). Corporations, in other words, are governed by the principle of limited liability. 

C corporations can be both **privately** and **publicly held companies**. In the former case, the shares are not traded on a public stock exchange and the companies are not required to disclose extensive information to the public about the company. Selling shares typically comes with restrictions, such as the right of first refusal (which dictates that any shares for sale should be provided for sale to existent shareholders first). In a public company, shares are traded on public exchanges and the company has to file extensive information publicly with the Securities and Exchange Commission. Note that, as S corporations can only have a maximum of 100 owners, it is not practical for them to go public.

Just as a C and S corporation, a non-profit corporation is also considered a legal person. It does not, however, have shares or pay dividends to owners. Essentially, there are no owners. Any profits cannot be distributed and are only to be used by the organization to promote their mission. The organization is led by a board of directors who monitor an executive board that conducts day-to-day operations. Public charities, private foundations, and other types of nonprofit organizations are often legally set up as nonprofit corporations. 

Jurisdictions outside of the United States (certainly with developed economies) will also offer their citizens a palette of legal structures for starting a business or organization. There are certainly going to be important differences between jurisdictions. That said, any jurisdiction will typically have one or more legal structures for setting up corporations, or at least legal structures that are very similar in nature. We will not split hairs here about the exact meaning of the term corporation. Instead, we can understand that, for a business or organization to be **incorporated**, in broad lines, has three implications.   

* It means that the business or organization has its own legal identity, one that is separate from the owners, directors, and employees. The expression to “incorporate” pertains, thus, to the creation of a new legal body. This is often emphasized with the expression “Corporations are people”. 
* It means there is a clear legal distinction between those who own the company—a group that can include individuals as well as other legal entities—and those who direct it. 
* Finally, incorporation means that the owners’ personal assets are protected from the debts and legal issues a corporation faces (except in certain extreme circumstances such as fraud). The owners of a company, in other words, have **limited liability**.  

While the details of legal structures vary within jurisdictions, most will have one or more legal structures that broadly align with incorporation as described above. And commercial banks, as stated, are typically limited to for-profit corporate structures. This is certainly the case in the United Sates. 

In the United States, commercial banks can receive their charters—a concept which we will explain shortly—from either a state or the federal government.<sup>[6](#footnote6)</sup> Commercial banks that operate under a state charter are designated as **state banks**. Those that are members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by it and the relevant state authorities. State banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve are regulated by the FDIC and the relevant state authorities. In September 2024,<sup>[7](#footnote7)</sup> roughly 20% of state banks were also members of the Federal Reserve.  Most states require their banks to also be members of the FDIC. A state bank must obtain a branch charter from any state other than the one of its incorporation if it seeks to have operations there.  

State banks are often organized as C corporations or S corporations with the rights to operate as a bank. Additionally, certain states also allow commercial banks to be structured as limited liability companies (LLCs). State chartered commercial banks which are structured as an LLC can also participate in the deposit insurance program from the FDIC, as long as they have four corporate characteristics: perpetual succession, centralized management, limited liability, and free transferability of interests.<sup>[8](#footnote8)</sup> In some cases, an entity might have already been operating with different purposes before the organizers of the commercial bank seek to establish it as one. In other cases, the corporate entity will be created upon the founding of the commercial bank. 

State banks can only operate if they have a bank charter. But what exactly is a bank charter? Is it just a license?

Roughly speaking, a bank charter is a license. But strictly speaking, a bank charter is more encompassing. The expression “to charter a corporation” means to establish its legal existence with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. To “charter a bank” similarly means to establish the bank with various goals and rights through government or legislative approval. The key documents which contain these goals and rights—the articles of association and sometimes other certificates—are known as the **bank charter**, at least once approved by the proper government or legislative authorities. A physical certificate is commonly extended that serves as evidence of the bank charter.<sup>[9](#footnote9)</sup> 

A license can be extended to a business or organization without requiring fundamental changes in its aims and rights. Hence, that commercial banks are chartered in the United States, really indicates that a new sort of business is established, regardless of whether it concerns an entirely new corporation or an existing corporation that is granted a bank charter. The bank chartering process is extensive. Any state corporation that is granted a charter becomes subject to a whole new set of regulatory and legal requirements, next to those that apply merely to corporations.  

Commercial banks can also be granted charters at the federal level, more specifically from the **Office of the Comptroller of the Currency** (**OCC**), an independent bureau within the US Department of the Treasury. Commercial banks that are chartered and regulated by the OCC are called **national banks**. They are easily recognizable because they must include in their name the designation “national”, “national association”, or “N.A.” Commercial banks with a national bank charter are required to be members of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and the Federal Reserve. They are allowed to operate anywhere in the United States without requiring additional branch charters. 

The national chartering process for commercial banks contains two main stages: the preliminary approval stage and the organization phase. The former is basically an approval of the main plans behind the new national bank, while the latter is the phase in which the national bank is actually organized (e.g., establish articles of association and an organization certificate, start to raise capital, develop policies and procedures, hire a number of executive officers). The OCC grants or rejects final approval at the end of the organization phase.<sup>[10](#footnote10)</sup>

An intriguing aspect to national banks is that they are chartered in the deepest sense of the word. National banks are not just state-established corporations with a federal license. They are actually a special type of corporation, a foundational legal structure.<sup>[11](#footnote11)</sup> While they have a lot of similarities to the C corporations that we see at the state level, they are their own unique entity with special rules and regulations. Therefore, unlike with a state bank, you cannot not just take an existing corporation created at the state level and turn it into a national bank. You need to create a new type of legal entity at the federal level.   

Besides commercial banks, the OCC can also charter, in the full sense of the term, several other related types of for-profit corporations (including savings associations, industrial loan companies, special purpose banks, and others). Some of these entities are also officially called “national banks”. These chartering powers for the OCC are fairly extraordinary. 

To start, the legal establishment of businesses and organizations is largely the prerogative of the individual states. While many types of companies and organizations are *licensed* at a federal level—such as stock exchanges and investment companies—they are not usually *chartered*, in the full sense of the word, at the federal level. Additionally, while the federal government does occasionally charter, in the full sense of the word, for-profit enterprises, this occurs by special legislative acts in all other cases than those cases that fall under the scope of the OCC.<sup>[12](#footnote12)</sup> The OCC is, thus, the only federal agency that has the prerogative to legally create for-profit enterprises.

National banks are primarily governed by the federal law on national banks and administrative rulings by the OCC. That said, many of the laws and regulations mimic state practices in corporate law. National banks are also subject to federal judicial rulings, though at the end of the 20th century, the US Supreme Court decided to defer to the states on the general standard of care for the directors of national banks.<sup>[13](#footnote13)</sup>

There are various reasons for why commercial banks should be incorporated, whether prescribed by law or not. But at least one prominent reason is the assurance of limited liability. Without limited liability, running a bank would be an extremely risky venture. In the United States, this automatically eliminates proprietorships, general partnerships, and limited partnerships, all of which come with unlimited personal liability for the debts and obligations of the enterprise for all or some of the owners. While a limited liability partnership does offer limited liability for all partners (and only for their own debts and obligations, not those of their partners), they are only allowed for certain professions like doctors, architects, and accountants.

A key aspect to modern banking is that many commercial banks are actually legal entities within more complex legal organizational structures. 

These complex structures typically consist of both **operational** and **holding companies**. An operational company is just what we would standardly understand as a company, one that actively engages in the production, distribution, and sale of goods and services. We might describe a holding company as follows:

* It does not have any real business operations of its own. Instead it holds stakes in other companies. These are frequently majority stakes, but they can also be significant minority stakes. 
* In case the holding company has a majority stake in a particular company, that company is usually called a **subsidiary**. The holding company is then called the **parent company**. 
* The holding company is not usually involved in the day-to-day operations of the companies they own. That is the responsibility of the company’s executives and employees. However, due to its significant stock ownership, the holding company will typically be able to exert a lot of influence through the company’s board of directors and at shareholder meetings. The influence here is mainly on a strategic level. 
* The holding company’s main goal is to profit from their holdings of companies through dividends, and companies are usually owned for a very long time. The holding company will, of course, also hold assets other than stocks in companies, such as bonds, cash, and so on. 

The existence of these more complex legal organizational structures within banking necessitates additional laws and regulations. These govern, for instance, the types of activities and interactions that can take place between a commercial bank and other entities within the same legal organizational structure.<sup>[14](#footnote14)</sup>

Most of the well-known brands in banking are not represented by single companies, but by complex legal organizational structures. Often these organizational structures facilitate the management of regional operations. 

In this manner, for instance, HSBC holdings plc—a holding company based in the United Kingdom—ultimately owns a variety of companies involved in banking and financial services, including HSBC Bank Mexico, HSBC Bank USA, HSBC Securities USA, HSBC Bank Egypt, and so on. For another example, when we refer to Citibank in the United States, we normally mean Citibank, N.A., the national bank, headquartered in New York City. This national bank is actually owned by a holding company called Citigroup. Next to Citibank, N.A., Citigroup ultimately owns the shares of many other operational and holding companies within the financial services industry. 


## 3. Banking 

Commercial banks are the core modern institutions that engage in **banking**, which we here understand as the provision of three core activities: the acceptance and management of deposits, the extension of credit, and the creation and destruction of money. That said, there are various other types of institutions which also engage in all three activities. 

In the United States, the two main institutions to know are **savings associations** (also known as **savings banks**, **thrifts**, or **savings and loans**) and **credit unions**.

Savings associations are very similar to prototypical commercial banks, but at least traditionally focus much more actively on products and services for savings, and not as much on checking and payment products. Historically, they have also focused mostly on extending retail mortgages. Many other countries also have financial institutions that are similarly focused, at least historically, on offering products and services for savings, such as **building societies** in the United Kingdom, Australia, and New Zealand; and **sparkassen** and **spaarbanken** in Germany and the Netherlands, respectively.  

Many savings associations in the United States are corporations owned by shareholders. Sometimes they are also for-profit **mutually owned** institutions, meaning that shares are owned by those involved with the savings association (e.g., as account holders, borrowers). Savings associations can also have a hybrid model of ownership. While savings associations do receive their own charters—either nationally by the OCC or from a state agency—they are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks.<sup>[15](#footnote15)</sup>

Next, **credit unions** offer similar services to commercial banks and savings associations, but are formed as non-profit corporations. That is, profits are not distributed to shareholders that own the bank, but instead are utilized to improve or decrease the costs of the products and services offered. Typically these institutions are also mutually owned, as is the case with many savings associations. Credit unions are typically focused on retail and small business clients. 

While savings association are regulated in a similar manner to commercial banks on the national level, credit unions are actually regulated and chartered by the **National Credit Union Administration** (**NCUA**). At the state level, credit unions are sometimes chartered and regulated by the same agency as banks and savings associations. Sometimes, however, they also have their own regulatory agency. Credit unions cannot be FDIC-insured. Instead, the NCUA manages a special insurance fund for credit unions called the **National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund**. 

As with savings associations, we can find institutions that are similar to the American credit unions in other countries. The building societies mentioned earlier are actually very much like credit unions as they are also non-profit. In Ireland, the same types of institutions are standardly called **mutual banks**. So-called **kredietbanken** in the Netherlands tend to be non-profit institutions, mutually owned by entrepreneurs that offer bank services and loans to entrepreneurs. 

Many common distinctions further clarify the nature of commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions. With a **community bank**, for example, we typically mean a commercial bank that has a local focus, and we similarly often speak of a **community credit union**. For another example, any commercial bank that only services other commercial banks and savings associations as well as their officers, directors, and is commonly known as a **banker’s bank**. A **corporate credit union** is, in turn, one that only similarly services other credit unions. 

Besides commercial banks, savings associations, and credit unions, there are some more exotic entities that seem to engage in all the three core activities of banking in the United States: the acceptance and management of deposits, the extension of credit, and the creation and destruction of money. 

An **industrial loan company** (**ILC**), for example, can offer all the same services as a commercial bank, including checking and savings accounts, though they cannot guarantee that deposits will be made available within seven days for (even if in practice funds are often available sooner). They can only be chartered by certain states and are subject to different regulatory regimes than commercial banks and savings associations. For example, ILCs can also be owned by nonfinancial companies. In this way, for example, both Toyota and BMW operate banks in the United States called the Toyota Financial Savings Bank and the BMW Bank of North America.<sup>[16](#footnote16)</sup>

In sum, the main institutions in the United States that are involved in the three core activities of banking— the acceptance and management of deposits, the extension of credit, and the creation and destruction of money—are commercial banks. However, there are various other, much less prominent, types of institutions that also offer all these services. Collectively, we can call them **banks and credit unions**,<sup>[17](#footnote17)</sup> even though various of those institutions, like savings associations and ILCs, are, in a legal sense, technically not always defined as banks. 

Many institutions in the United States might carry the label “bank” in the name, but are not really banks in the sense of the types of institutions just described. In fact, they sometimes are fairly far removed from that notion. 

Consider, for instance, **investment banks**. These are institutions which have among their core activities helping companies and governments with raising capital (through stock and bond issuances); providing advice on mergers, acquisitions, and corporate restructurings; and helping manage the investment portfolios of clients such as pension funds, hedge funds, family offices, and wealthy individuals. Typically, these institutions would not provide commercial banking services, even though they may be part of a larger brand that also includes one or more entities occupied with commercial banking. 

In some cases, making sense of the label “bank” will be a fairly complicated exercise. A common distinction that can bring such complications is that of a **private bank**. 

Sometimes an entity called a private bank is just as commercial bank that is privately owned (i.e., the stock is not traded on a public exchange). Often, however, the term also refers to for-profit companies that are specialized in offering wealth management services to high net-worth individuals and families, a service known as **private banking**. 

The possible services that are provided by private banks in the latter sense can vary widely and may include checking and savings account services, advice on investments and taxes, estate planning, custody services, high-value mortgages, and so on. In some cases, such private banks are just specialized commercial banks. But in other cases, these “banks” are really somewhat different types of institutions. 

Many other countries and regions will have banking institutions that show similarities to those in the United States, particularly in the developed economies. We have already made some comparisons throughout this section. It should be re-iterated, of course, that all countries and regions will have their own idiosyncracies and features. And in some cases, the landscape can look substantially different. The four large banks that, for example, dominate China—the Bank of China, the China Construction Bank, the Agricultural Bank of China, and the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China—are predominantly state-owned, presenting a marked difference with the United States and other developed economies.
