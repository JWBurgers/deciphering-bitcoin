# Chapter 3: Europe's Economic Rise

After the Fall of the Western Roman Empire, most of Europe became
markedly less complex in its economic, cultural, and administrative
organization. Compared to Roman Europe, society by the end of the Early
Middle Ages was predominantly agricultural and manorial. As the period
drew to a close, however, Europe began to enter into a long period of
economic ascendancy that saw an expansion of trade, advances in
financial and business practices, population growth, growth in urban
centers, and the introduction of new technologies. With some
interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the end of
the Middle Ages.

In the early modern period, Europe then really began to diverge
economically and technologically from much of the rest of the world, a
gap that widened dramatically during the Industrial Revolution. While
Great Britain began to industrialize substantially in the second half of
the eighteenth century, for most of Europe that process largely
transpired in the nineteenth century. Some European extensions–such as
the United States, Canada, and Australia–experienced similar
transformations. Japan was the only main non-European society that
industrialized before the Second World War, namely in the late
nineteenth and early twentieth century.

The modern history of money, payments, and banking largely begins during
Europe's economic ascendancy in the High and Late Middle Ages. Our
purpose in this chapter is to provide the background conditions against
which this economic rise began, how it evolved over time, and the
possible causes of Europe's economic and technological ascendancy. In
*Chapter 1*, we describe the economic, political, and social conditions
in the Early Middle Ages. Chapter 2 then turns to the economic expansion
that sets in around the start of the High Middle Ages. In *Section 3*,
we briefly explore the drivers of this economic boom.

## 1. The Early Middle Ages

From the end of the fourth century onwards, the Roman Empire was
permanently divided into a Western Roman Empire and an Eastern Roman
Empire, each with their own emperors.[^1] At the time of the split, the
western half of the Empire roughly covered the area of Continental
Europe below the Rhine and Danube until modern-day Croatia and
Bosnia-Herzegovina in the East, the island of Great Britain until
Hadrian's Wall, and the northern coastline of Africa stretching from
modern-day Morocco to Egypt.

The fall of the Western Roman Empire is typically dated to 476. In that
year Odovacer–a commander of various groups of foederati, barbarian
troops serving the Western Empire–deposed Romulus Augustus in Ravenna,
and declared himself the King of Italy. But the ending of the Western
Roman Empire was, of course, hardly a single event.

Great Britain was left by Roman troops already in the early fifth
century, ushering in an era of immigration by continental
tribes–notably the Saxons, Angels, Jutes, and Frisians–that lasted
well-into the sixth century. The Vandals had conquered the northern part
of Africa by 439 and famously sacked the city of Rome in 455. And many
areas in Roman Continental Europe had fully or largely already come, by
476, under the control of various tribes, including the Ostrogoths,
Visigoths, Franks, Burgundians, Lombards, and others. Many of these
tribes had been displaced into Western and Central Europe by the
extremely violent incursions of the Huns–a people who originated
somewhere deep from within the Eurasian Steppe–during the late fourth
and fifth centuries.

When the final emperor, Romulus Augustus, was deposed by Odovacer,
Europe had, therefore, to a large extent already transformed into a
collection of tribal kingdoms. These kingdoms were only loosely
associated with certain regions and the "inhabitants" identified more
with charismatic warrior kings and leaders, rather than certain
territories. The tribal peoples were largely agricultural and pastoral,
and would have mostly settled in the countryside and in small villages.
The existent inhabitants often would have continued to live within these
new tribal kingdoms. Those who identified as Romans, particularly, would
have often remained in the larger towns and cities. Identity was
probably quite fluid in this period around the fall of the Western Roman
Empire.

The period of European history from the fall of the Western Roman Empire
until the year 1000 is standardly called the Early Middle Ages.
Traditionally, it has also often been called the **Dark Ages**. That
label was inspired by the supposed political, societal, and economic
collapse of Roman Europe in the fifth century and the region's
subsequent period of prolonged stagnation.

Modern historians and archaeologists have varied judgements on the
severity and speed of the impact of the events of the fifth century, and
the degree to which a characterization of the subsequent period as the
"Dark Ages" is actually justified.[^2] After the fall of the Empire, for
example, certain institutions such as systems of taxation and
administration, the church hierarchy, and Roman coinage continued to be
maintained in many of the new kingdoms.[^3] Writing about the Franks in
the sixth century, Gregory of Tours could still identify those families
which belonged to the Roman senatorial classes.[^4] Overall, however,
scholars do broadly agree that the European territories formerly within
the Western Roman Empire were markedly different by the ninth and tenth
centuries than under Roman rule.

Roman political, commercial, and social life had centered around its
towns. But during the Early Middle Ages, their populations dwindled,
often drastically. Many towns such as Trier and Tongeren were
practically abandoned for a long period.[^5] While Rome might have
housed almost a million inhabitants in the early part of the millennium,
by the early ninth century that number had dropped to a few tens of
thousands.[^6] By the ninth century, European building standards were
much less sophisticated. Scholarship and literacy was much less
widespread and mostly confined to the monasteries. Administrative and
taxation systems were less sophisticated. And the economy was largely
manorial, self-sufficient, and less specialized than in Roman times.

In particular, any trade in commodity goods–which was the main form of
voluntary economic exchange–was generally at a local level.
Long-distance trade was, by contrast to Roman times, a minor economic
activity.[^7] According to De Roover,[^8] "\[w\]hat little
\[long-distance trade\] survived was carried on by groups of travelling
merchants who catered for the rich by selling them luxuries or who
exploited the poor by charging high prices for necessities in times of
famine or distress". Given the hazardous conditions, a merchant "had to
possess a great deal of physical endurance and be ready to face many
hardships".[^9] In addition, "moral stamina and some intellectual
baggage were indispensable prerequisites to success".[^10]

The fall of the Western Roman Empire did not impact all parts of Roman
Europe equally. The overall decline seems to have been most significant
in the north of Europe.[^11] Most extremely, in what had once been Roman
Britain, few remnants of classical civilization apparently survived the
start of the Early Middle Ages, as pagan Anglo-Saxon control over the
region spread in the fifth and sixth centuries.[^12] The usage of Roman
coins, for instance, stopped within a few decades of the departure of
the Roman armies.[^13] Despite the overall stronger regression in the
north, Dean and Waley still offer the following reflection on the
transformation that took place in Italy in the Early Middle Ages:[^14]

> Between the fifth and tenth centuries AD \[municipal\] institutions
> were shattered and the very nature of political and social authority
> was reshaped by a series of events which included the conquests and
> settlements of Goths and Lombards. The gradual but profound
> transformation of these centuries involved a great contraction in city
> life and an almost complete break in urban government. A few of the
> larger cities, among them Rome itself, and Milan, survived as
> important centers of population, but most of the ancient
> municipalities must have declined into sordid if picturesque decay,
> villages in a setting of classical ruins.

The area of Europe outside of the Western Roman Empire was predominantly
inhabited by agricultural and tribal societies. This very much remained
that way after the events of the fifth century. In general, these
peripheral areas were also marked by very limited urbanization, little
long-distance trade, unspecialized economic activities, low rates of
literacy, and so on. The regions in scope here are those north of the
Rhine, as well as north and north-east of the Danube; modern-day
Scandinavia; and the parts of the British Isles that were not settled by
the Romans, including modern-day Ireland and Scotland. Notably, excluded
from the overall picture of an economically undeveloped and rural Europe
during the Early Middle Ages were the Iberian Peninsula and Byzantium.

The Visigoths, a Germanic tribe, notably sacked Rome in 410 and then in
the early fifth century established a kingdom centered around
southwestern Gaul, a Roman name for a region that encompassed much of
Western Europe including modern-day France and Belgium. The Visigoths
then increasingly expanded into the Iberian Peninsula. After a decisive
defeat by the Franks at the Battle of Vouillé in 507, the Visigoths
largely retreated into the Iberian Peninsula, eventually establishing
their capital in Toledo. The experience of Visigothic Spain was much the
same as the rest of Roman Europe at the beginning of the Early Middle
Ages. However, in 711 the region was invaded by Arab Muslims with
profound consequences.

From the early seventh century onwards, Arab Muslim forces rapidly
expanded their territory from the original base in Medina, first under
the leadership of Mohammed (570 – 632) and later under the leadership
of the Rashidun (632 – 661) and Umayyad (661 – 750) caliphs.[^15] They
invaded the Iberian Peninsula in 711 and were only stopped near Paris at
the Battle of Tours in 732 by the Franks under the military leadership
of Charles Martel (c. 688 – 741), also known as "the hammer". From then
onwards the Muslim occupation of Europe was largely confined to the
Iberian Peninsula. After the takeover of the Caliphate by the Abbasids
(750 – 1258), the Umayyads actually fled to the Iberian Peninsula and
established the **Emirate of Córdoba**, the first Islamic state
independent from the Caliphate. Under Muslim rule, the Islamic region of
the Peninsula–then known as **Al Andalus**–appears to have been much
more urbanized and developed than the rest of Europe until the end of
the Early Middle Ages.

Next, the Eastern Roman Empire continued to exist after Romulus Augustus
was deposed in the West. We commonly call it **Byzantium** from this
point onwards–after the Greek colony that was originally established
at Constantinople–though the inhabitants themselves would have
probably just seen themselves as Romans and their polity as the
continuation of the Roman Empire. Byzantium was much less affected by
the barbarian migrations and invasions that marked Europe in the fourth,
fifth, and sixth centuries. After 476, Byzantium continued to thrive,
even conquering much of the formerly West Roman Mediterranean under the
leadership of **Justinian I** (482–565) in the sixth century. Another
notable way in which Byzantium diverged from Europe in this period is by
Greek replacing Latin as the primary language.

Byzantium shrank significantly in the seventh century as a result of the
Arab Muslim invasions. Notably, it lost much of the Levant and adjacent
territories in the Near East, as well as its territories on the north
African coast. From the early eighth century onwards, the boundaries of
the Byzantine Empire continued to shift substantially until the end of
the Early Middle Ages. If we largely confine ourselves, however, to the
core region of Byzantium, namely Constantinople, Anatolia, and Greece,
the empire seems to have been, in general, substantially more wealthy
and urbanized than the rest of Europe during the Early Middle Ages.

A key aspect about Europe during the Early Middle Ages was the frequent
waves of raiding, migration, and invasion. We have already mentioned the
Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, Franks, Saxons, and other tribes that
transformed Roman Europe in the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries.
While Islam was confined to the Iberian Peninsula by the middle of the
eighth century, Muslims nevertheless continued to intensively raid
Mediterranean Europe until the tenth century. Many Europeans were taken
as slaves to Africa and the Near East. Other notable waves of raiding,
migration, and invasion during the Early Middle Ages came from the
Magyars and Vikings.

From the late ninth until the middle of the tenth century, much of
Western and Central Europe was the subject of intense and extremely
violent cavalry raids by the Magyars, a people that originally came from
the steppes east of the Carpathian mountains. At the Battle of Lechfeld
(955), the Magyars suffered a heavy defeat against the East Frankians
under the leadership of King Otto I (912 – 973). The raids ended and
the Magyars settled in the region of modern-day Hungary.

The Vikings, in turn, is a collective name for the various peoples from
Denmark, Norway, and Sweden at the time. At the turn of the ninth
century, they began raiding the British Isles and much of Continental
Europe, predominantly along the coastlines and river routes. Later in
the ninth century, they also began to migrate and settle in certain
areas. Notable lasting Viking settlements and integration with local
populations occurred in modern-day Scotland, Ireland, England, France,
and in an area that spans across modern-day Ukraine, Belarus, and
Russia.

In the region of modern-day England, under the leadership of the king of
Wessex, Alfred the Great (c. 849 – 899), the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms
united to halt the progress of the Vikings by the end of the ninth
century. The territory in Eastern and Northern England settled by
Vikings and governed by their laws–known as **Danelaw**
(865–954)–was eventually integrated back into Anglo-Saxon territory
under the leadership of Alfred's son Edward the Elder (c. 874 – 924).
The entire unified territory increasingly became known as "England" and
its people as the "English". Supposedly, Athelstan (c. 894 – 937), son
of Edward the Elder, was the first to use the title "King of the
English". In the early eleventh century, Cnut the Great (c. 995–1035)
briefly established the so-called North Sea Empire, consisting of
England (1016), Denmark (1018), and Norway (1028). Supposedly, he was
the first to use the title "King of England". Shortly after Cnut the
Great's death, in 1042, England came back under Anglo-Saxon rule with
the ascendancy of Edward the Confessor (1003 – 1066) to the throne.

In Ireland, Scotland, and other parts of the British Isles, the Vikings
also made lasting settlements, notably founding cities such as Dublin
and Cork. In Western Europe, the Viking leader Rollo in 911 made a deal
with Charles the Simple (879 – 929), the Carolingian king of West
Francia (or simply Francia). In exchange for the territory of
Normandy–or "land of the Northmen"–Rollo would convert to
Christianity, swear allegiance to Charles, and defend West Francia
against other Viking raiders.[^16]

Finally, a group of mostly Swedish Vikings known as the Varangians
established a large state known as Kyivan Rus in the late ninth century.
The territory eventually spanned the modern-day territory of Ukraine,
Belarus, and Russia. The state originally had its capital at Kyiv, but
eventually started to splinter into different principalities in the
eleventh century. The Varangians that settled in the region mixed with
the Slavs, Finnic, and other peoples, and eventually the inhabitants of
the region largely adopted the Slavic identity.

In the Early Middle Ages, fairly large territories were, at times,
united under single rulers. The Ostrogoths ruled Italy and neighboring
areas between 493 and 553. In the sixth century, Justinian I, emperor of
the Eastern Roman Empire or Byzantium, gained control over much of
southern Europe. The Anglo-Saxons largely unified England by the end of
the 10th century, and Kyivan Rus was also a fairly large state by the
end of the tenth century. But most notable with regards to territorial
unification in the Early Middle Ages are certainly the Franks.

Clovis I (c. 466 – 511), king of the Salian Franks, united the various
Frankish tribes during his reign across a territory roughly equivalent
to Roman Gaul, a region that encompassed much of Western Europe
including modern-day France and Belgium. After his death, Clovis'
territory was split into several separate Frankish kingdoms. The
Merovingian dynasty that ruled these various kingdoms–named after
Clovis' grandfather Merovech–lasted until 751. At this point, the
Merovingian kings were deposed and the Franks united under the son of
Charles Martel, **Pepin the Short** (c. 714 – 768).

Pepin rapidly began expanding the territory of the united Franks. That
expansion continued under the reign of his son, **Charles the Great**
(c. 748 – 814). The latter, also known as **Charlemagne**, was famously
crowned Holy Roman Emperor by Pope Leo III in 800 to revive the idea of
the Western Roman Empire, at least in the mind of the pope.
Charlemagne's kingdom covered practically all of Western and Central
Europe. He was succeeded by his son Louis the Pious (778 – 840). After
the death of Louis, the empire was divided among his sons into three
large kingdoms through the **Treaty of Verdun** (843): West Francia,
roughly the region of modern France and Belgium; East Francia, roughly
the region of modern Germany and some surrounding territories; and
Middle Francia, a large strip of land from the Low Countries into
northern Italy.

While many polities during the Early Middle Ages, thus, covered large
territories, they were often really little more than loosely united and
usually not closely governed and administered. Throughout the period,
influence was instead largely with local and regional rulers. The rule
of Charlemagne over the united Franks and various other peoples might
have been more organized and one of the few such exceptions to the
decentralized political character of the Early Middle Ages. In any case,
nothing like the organized rule of the Western Empire over Roman Europe
was achieved in the period.[^17]

In addition to England, we see the origins of two key modern European
states arise in the wake of the Treaty of Verdun. While Middle Francia
quickly disintegrated into various regions after the treaty, West
Francia and East Francia are the precursors to modern France and
Germany.

In West Francia, the Carolingian dynasty lasted until 987 when Hugh
Capet (c. 939 – 996) assumed the throne. During the early years of the
**Capetian dynasty** (987–1328), West Francia or Francia increasingly
became known as France. While the main line of the Capetians ended with
the death of Charles IV in 1328, two cadet branches–the **House of
Valois** (1328 – 1589) and the **House of Bourbon** (1589 – 1792)–
essentially ruled France until the start of the **French Revolution**
(1789).

For almost a hundred years, France was then marked by substantial
political and social turmoil, shifting between dictatorship,
constitutional monarchy, and republican rule.[^18] After a bad defeat
against Prussia in the Franco-Prussian War (1870), Napoleon
III–Napoleon's nephew who was essentially a military dictator–was
captured and the Third Republic (1870 – 1940) was proclaimed. While the
war was devastating to France, the country was finally able to enter a
period of real political and social stability uniting the various
republican, liberal, and conservative factions.

In East Francia the Carolingian dynasty would last until 911. In 918,
Henry the Fowler (876 – 936) was elected the King of the East Franks.
This title passed to his son Otto I (912 – 973) in 936. The latter was,
in 962, also crowned **Holy Roman Emperor** by the pope. During the
tenth and eleventh centuries, the people in this region would
increasingly be called Teutons or Germans. The King of the East Franks
eventually became known as the **King of Germany** and for almost a
millenium the king was an elected position. Before 1356, voting was the
prerogative of powerful nobles though voting rights were not always
clear. Subsequently, voting was more organized and the prerogative of
certain political rulers and ecclesiastical authorities, about a dozen
at the turn of the nineteenth century. Usually, the king would also be
crowned Holy Roman Emperor by the pope at some point during their reign.
While the Kingdom of Germany was always the core of the Empire, it also
included other territories. The Holy Roman Empire and the Kingdom of
Germany only ceased as political entities in 1806, after Napoleon had
crushed Austrian, Prussian, Russian, and English forces at the **Battle
of Austerlitz** (1805).

Unlike England and France, the Kingdom of Germany as well as the broader
Holy Roman Empire remained more decentralized political entities. Until
1806, the main power within the Kingdom of Germany and the Holy Roman
Empire was the **Habsburg Dynasty**. Initially a minor noble family from
Switzerland, they gained control over a sizeable land area around
modern-day Austria in the thirteenth century, officially becoming
recognized from then onwards as the Dukes of Austria. In 1358, the ducal
title was elevated to Archduke, though it was fully recognized by the
emperor only in 1453. The Habsburgs slowly expanded their territory in
central Europe during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and from
the sixteenth century onwards became one of the foremost European
landowners. From the first half of the fifteenth century, every King of
Germany elected, but one exception (Charles VII of Bavaria, 1742–1745),
was a Habsburg. Yet, even the Habsburgs had limited influence over other
the territories within the Kingdom of Germany and the broader Holy Roman
Empire.

The **Congress of Vienna** (1814 – 15) was a series of international
political meetings where European leaders attempted to draw out a
political order that could guarantee stability and peace after the
defeat of Napoleon. One product was the creation of a German
Confederation (1815 – 1866) consisting of 39 states. In 1804, the
Archduke of Austria Francis II (1768 – 1835) had wanted a title equal
to that of Napoleon so rebranded the Habsburg-controlled lands as the
**Austrian Empire** and declared himself emperor. It was the main state
in the loose confederacy of German states, and the Habsburgs held the
presidency throughout the confederation's existence.

In the 1860s, the Prussians, who had become a formidable power by the
end of the eighteenth century, began a process of German unification
through the incorporation of allied states and conquest. After the
defeat of Austria in the Austro-Prussian War (1866), the German
Confederation was dissolved and the Austrian Empire–which transformed
into the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary in 1867–receded from German
affairs. After the Prussian victory over France in the Franco-Prussian
War (1870 – 1871), the various German states were formally unified on
January 18, 1871 into the Prussian-led **German Empire** with **King
Wilhelm I** of Prussia (1797 – 1888) crowned as the first emperor.

## 2. Economic expansion

By the thirteenth century, relations among the nobility in Europe were
marked by lordship, vassalage, and fiefs. A **fief** was most commonly a
piece of land held in tenure by a **vassal** from a **lord**; that is,
the vassal had the right to use and derive benefits from the land, but
technically at least it remained the property of the lord.[^19] Vassals
in return would provide certain services, particularly with regards to
military campaigns. A key contribution was that of mounted knights which
required substantial funds to furnish. In the case of small fiefs, the
vassal might just serve with a few members of the household. In the case
of larger fiefs, dozens of knights might have been provided. Vassals
would also provide other types of military personnel such as archers and
infantrymen as well as contribute provisions, money, and other types of
support for military campaigns.

The lord-vassal relationships existed between monarchs and higher
nobles. Those higher nobels, in turn, standardly had numerous vassals of
their own. Sometimes, the hierarchy may have extended even further
downwards. The relationship between a lord and a vassal was a personal
one. It was cemented by an **act of homage**, a ceremony in which the
vassal swore loyalty and service to the lord in exchange for protection.
Typically the relationship involved a fief, though this was not always
the case. The opportunity to participate in the lord-vassal structure
was in the thirteenth century mostly hereditary with titles and fiefs
passing through noble families over generations. Exceptions were,
however, sometimes made.

The description of the lord-vassal system above is, of course, stylized.
Even in the European regions to which it was most applicable for the
thirteenth century, laws and customs varied considerably and other forms
of landownership and military recruitment also existed. Furthermore, the
lord-vassal system was not the predominant mode for noble relationships,
military recruitment, and land organization everwhere. Iceland, for
example, was quite different in this regard, both during the Free State
period (c. 930 – 1262) and under subsequent Norwegian (1262 – 1380)
and Norwegian-Danish (1380 – 1814) rule.[^20]

The lord-vassal system seems to have obtained its first real foothold
among the Franks and the origins of the system are commonly attributed
to reforms and events that transpired particularly during the rule of
the Carolignians.[^21]

To manage their Empire, the Carolignians relied on the Frankish elite
for local and regional administrative, judicial, and military tasks. The
key administrators were counts, but also included dukes, marquesses,
viscounts, and castellans (later barons). These were standardly
appointed positions. They typically came with lands that were held for
life, though sometimes the rights to the land extended to subsequent
generations. The Carolignians used these offices as well as independent
land grants in securing military service from nobles. Such practices
were certainly sensible given that the coinage systems which had existed
in much of Europe during Roman times had almost entirely
dissappeared.[^22] Land and offices, therefore, functioned as "money".
Over time, the local and regional offices of the Carolignian Empire
morphed into hereditary positions accompanied by fiefs.

While medieval historians are shy to use the term today, the lord-vassal
system, was a paradigmatic feature of what has long been called
**feudalism** or the **feudal system** in history books, the term
"feudalism" originating in the eighteenth century from the medieval
Latin word for fief (*feodum*).

The lord-vassal system developed slowly over time, at varying rates in
different parts of Europe. The system is associated primarily with the
High Middle Ages. A developed lord-vassal system already marked France
in the eleventh century and possibly already much earlier. It also
marked England by the end of the eleventh century. There is a
long-standing discussion among historians whether it was mainly
instituted by William the Conqueror (1066) or already existed in some
fairly developed form before the Norman invasion. The lord-vassal system
reached its peak in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and, then,
declined slowly over time.[^23]

Hence, from the Carolignian rulers onwards a **hereditary aristocracy**
(or nobility) slowly emerged throughout Europe: a class of society
holding special political, social, and economic privileges. It was
supported to a significant extent by the lord-vassal system. While
Europe had rulers in the form of Kings and a Holy Roman Emperor that
technically controlled large territories, Europe remained in practice
decentralized from the end of the Early Middle Ages onwards. England was
a notable exception and already exercised more organized centralized
control at this time than most of the Continent (though far from that of
a modern state). Only towards the end of the Middle Ages and into the
early modern period do more strongly centralized states generally begin
to emerge.

Another institution associated with the Middle Ages is the relationship
between lords and serfs. A **serf** was a peasant that worked on an was
tied to the land of a lord. They enjoyed the protection of a lord in
exchange for a share of the agricultural output, the payment of dues and
taxes, and other the provision of other services, including military
service. Even though the relationship with a lord was normally highly
exploitative, a serf was not a slave. While a slave was seen as property
and did not enjoy legal rights, serfs did indeed have certain legal
rights even if limited. During the Middle Ages, some peasants also
enjoyed more rights like the freedom of movement and the freedom to
enter into contracts.

In the English-American tradition, the socio-economic system of largely
self-sufficient estates controlled by lords and worked by serfs is
normally called **manorialism**, though continental historians
frequently also characterize it as a paradigmatic feature of feudalism.
The institution of serfdom can be traced back at least to third century
when many Romans fled the towns and cities for the countryside. These
so-called *coloni* would work the land and share part of their output
with the landowner. Under the rule of Diocletian, these *coloni* were
bound to the land and not allowed to leave without permission. The state
became hereditary. While the Roman economy had relied heavily on slave
labor, during the Early Middle Ages, slave labor largely receded within
of Europe. By the start of the High Middle Ages, serfdom was a far more
common institution than slavery. Regions naturally varied substantially
in how much they were marked by serfdom.

By the end of the Early Middle Ages, Europeans had also largely become
Christian and followers of the **Catholic Church**, which was the name
for the mainstream Christian church at the time ("catholic" means
"universal").[^24]

During the fourth and fifth centuries, many of the barbarian tribes
turned to Christianity. However, they usually adopted a particular
variant known as **Arianism**, a Christian doctrine which had arisen in
the fourth century and which saw Christ as not being co-eternal or
consubstantial with God.[^25] This conviction was, in contrast, to the
official doctrine set at the Catholic Church's first **ecumenical
council** at Nicaea in 325, which decreed that God, the holy spirit, and
Christ were co-eternal and consubstantial.[^26]

By contrast to the other Barbarian tribes, Clovis I in 508 converted to
Catholic Christianity. Various other continental tribes subsequently
began converting to Catholic Christianity from Arianism or Paganism,
including the Visigoths, Lombards, and Burgundians. The Irish supposedly
converted to Catholic Christianity during the fifth and sixth centuries.
According to legend, much of the effort was led by St. Patrick. The
Anglo-Saxons in England mostly converted to Catholic Christianity from
Paganism in the seventh century. Charlemagne forced the continental
Saxons to convert at the end of the eighth century. By the end of the
ninth century, Christianity began spreading among Viking settlements and
in Scandinavia. In the late tenth century, the first ruler of the Polish
state, Mieszko I (c. 922 – 992), was baptized. In the early eleventh
century, the Hungarian king Stephen I (c. 975 – 1038) began promoting
Christianity among his people.

So, by the end of the tenth century, much of Europe was Christianized.
While some regions, particularly the Balkans and parts of Eastern
Europe, were converted under the auspices of Byzantium, much of Europe
followed the Catholic Church in Rome. Under Charlemagne's reign,
substantial effort had been expended on standardizing practices and
customs in churches and monasteries across western and central Europe.
He had also standardized the Roman alphabet in his effort to preserve
books, manuscripts, and learning. By the end of the tenth century, the
raids, migrations, and invasions that had so marked the Early Middle
Ages also had largely subsided. Only Eastern Europe would experience
another such event, namely the invasion of the Mongols in the thirteenth
century. Finally, the lord-vassal system was emerging and would
eventually spread throughout much of the continent by the thirteenth
century.

It is in this environment of somewhat more order and stability at the
turn of second millenium that Europe enters into a long period of
economic ascendancy. The subsequent period saw an expansion of trade,
advances in financial and business practices, population growth,
increasing urbanization, and the introduction of new technologies. With
some interruptions and fluctuations, these trends continued until the
end of the Middle Ages. The key era of economic development was probably
the late twelfth to early fourteenth century–also known as the **long
thirteenth century**–after which economic expansion was interrupted by
wars, famines, and epidemics, notably the **Black Death** (1347–1351).
While traditionally most of the Late Middle Ages (1300–1500) was seen
as one of abject depression by historians, it now appears the
interruptions to economic progress were not nearly as severe and
long-lasting as originally envisioned.[^27]

Data on population growth and urbanization can give some sense of the
developments during the High and Late Middle Ages. A modern degree of
accuracy for such data is beyond our reach. But, indicatively, it has
been estimated that, from 1000 to 1300, the population of Europe roughly
doubled. While around 35 to 40 cities had a population of 20,000 or more
in the year 1000, this was probably closer to 100 in the year 1300.
While likely only four cities had more than 50,000 inhabitants at the
outset of the new millenium, this seems to have tripled by 1300. The
largest of these was Paris with an estimated 160,000 inhabitants.
Venice, Milan, and Genoa, each had around 100,00 inhabitants.[^28] Other
sizeable cities in Italy included Bologna, Rome, Naples, and Palermo.
Elsewhere in Europe, the populations have been estimated to be around
50,000 for Ghent; 35,000 for Bruges; 35,000 to 45,000 for London; 40,000
for Cologne.[^29] While urbanization only seems to have increased by a
fraction for Europe overall during the High Middle Ages, supposedly the
rate of urbanization approached 30% in northern Italy and the Low
Countries by 1300.[^30]

The Black Death and other epidemics decimated the European population in
the fourteenth century. Many towns and regions lost over a quarter of
their population or more to the Black Death alone. Nevertheless, by
1500, the European population size seems to have largely recovered.[^31]
Though the overall urbanization rate for Europe was only somewhat larger
by the end of the Late Middle Ages than at the start, the number of
really large cities had increased substantially. Whereas about twelve
cities had 50,000 or more inhabitants in 1300, this had increased to
around twenty-one in 1500.[^32]

Estimates of economic growth rates before the nineteenth century are
little more than informed guesses. But it is clear that pre-industrial
economic growth rates were moderate compared to modern growth rates.
According to Maddisson, the economic growth rate for Europe between 1000
and 1500 was, on average, around a quarter of a percentage point.[^33]
Throughout most of human history, economic growth has largely been
absorbed into population growth. Per capita GDP growth and living
standards would, therefore, have generally increased very little, a
phenomenon known as the **malthusian trap**. The same was largely true
for Europe in the High and Late Middle Ages, though two regions start to
show noticeable per capita GDP growth in the Late Middle Ages: Holland
and Flanders.[^34]

Middle Francia had quickly disintegrated after the Treaty of Verdun.
During the High Middle Ages, the region historically referred to as the
**Low Countries**–approximately the area of the modern-day
Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg–comprised a patchwork of duchies,
counties, and other semi-independent territorial entities. At the end of
the fourteenth century, however, a large part of the region came under
the control of the **House of Burgundy**–a cadet branch of the French
royal House of Valois. Over time, the Burgundian State–the name for
the Duchy of Burgundy and the other territories under its control–was
able to expand its sphere of control over the Low Countries through
conquest, land purchases, diplomacy, and inheritance. The area
collectively became known as the **Burgundian Netherlands**. While the
territories remained semi-independent, the Burgundians did increase
their control over them with time, for instance, with regards to the
coinage.[^35] Burgundian control of the Low Countries continued until
1482.

It is within these political circumstances that the two primary economic
regions of the Low Countries, Flanders and Holland, began to display per
capita GDP growth. Some of the key economic activities at the time in
the region were the production of textiles, the maritime and fishing
industry, and trade.

The **First Great Divergence** (1500 – 1800) coincides with the early
modern period and refers to the main initial divergence between Europe
and some of its extensions, notably the United States, and the rest of
the world, especially China, in terms of economic development,
productivity, and technological progress. According to Maddisson,[^36]
Europe experienced a growth rate of a little over 0.4 percent from 1500
to 1820. Per capita GDP growth was still very modest at about 0.15
percent for Western Europe and 0.1 percent for Eastern Europe.[^37]

Two main European areas showed per capita growth rates in the early
modern period. One area was the **Dutch Republic**, particularly the
region of Holland. The other main area of growth in the early modern
period was England and later also Scotland. While northern Italy was the
economic center of gravity at the outset of the sixteenth century, this
shifted towards northwestern Europe as the early modern period
progressed, particularly to the Dutch Republic and England.[^38]

Burgundian control of the Low Countries remained until 1482. After the
death of Charles the Bold at the Battle of Nancy (1477), Louis XI
brought the Duchy of Burgundy, which was technically a fief of the
French King, back under his control. Charles' only child, Mary of
Burgundy (1457–1482), retained possession of the Burgundian
Netherlands. In 1477, she married Maximilian I of Habsburg (1459–1519),
the eldest son of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick III (1415–1493). After
the marriage of Mary and Maximilian, they jointly ruled the Burgundian
Netherlands under the auspices of Frederick III. After Mary's death in a
horse-riding accident in 1482, Maximilian acted as a regent for their
son Philip the Fair (1478–1506), who was still only four years old. The
year 1482, thus, marks the beginning of the **Habsburg Netherlands**.

Through a felicitious marriage between Philipe and Joanna of Castile in
1496, their oldest son, Charles V (1500–1558), was able to inherit a
vast patchwork of territories over time, including the core Habsburg
lands; the Habsburg Netherlands; Castile, Aragon, and Navarre in Spain;
Siciliy, Sardinia, and Naples; and various other areas. In 1556, Charles
V abdicated his control over Spain, the Italian and oveseas colonies,
and the Habsburg Netherlands to his eldest son, Philip II. In the same
year, the Austrian Habsburg lands were handed over to his brother,
Ferdinand I who eventually also became Holy Roman Emperor in 1558. The
two branches of the Habsburgs evolved separately from 1556 onwards.

The region under the control of the Spanish Habsburgs was known as
**Spanish Netherlands** from then onwards. Primarily due to Philip II's
heavy-handed rule, revolts started breaking out in the Low Countries,
which led to the Eighty Years War (1568–1648). In 1581, the seven
provinces formally declared their independence from Spain. The territory
of the seven provinces and the various generality lands that were
controlled directly by the States General of the Dutch Republic was
roughly equivalent to that of modern-day Netherlands. It is in the early
days of the Eighty Years War that the area, as the Dutch Republic from
1581 onwards, began to display immense economic growth.

The southern part of the Low Countries–roughly equivalent to
modern-day Belgium and Luxemburg–remained under control of the Spanish
Habsburgs. From 1701 onwards it was heavily contested in the **War of
Spanish succession** (1701–1714), eventually being transferred to the
Austrian Habsburgs by the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. Flanders had still
shown substantial economic growth in much of the sixteenth century and
was at the time one of its wealthiest regions. Yet many of these
activities moved to the north and Flanders was much more stagnant in the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The other main area of growth in the early modern period was England and
later also Scotland. Wales ceased to be a separate kingdom in the
sixteenth century. It was formally incorporated and became subject to
English legal and administrative systems. After the death of Elizabeth
in 1603, the last of the Tudors, the Scottish King James VI also
ascended to the English throne and was crowned as James I. As the
English monarch was also the ruler over Ireland, James I now ruled over
all three states in what was known as the **Union of the Crowns**. In
1707, Scotland and England, then, formally fused through the **Treaty of
Union**, which created the **Kingdom of Great Britain**. After the
Union, Scotland also began to display significant economic development
and it is in this period that the **Scottish Enlightenment** flourishes
as an intellectual movement with groundbreaking thinkers such as Adam
Smith (1723 – 1790) and David Hume (1711 – 1776).

From the seventeenth century onwards, the American Colonies also
embarked on a journey of impressive economic growth by pre-modern
standards.[^39] While much of that economic growth was due to the
spectacular growth of the population, it seems that the American
colonies might have also experienced a per capita growth that was about
twice that seen in the Dutch Republic and England.[^40] Given its much
lower starting position, however, the thirteen colonies had an economic
output that was only somewhat more than 30 percent of Great Britain's on
the eve of the Revolution.[^41]

Only during the Industrial Revolution do per capita GDP growth rates
start increasing to modern levels, as the efficiency gains from
knowledge and technology improvements started to far outpace population
growth.[^42] Industrialization first began in England and its Industrial
Revolution is typically dated from 1760 to 1840. In the rest of Europe,
industrialization was mainly a nineteenth century phenomenon. The same
is true of the United States. And during industralization is when Europe
and the United States really strongly pull away from the rest of the
world economically and technologically. This period is, therefore, often
called the **Second Great Divergence** or simply the **Great
Divergence**.

## 3. Drivers of Economic Expansion

What were the main drivers of the economic expansion throughout much of
the High and Late Middle Ages? And what made Europe diverge so
substantially from the rest of the world in the early modern period and
particularly during industrialization?

In the eighteenth century, Montesquieu argued that climate and geography
had sustantial impact on political and legal systems and, therefore, on
national prosperity.[^43] In the early twentieth century, Max Weber
argued that modern capitalism first emerged in northern Europe,
particularly in protestant regions, due to the protestant work
ethic.[^44] These types of arguments still circulate in explainig global
inequality and the different economic and technological trajectories of
nations. Another common idea is that policymakers in many undeveloped
countries are ignorant about how to produce prosperity, hence, why
global inequality persists.[^45]

Economic prosperity, however, seems to depend foremost on a society's
political and legal institutions. The economic and technological
divergence of Europe and the United States from the rest of the world
can then be explained by their unique political and legal
institutions.[^46]

According to Acemoglu and Robinson,[^47] the key is to attain political
institutions where power is distributed across a broad coalition with
checks on power, yet with sufficient centralization to maintain law and
order. Such inclusive political institutions tend to uphold the rule of
law, promote legal equality, protect property rights, allow businesses
and workers to exit and enter into markets, and provide public services
such as education and infrastructure. They also allow technological
advancements to flourish. Inclusive political institutions, therefore,
are the real engines of economic prosperity.

States where small groups wield political power, by contrast, tend to
create economic institutions which extract wealth from the rest of
society to the benefit of ruling group. While such states can indeed
achieve economic growth for some time, they typically cannot produce
long-lasting prosperity. This is why the Soviet Union eventually
stagnated, after initially showing decades of growth by moving peasants
into more productive industrial activities. A similar fate likely is
also to befall China as it has failed to use the economic momentum of
the previous decades to embrace more inclusive political institutions
that can bring about enduring prosperity.

Inclusive political and economic institutions tend to persist. Political
and economic institutions that only benefit a narrow elite also tend to
be stuck in a vicious cycle. This largely explains why global inequality
is so entrenched. The movement towards more inclusive political and
economic institutions frequently depends on critical historical
junctures and is often affected by what at first blush might appear to
be minor contingencies.

Western Europe, for example, made a signficant move towards more
inclusive political and economic institutions in the fourteenth century.
The created an acute shortage of labor which empowered peasants in
Western and parts of Central Europe, enabling them to escape the yoke of
feudalism, obtain higher wages, and demand better treatment. Towards the
East in places such as Hungary, Prussia, Bohemia, Poland, Ukraine, and
Russia, the same events eventually led to a more servile status for
peasants in what is known as the **second serfdom**. In the words of
Acemoglu and Robinson,

> Though in 1346 there were few differences between Western and Eastern
> Europe in terms of political and economic institutions, by 1600 they
> were worlds apart. In the West, workers were free of feudal dues,
> fines, and regulations and were becoming a key part of a booming
> market economy. In the East, they were also involved in such an
> economy, but as coerced serfs growing the food and agricultural goods
> demanded in the West. This institutional divergence was the result of
> a situation where the differences between these areas initially seemed
> very small: in the East, lords were a little better organized; they
> had slightly more rights and more consolidated landholdings. Towns
> were weaker and smaller, peasants less organized. In the grand scheme
> of history, these were small differences. Yet these small differences
> between the East and the West became very consequential for the lives
> of their populations and for the path of institutional development
> when the feudal order was shaken up by the Black Death.[^48]

While political institutions were still more rudimentary in the High and
the Late Middle Ages, they surely had some role in the economic
expansion of the period.

The feudal lord-vassal system decentralized political power between
monarchs, nobility, and clergy. Monarchs were generally quite dependent
on their direct vassals for raising armies. While the monarch could
generally tax subjects on royal domains, those who lived on fiefs owned
by noble and clerical vassals could only be taxed indirectly, namely
through their Lord at the request of the monarch.[^49] As already noted
by Montesquieu in the eighteenth century, this system prevented imperial
overreach and fostered long-term order even if it was not always stable.
In his words,[^50] the system

> diminished the whole of the weight of lordship by giving many people
> various kinds of lordship over the same thing or the same persons,
> which set various limits to empire that was too extensive, which
> produce rule with an inclination to anarchy and anarchy with a
> tendency to order and harmony.

In an illustrative study, Blaydes and Chaney show that by 1100 European
rulers already lasted longer than their islamic counterparts. In the
Islamic world, the sultans generally relied on slaves from the
non-Islamic world to maintain their armies, an institution called
**mamlukism**. These mamluks generally lived an existence separately
from the rest of the population–in terms of physical location,
language, and customs–and were directly loyal to the sultan. The
nobility in the Islamic world, therefore, had much less influence than
in Europe. According to Blaydes and Chaney, the feudal system of
military recruitment drastically increased the costs of unsuccesful
revolts for the European nobility.[^51]

Europe has a long tradition of utilizing sovereign-constraining and more
inclusive political institutions. In the sixth and fifth century BC,
Athenian political power was dispersped among male citizens through the
general assembly and various administrative and judicial offices that
were mostly allocated by lottery.[^52] For nearly 500 years, power was
constrained in the Roman Republic by competition between the Senate,
Assemblies, and other administrative, judicial, and military
institutions.[^53] At the start of the High Middle Ages, monarchs
typically had informal councils and assemblies with nobles, clergy, and
sometimes peasants. Parliaments in the "modern" sense, the key
institution to check political power and provide a voice to a broad
coalition in society, then began to appear during the High Middle
Ages.[^54]

The first parliament was arguably founded at Léon in 1188.[^55] From
there parliaments gradually spread throughout other parts of Spain and
the rest of Europe. They arose for monarchies as well as specific
regions. These early parliaments could only be convened by the monarch
(or another ruler at the regional level) and did not meet according to a
regular schedule. The key power attained mostly during the Late Middle
Ages was that monarchs required parliamentary approval to levy new
taxes, including notably for wars. According to Van Zanden, Buringh, and
Bosker,[^56] the key driving force behind the formation of parliaments
in the High and Late Middle Ages was the growth of independent towns and
cities with corporate identities and rights. Including these communes
into the informal feudal assemblies and councils transformed them into
parliaments.

The main geographical exception to the growth of parliaments was
northern Italy. Many of the region's communes were highly independent
from the Holy Roman Empire and other sovereigns, some also became fully
independent city-states in the eleventh and twelfth centuries with the
power to set their own foreign policy, raise armies, tax their
populations, and so on.[^57] The absence of monarchical influence in
northern Italy during the crucial years of the growth of parliaments
probably explains why the parliamentary institution did not really take
root in that region.[^58] But while parliaments may not have developed
as strongly in these regions, inclusive political institutions did
develop in most of the northern Italian towns and cities. The municipal
institutions generally gave a high degree of influence to nobles,
wealthy merchants, and, depending on the case, to the new middle classes
such as shopkeepers, notaries, and artisans. Following the Ancient
Athenian tradition, lottery prodecures were common in the selection of
executives and administrators.[^59]

Many of the northern Italian towns were able to amass great prosperity
in the High and Late Middle Ages, and northern Italy was the most
powerful economic region in Europe. That prosperity fueled the revival
of Greek and Roman learning and the general architectural, artistic,
scientific, and literary advancements of the **Italian Renaissance**
from the fourteenth to the sixteenth centuries. Above all this
prosperity extended to Venice and Florence. According to the famous
nineteenth century German historian Jacob Burckhardt, Venice and
Florence were "of deep significance for the history of the human
race\.... \[N\]either can be compared to anything else which the world
has hitherto produced".[^60]

In the early modern period, the economic impact of inclusive and
sovereign-constraining political and legal institutions began to produce
more pronounced differences between some parts of Europe and the rest of
the world.

The monarchs in many European states increasingly obtained unchecked and
centralized control over their territories with professional
bureacracies, standing armies, unified laws, and weak parliaments and
other checks on power. Rulers often claimed that their authority came
directly from god, a tenet known as the **divine right of kings**. These
strong centralized monarchies with unchecked power came to be known as
**absolute monarchies** and the ideology behind such rule as
**absolutism**. While monarchs were generally forced to rule through
elites during the Middle Ages, those checks on power, therefore,
dwindled within many European monarchies in the early modern period.

Perhaps the epitome of the absolutist state in Western Europe was
France. In the wake of the French Wars of Religion (1562 – 1598), the
monarchy increasingly seized control. The kings of France did not
convene the French Parliament–the **Estates General**–at all between
1614 and 1789. Louis XIV (1638 – 1715), the Sun King, supposedly
famously quipped "L'État, c'est moi" ("The State, it is me").

In 1469, the marriage of Ferdinand II of Aragon (1452 – 1516) and
Isabella I of Castille (1451 – 1504) united much of the Iberian
Peninsula outside of Portugal. In 1492, the last Muslim Kingdom in the
Iberian Peninsula, the Emirate of Granada, was defeated. The daughter of
Ferdinand and Isabella, Joanna, married Philip of Habsburg. Their son,
Charles V, became the first Habsburg king of Spain (1516). His son,
Phillip II, then began unifying Spain administratively. In this period,
increasing absolutism was fueled by the Crown's control over colonies
and international trade from which it profited spectacularly. It is from
this period until the early nineteenth century that an absolutist
monarchy ruled Spain.

The character of the Italian city-states and communes also had changed
by the modern period. In 1434, the Medici family gained *de facto*
control over Florence's republican institutions. This lasted until 1494
when they were expelled from the city. After several decades of struggle
between the Medici and republican forces, the Habsburg King Charles V
invaded Florence in 1530. He transformed the city into a Duchy with
Alessandro di Medici as the first duke. Eventually, the title changed to
the Grand Duke of Tuscany, as surrounding areas were annexed. The
territory then experienced fairly absolutist rule of which almost two
hundred years under the Medici. Many of the Italian communes and
city-states underwent such transformations earlier and Florence was one
of the few to survive into the fifteenth century. Venice's republican
institutions had become much more aristocratic in the early fourteenth
century.[^61] While broadly the same institutions remained by the time
Napoleon invaded in 1797, they had become controlled by a very small
aristocracy.[^62]

Two states were notable exceptions to the progress of absolutism in
Europe: the Dutch Republic and England (Great Britain after 1707).

The first meeting of the Estates General in the Low Countries only took
place in 1406. In the 1570s, however, it became an independent body for
the Dutch Republic with scheduled operation. Thereby, it was the first
major parliament in Europe to acquire such modern features. The same
transformation occurred for the Estates Generals within each of the
Republic's regions, notably in Holland. In England, the foundation for a
national parliament was laid by the **Magna Carta** (1215) and by the
end of the thirteenth century it became the first such institution in
Europe to meet fairly frequently. Independence from the monarchy and
regular operation was attained, however, only after the Glorious
Revolution (1688). After the Glorious Revolution, England adopted
increasingly strong property rights, more equal application of the law,
and arbitrary taxation and monopolies were abolished. A major canal boom
also followed in the eighteenth century.

These increased parliamentary rights in the Dutch Republic and England
were not just historical accidents. The Dutch Revolt (1568 – 1648), the
English Civil Wars (1642 – 1651), and the Glorious Revolution (1688)
were, in fact, largely driven by merchants that sought to increase the
power of parliaments at the expense of the monarch.[^63] The subsequent
success of parliaments in these regions would have also been supported
by their geographies. According to Stasavanger,[^64] as distance and
communication constraints were lower than in bigger states such as
France and Spain, the Dutch Republic and England were able to acquire
substantial power over expenditures in the early modern period. This
also allowed those states to raise more revenues.

The development of strong parliaments and a more inclusive political
regime had substantial impact on property rights, the rule of law, and
other economic institutions that were likely a substantial factor in the
strong economic development and per capita income growth in the Dutch
Republic, particularly in Holland, and in England (and Scotland after
1707) in the early modern period relative to the rest of Europe. These
areas were actually the main engines of economic growth and the center
of economic power clearly shifted from northern Italy to northwestern
Europe.[^65]

While Europe had generally begun to pull away from the rest of the world
already in the early modern period, it most decisively diverged in the
modern period. While Great Britain began to industrialize in the 1760s,
this was largely a nineteenth century phenomenon in the rest of Europe.
The key event to broader European industrialization was actually the
French Revolution (1789).[^66]

When Louis XIV (1754 – 1793) ascended to the throne in 1774, his
predecessors had already amassed massive debts. The situation worsened
under his reign, particularly due to France's financing of the American
Revolution (1775 – 1783) which was done almost entirely with borrowed
money. In 1787, he made moves to levy new taxes. The country was in a
poor economic state and had recently suffered some bad harvests. While
the clergy and nobility had traditionally many exemptions on taxes,
Louis XVI decided he now had to tax them. So, he convened the Assembly
of Notables–consisting of nobility and church officials–for the
first time in 150 years. But as they would only agree to new taxes with
more political power, Louis refused and next year just tried to impose
new taxes without their permission through a royal edict.

The regional high courts of justice–known as parlements–were
required to register such edicts for them to have the effect of law.
While normally the parlements would have complied with royal edicts, the
parlement of Paris and various other regional parlements now refused to
register the law on the basis that it lacked legitimacy. Without any
alternative, Louis called the Estates General to Versailles on May 5 of
1789 for the first time since 1614.

The Estates General was composed of representatives within three
**estates**: the clergy (the first estate), the nobility (the second
estate), and the rest of society (the third estate). While traditionally
each estate had received one vote, the third estate demanded that voting
proceed by head. Given that they had more than 95% of the
representatives, this would have clearly favored the third estate. After
Louis refused to grant voting by head, the third estate proclaimed
itself the legitimate government of the French nation calling themselves
the National Assembly. A large number of clergy and some nobles joined
them. They convened separately and effectively sidelined the Estates
General.

The delegates of the third estate had been influenced by the ideas of
enlightment thinkers such as John Locke (1632 – 1704), Montesquieu
(1689 – 1755), Voltaire (1694 – 1778), Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712 –
1778), and Adam Smith (1723 – 1790). They were particularly angered by
the privileges enjoyed by nobles and clergy, the excesses of the French
monarchy, and the dire living conditions for much of the general public.
In general, they believed absolutism was responsible for the plight of
France. The news of the events in Versailles began to circulate in
Paris. As many in the public shared the sentiments of the
representatives at Versailles, riots broke out in the Summer of 1789.
Fearing an invasion of Paris to restore order, Parisians descended on
the **Bastille** to seize weapons for self-defense on July 14, 1989,
butchering many of the guards in the process.

While initially many revolutionaries had sought a constitutional
monarchy, republican radicals–who sought complete abolition of the
monarchy and a popular government– increasingly became influential as
the revolution progressed. Their rise was particularly encouraged by
fears that the nobility and clergy were conspiring with foreign powers
to overthrow the revolutionary government. The attempt by Marie
Antoinette and Louis XVI to flee France in June of 1791 and the outbreak
of war with Austria in April 1792 further fueled the rise of republican
radicals.

The most notable of republican radicals were a group that called
themselves the **Jacobins**. They increasingly began to influence public
sentiments against the monarchy, nobles, and clergy. On August 10 of
1792, thousands attacked Tuileries Palace where the royal family was
staying. While Louis XVI initially fled, he was soon arrested. In the
so-called September massacres of 1792, thousands of nobles and clergy
were executed by militias that broke into prisons in Paris. Similar
events followed in other parts of the country. While the Jacobins did
not directly organize such events, they certainly created an environment
for them.

In September 1791, a new constitution had been written and the National
Assembly disbanded. In October 1791, it was replaced by the legislative
assembly of the constitutional monarchy. During the events of August 10,
1792, a radical group, many of them Jacobins, formed a revolutionary
municipal government in Paris called the **Paris Commune** (1792 –
1795). Recognizing the end of the monarchy, the Legislative Assembly
dissolved itself in September and called for elections to a new body,
the National Convention, that would govern a republic. The National
Convention was elected by universal suffrage for males over 25. It
convened for the first time on September 21 at which point the
convention officially abolished the monarchy and declared France a
republic. The National Convention included many of the foremost
Jacobins, including Maximilian Robespierre, Louis Antoine de Saint-Just,
and Georges Danton.

The Jacobin-led rule in the First Republic became increasingly violent.
In January of 1793, Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette were beheaded by
guillotine. In April 1793, the Committee of Public Safety was formed,
the primary executive body in the new republic. From September 1793 to
July 1794, mainly under the leadership of Robespierre, carried out a
violent campaign against all suspected enemies of the republic. Known as
the **Reign of Terror**, thousands of nobility, clergy, and other
supposed conspirators were killed. In July 1974, delegates of the
convention organized the arrest and execution of many high-profile
Jacobins, including Robespierre and Saint-Just. Many Jacobins were also
arrested, expelled from government, or just lost their influence. While
the government became more moderate, it was not that popular with the
public.

Pretty soon after declaring war on Austria, other European powers–for
fear of French revolutionary ideas and other reasons–joined Austrian
forces, including Prussia, Great Britain, Spain, and the Dutch Republic.
Initially France was not that successful, but by July 1794 its
performance had started to improve. While the French army was less
professional, the successful introduction of mass conscription in 1793
had particularly begun to bear fruit. What motivated France in their war
with European powers was not only the desire to protect its own
revolutionary government, but also to export the ideals of the French
Revolution elsewhere. In the subsequent years, France managed to
establish many sister republics, often short-lived, including the
Batavian Republic (1795 – 1806) instead of the Dutch Republic, the
Helvetic Republic (1798 – 1803) to replace the old Swiss confederacy,
and the Ligurian Republic (1797 – 1805) to replace the Republic of
Genoa.

While France was successful militarily, the government was unable to
overcome the country's economic problems, factional tensions, and
general unrest. Napolean Bonaparte (1769 – 1821) grew in popularity
within this environment of weak government. His military success,
particularly in the Italian campaigns, humble background, and
republicanism fueled this popularity. In 1799, Napolean orchestrated a
coup and installed himself as head of state. By that time, French
control and influence had already expanded into the Low Countries,
Switzerland, much of Italy, and parts of the Holy Roman Empire. But
under Napoleon, France consolidated this influence and further expanded
it through conquest, annexation, and alliances. In 1804, Napoleon
declared himself emperor. By 1806, all the sister republics had
disappeared, either annexed or turned into monarchical clients states by
Napoleon.

The French Revolutionary Wars (1792 – 1802) and the Napoleonic Wars
(1803 – 1815) had a substantial impact on Europe. Particularly Napoleon
supported the establishment of strong bureaucracies, legal equality, the
protection of private property, the abolition of guilds, and the
elimination of feudal laws. The re-introduction of monarchies in many
regions was more practical, rather than a complete abandonment of the
ideals of the Revolution. By 1814, allied forces had conquered France
and Napoleon was exiled to Elba. After his escape in 1815, he was able
to raise an army, but suffered a crippling defeat at the **Battle of**
**Waterloo** in June. He was, then, exiled to St Helena and died in
1821.

While the French Bourbon monarchy had been re-instated in 1814 through
Louis XVIII, there was no return to absolutist government. When his
successor, Charles X, attempted to concentrate monarchical power again,
protests broke out. After the July Revolution of 1830, Charles X fled to
England and the House of Orleans assumed the throne. The monarchy
promoted the growth of business and industry. By the 1840s, however, an
economic downturn and lack of ability to keep order further protests
broke out, which led to the final deposition of the monarchy and the
beginning of the Second Republic. Napoleon III was elected President in
December 1848, but seized power by force in December 1851 and declared
himself emperor in 1852. He also promoted business and economic
development. After an economic downturn and a bad defeat against Prussia
in 1870, Napoleon III was captured and the Third Republic was
proclaimed. This time it stuck and the French were able to build a
political and social system that united the French people better.

While throwing the yoke of absolutism was messy, the Revolution set
France on the road to more inclusive political and economic institutions
which allowed the country to industrialize and build long-lasting
prosperity. While monarchies persisted after Napoleon's fall, guilds,
feudal laws, aristocratic privileges, and absolutism were substantially
weakened in many parts of Europe. The French Revolution also inspired
independence movements, unification movements, and political revolutions
and reforms throughout the nineteenth century. In the early 1820s, there
were liberal uprisings in Spain and Italy. The same decade saw Greece
obtain its independence from the Ottoman Empire. Belgium had been
included in the Netherlands at the Congress of Vienna and successfully
gained independence in 1830. As in France, the year 1848 saw widespread
European protests for liberal constitutions and universal suffrage.
Great Britain greatly expanded the male suffrage in the nineteenth
century, extending it fully to all adult males and females in 1928.

The French Revolution had set in motion the road towards more inclusive
political institutions, industrialization, and long-run prosperity
throughout much of Western and Central Europe.
