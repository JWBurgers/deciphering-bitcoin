# Introduction

In this first part of the book, we offer a thorough survey of the conventional practices involved in money and payments in the United States, including the institutions, systems, laws, regulations, norms, and language. On occassion, we will also reflect on practices elsewhere.  

The subject of money and payments is quite daunting. Within most countries, money and payments is subject to extensive legislative coverage and regulatory oversight. There is a huge body of academic and policy literature related to it. Many of the key details on our subject matter are burried away deeply in legal, regulatory, and procedural documents, or, even worse, merely within the everyday practices of institutional insiders. The landscape of money and payments is also constantly evolving, and practices and institutions vary across geographic regions, sociodemographic strata, and other dimensions. And perhaps even more so than in other domains of expertise, the landscape is riddled with ambiguous and arcane terminology. 

Naturally, our survey will be far from complete, even if it is mostly limited to the United States. But that is not a cause for concern in our case. Our purpose with the survey is only to build a solid understanding of the basic architectural principles that govern money and payments, not to offer an exhaustive overview.  

What types of payment methods exist and what are the basic principles behind their operation? What is the role of commercial banks and central banks in the operation of these payment methods? What other players are involved? What types of challenges arise when payments travel across borders? What is the difference between central bank reserves and commercial bank money? What is monetary policy? And who really controls the supply of money in modern economies? 

These are the types of questions that we will broadly answer with the survey. 


## Survey purpose

Why is starting with a detailed survey into conventional money and payments practices so important? 

At the basis of our approach is the conviction that Bitcoin’s main significance is for the world of money and payments, particularly as an alternative to conventional money and payment options. 

So, in our view, the best way to comprehend the nature of Bitcoin (i.e., what it is) is to juxtapose it with conventional money and payments practices. We believe this is particularly important for explaining why Bitcoin is a monetary and financial innovation. In addition, we hold that the merits and downsides of Bitcoin can only be sensibly weighed and discussed if the alternatives are really clear. Too often, Bitcoin is embraced on the basis of mere handwaiving about conventional money and payments practices. A deep appreciation is necessary for more thoughtful reflection.

Lastly, we also have a practical mission with this textbook. We would like to encourage our readers to contribute to the Bitcoin ecosystem. In this regard, it is also important to illustrate the evolution, shortcomings, and successes of conventional money and payments practices, as Bitcoin only has value because it can offer something different. Nearly two decades of experience has shown that, when building products and services, it is very easy to forget the properties and principles that make Bitcoin valuable in the first place for gains in convenience, efficiency, and control. 

We should note here that the topic of money and payments is subject to fierce debate in academic, policy, and certainly Bitcoin and cryptocurrency circles. And our framing of money and payments in the survey undoubtedly impacts our understanding of the nature and value of Bitcoin as well as how to productively build tools, products, and services with Bitcoin. 

That said, our disposition towards the conventional practices and their historical evolution is fairly neutral. Compared to many proponents of Bitcoin, for example, we hold much less skepticism towards central banks and fiat money. And even from this more nuanced position, we are bound to conclude that Bitcoin holds real value and will continue to grow in importance as an alternative for conventional money and payments. So, our discussion about Bitcoin should at least be reasonably robust with regards to our framing of the conventional world of money and payments. 


## Survey scope

There is substantial diversity in money and payment practices across geographic areas, sociodemographic strata, and other dimensions. This makes it difficult and awkward to speak about them on a completely generic level. In addition, to really make sense of some of these practices we require deeper legal, institutional, and historical context. 

On the basis of these considerations, we selected to narrow our focus of the survey to the United States. It is a great choice both because of its global importance and the availability of information about it. We will only occassionally reflect on money and payment arrangements elsewhere in the World. 

That said, while money and payments practices across the globe and their historical evolution are marked by variation, they are similar enough. From the discussion on the United States, that is, you can glean the basic architectural principles that permeate the global landscape for money and payments, particularly through occasionally reflecting on them at a global level. These basic principles emerge from the discussion by delving into a specific legal, institutional, and historical context. 

While we believe that a good foundation consisting of more than mere lip service on conventional money and payments is necessary for grasping Bitcoin, we have also sought to limit the scope significantly. We will mostly shy away from any discussion on low-level technical details such as those related to messaging standards and protocols, security standards, legal and regulatory environments, and implementation procedures. Instead, we will operate on a fairly high level and focus on the main players, processes, and systems involved. We should also note that certain positions and interpretations are only offered with a limited defense. 


## The fintech sector

To avoid confusion, when we talk about the “conventional” world of money and payments, that largely includes the innovations produced by the fintech sector: a sector in finance that seeks to disrupt the traditional financial industry, particularly by leveraging new technologies and business models. Typically, the solutions offered by this sector specifically attempt to disrupt the business models of commercial banks. In other words, Venmo, Paypal, Google Wallet, M-Pesa, virtual credit cards, Wise, and all such fairly recent additions to the payments landscape are understood here as “conventional”.

To label the fintech sector as “conventional” is not intended to be dismissive. For example, in the last two decades or so, there have been rapid advancements in content management and ecommerce platforms. We have also seen substantial growth in non-traditional payment processors such as Stripe and Mollie. These developments now make it easier than ever to start a store online and accept a wide variety of payment methods. Even the technically uninitiated can probably manage to set up a basic online store with just a few hours of work. That is drastically different from the early days of the Internet when setting up an online store required much more technical expertise and effort.  

But while these types of developments are highly significant, the solutions offered by the fintech sector do not fundamentally change the nature of our practices around money and payments. They are really more improvements on the traditional monetary and financial system, rather than strongly distinct and independent from it. Hence, why we refer to them here as “conventional”. (As we will argue in Part II, this is in contrast to Bitcoin, which is really more seperate from the traditional monetary and financial system.) 


## Overview

For our discussion, we assume that the reader possesses only a basic understanding of the topics involved, namely that gleaned from everyday experiences. So we will largely start from the ground up with money and payments, frequently utilizing examples, and taking little for granted. 

Many of the concepts introduced are also listed in reference works with technical definitions. An example is the glossary offered by the Bank of International Settlements’ **Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures**—the main global forum for discussions on payment systems.<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> But these types of references are really more suited for professional practitioners. We will instead strive to offer more intuitive characterizations. 

Particularly banks play a key role in money and payments. Thus, our exploration starts in *Chapter 1* with an overview of banks and banking. Next, we move to payments within and between banks in *Chapter 2*. In *Chapter 3*, we turn to payment methods that go beyond banks and core payment infrastructure. We will discuss payment cards in detail, as well as various other payment methods. *Chapter 4* builds on our discussion about banks and payments to investigate conventional practices with regards to money. We conclude in *Chapter 5* with an overview of the organizational and networking architectures involved in money and payments.
