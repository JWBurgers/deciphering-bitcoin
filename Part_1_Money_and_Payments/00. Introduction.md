# Introduction

The central contention of this book is that Bitcoin’s primary significance is as a monetary and financial innovation, one that serves as an important and valuable alternative to conventional money and payments options. 

We can hardly defend that claim in a vacuum. While our enquiry into the nature and value of Bitcoin demands diving into numerous related subject matters, it most urgently demands a thorough understanding of money, payments, and banking. The real innovation that Bitcoin offers as well as the system’s merits and downsides can only be sensibly brought into view if the alternatives are really clear. Too often, Bitcoin is embraced on the basis of mere handwaving about conventional practices in money, payments, and banking. A deep appreciation is necessary for more thoughtful reflection.

A grounding of the nature and value of Bitcoin against the traditional monetary and financial system is not just an academic curiosity. Every day thousands of developers, businesses, and users make decisions that impact the overall character of Bitcoin. Nearly two decades of experience has shown that, when building products and services, it is very easy to forget the properties and principles that make Bitcoin valuable in the first place for gains in convenience, efficiency, and control. If we really want to offer a lasting proposition, then we must have a clear view on what makes Bitcoin different and also learn from the evolution, shortcomings, and successes of conventional money, payments, and banking. 

The first part of this book is, therefore, precisely tasked with establishing a thorough understanding of money, payments, and banking. Our primary focus is on the institutions, systems, laws, regulations, norms, and language of money, payments, and banking in the United States. In light of the Republic’s historical roots, however, especially our historical discussion also extends to England and the rest of Europe. 

Our task at hand is fairly daunting. Money, payments, and banking are subject to extensive legislative coverage and regulatory oversight. Many of the key details on our subject matter are buried away deeply in legal, regulatory, and procedural documents, or, even worse, merely within the everyday practices of institutional insiders. The landscape of money, payments, and banking is also constantly evolving and product of extensive, often contested historical developments. And perhaps even more so than in other domains of expertise, the landscape is riddled with ambiguous and arcane terminology.  

Naturally, our survey will be far from complete, even if it is mostly limited to the United States. But that is not an obstacle to our task at hand. Our aim is only to expose the main architectural principles that structure money, payments, and banking, as that is sufficient to support a rigorous assessment of the nature and value of Bitcoin. To that end we set aside many details and controversies; a selective, piecemeal pictures will suffice. 

There are at least two potential criticisms of our survey: first, that our assessment of the nature and value of Bitcoin is highly influenced by controversial views on money, payments, and banking; and, second, that the focus on the United States biases our findings.  

With regards to the first potential objection, we acknowledge here explicitly that the subject of money, payments, and banking is subject to fierce debate in academic, policy, and certainly Bitcoin and cryptocurrency circles. And our framing of the subject matter in our survey undoubtedly impacts our understanding of the nature and value of Bitcoin as well as how to productively build tools, products, and services with Bitcoin. 

That said, our disposition towards conventional practices on money, payments, and banking and their historical evolution is fairly neutral. Compared to many proponents of Bitcoin, for example, we hold much less scepticism towards central banks, fiat money, regulatory oversight, and fractional reserve banking. Much of what we say about money, payments, and banking is, in fact, fairly mainstream. And it is precisely for this reason that we believe our conclusion, namely that Bitcoin holds real value and will continue to grow in importance as an alternative for conventional money and payments, holds real weight. That is, we believe our fairly positive conclusions about Bitcoin are reasonably robust with regards to our framing of the conventional world of money, payments, and banking. 

With regards to the second objection, it is indeed true that there is substantial diversity in money, payments, and banking across geographic areas, sociodemographic strata, and other dimensions. This is precisely what makes it difficult and awkward to speak about them on a completely generic level. In addition, to really make sense of some of the institutions and practices we require deeper legal, institutional, and historical context. We think a discussion that revolves around the United States, therefore, makes more sense than a completely generic one. It is a great choice both because of its global importance and the availability of information about it. 

That said, we are not convinced our approach leads to a strong bias of any kind. To start, while our primary focus is on the United States, particularly our historical discussion also focuses on England and the rest of Europe. Even more important, while money, payments, and banking varies across the globe, the different countries and regions are *similar enough*. So from our geographically limited exposition, you can nevertheless glean the basic architectural principles that permeate much of the global landscape for money, payments, and banking. They emerge from delving into a specific legal, institutional, and historical context. 


## The fintech sector

To avoid confusion, when we talk about the “conventional” world of money, payments, and banking that largely includes the innovations produced by the **fintech sector**: a sector in finance that seeks to disrupt the traditional financial industry, particularly by leveraging new technologies and business models. Typically, the solutions offered by this sector specifically attempt to disrupt the business models of commercial banks. In other words, Venmo, PayPal, Google Wallet, M-Pesa, virtual credit cards, Wise, and all such fairly recent additions to the payments landscape are understood here as “conventional”.

To label the fintech sector as “conventional” is not intended to be dismissive. For example, in the last two decades or so, there have been rapid advancements in content management and ecommerce platforms. We have also seen substantial growth in non-traditional payment processors such as Stripe and Mollie. These developments now make it easier than ever to start a store online and accept a wide variety of payment methods. Even the technically uninitiated can probably manage to set up a basic online store with just a few hours of work. That is drastically different from the early days of the Internet when setting up an online store required much more technical expertise and effort.  

But while these types of developments are highly significant, the solutions offered by the fintech sector do not fundamentally change the nature of our practices around money and payments. They are really more improvements on the traditional monetary and financial system, rather than strongly distinct and independent from it. Hence, why we refer to them here as “conventional”. (As we will argue in *Part II*, this is in contrast to Bitcoin, which is really more separate from the traditional monetary and financial system.) 


## Overview

For our discussion, we assume that the reader possesses only a basic understanding of the topics involved, namely that gleaned from everyday experiences. So, we largely start from the ground up with money, payments, and banking, frequently utilizing examples, and taking little for granted. 

Many of the concepts introduced are also listed in reference works for the financial sector with technical definitions. An example is the glossary offered by the Bank of International Settlements’ **Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures**—the main global forum for discussions on payment systems.<sup>[1](#footnote1)</sup> But these types of reference works are really more suited for professional practitioners. We will instead strive for more intuitive characterizations. 

Particularly banks play a key role in money and payments. Thus, our exploration starts in *Chapter 1* with an overview of banks and banking in the United States. The modern history of money, payments, and banking is closely intertwined with the history of the corporation. So, in *Chapter 2*, we direct our attention to the evolution of the joint stock company into the modern corporation. For this historical evolution, we turn primarily to developments in England and the Dutch Republic from the sixteenth century onwards to get a full picture. We briefly comment on the evolution of the modern corporate regime in the United States, particularly after the American Revolution. These first two chapters also introduce many of the basic concepts necessary for subsequent chapters. 

In *Chapter 3*, we present a brief history of money, payments, and banking until the early modern period. Our discussion mainly centers on the merchant and deposit bankers from the Late Middle Ages to the early modern period. In *Chapter 4*, we discuss money, payments, and banking in Colonial America. 

In the last three chapters of *Part I*, we turn to the modern-day institutions and practices surrounding money and payments in the United States, occasionally reflecting on those arrangements elsewhere. In *Chapter 5*, we explain the main architectural principles behind bank payments. We turn to other payment methods, particularly using payment cards, in *Chapter 6*. Throughout the first six chapters, we often indirectly reflect on what money is and where it comes from. In the final chapter, we make that subject center stage.
